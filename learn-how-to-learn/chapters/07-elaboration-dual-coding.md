# Chapter 7: Making It Stick: Elaboration, Dual Coding and Concrete Examples

## Why this chapter matters

Imagine two students revising the same biology sentence: *"Arteries have thick, elastic walls."* The first student reads it four times, highlights it in yellow, and moves on. It feels smooth. It feels learned. The second student stops and asks a strange little question: *"Wait — why would that be true?"* She thinks for a moment and answers herself: *"Because blood leaves the heart in powerful bursts, under high pressure, so the walls have to stretch and bounce back without bursting."*

Guess who remembers it next week.

The first student mistook *familiarity* for *understanding* — a trap we met earlier in this book. The second student did something small but powerful: she built the fact into a web of things she already knew. She reasoned it out instead of memorizing it. That single move is the heart of this chapter.

In the last few chapters you learned the two heavyweights of effective study — **retrieval practice** (testing yourself) and **spaced practice** (spreading study over time). This chapter is about the supporting cast: **elaboration**, **dual coding**, and **concrete examples**. These techniques don't replace testing and spacing — the researcher John Dunlosky and his colleagues (2013) rated them only "moderate" in usefulness, a notch below the top two. But used well, they make each fact richer, stickier, and easier to pull back out. Let's learn how to make things stick.

---

## Elaboration: ask "why?" and build a web

**Elaboration** means deepening a memory by connecting new information to what you already know — usually by asking *why* and *how* it's true. Instead of storing a lonely fact, you tie it to a dozen nearby facts, so there are many mental paths leading back to it.

The most studied version is called **elaborative interrogation** — a fancy name for a simple habit: when you meet a stated fact, ask yourself *"Why does this make sense?"* and generate the explanation yourself before checking.

Think of a fact as a single house in a huge city. If only one road reaches it, and that road is blocked, you're lost. Elaboration builds extra roads. When recall time comes, if one path fails, another still gets you there.

### A close cousin: self-explanation

**Self-explanation** is elaboration applied while you study something step by step — you explain to yourself how each step follows and how it links to what you already know, instead of reading passively.

The classic evidence comes from Michelene Chi and colleagues (1989). They watched students study worked physics examples. The high-performing students spontaneously talked through the examples far more — roughly **52 explanatory statements versus 18** for the weaker students, and they tied those explanations to underlying physics principles. In a later study, Chi et al. (1994) *prompted* students to self-explain a biology text and found they learned more than students who weren't prompted. The lesson: the good students weren't just smarter — they were *doing something different*, and it's something you can copy.

### Why it works, and its honest limits

Elaboration works because of **levels of processing**, an idea from Craik and Lockhart (1972). They argued that memory strength depends on *how deeply* you process something. Craik and Tulving (1975) tested it: people who judged a word's *meaning* (deep, semantic processing) later recognized it far better — often about double — compared with people who only judged its typeface (shallow, visual) or whether it rhymed with another word (phonetic). Meaning sticks; surface fades.

But be honest about the limits. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated elaborative interrogation and self-explanation as only **"moderate utility."** Why? They lean on prior knowledge — if you know nothing about the heart, "why" questions have nothing to hook onto. And most of the supporting studies are short lab tasks, not year-long classrooms. Helpful, yes. Magic, no.

**In short:** Turn each fact into a "why?" and answer it from what you already know, and you build multiple mental roads back to the memory.

## The generation effect: struggle first, then check

Here's a rule that sounds almost too simple: **producing an answer yourself beats reading the same answer.** Psychologists call this the **generation effect**.

Slamecka and Graf (1978) named it across five experiments. Some people simply read word pairs like *"rapid – fast."* Others got a rule and a cue and had to generate the second word themselves — *"a synonym of rapid starting with f: fast."* The generators remembered far more, whether tested by recall, recognition, or confidence. A later review of 86 studies (Bertsch et al., 2007) put the average effect size at about **d ≈ 0.40** — a solid, dependable boost.

Think of it like a muscle. Reading the answer is like watching someone else lift the weight. Generating it — even *straining and failing* for a moment before you check — is your own muscle contracting. The effort itself is what strengthens the memory.

This is why elaboration and retrieval are secretly the same family. When you ask "why do arteries have thick walls?" and *generate* the reason before peeking, you get the generation effect for free. The mistake beginners make is asking the "why" question and then instantly reading the answer — that throws the benefit away.

**In short:** Always try to produce the answer yourself before you look — the mental effort of generating is what carves the memory in.

## Dual coding: pair words with pictures

**Dual coding** is the idea, from Allan Paivio, that your mind can store information in two separate systems — a **verbal** system (words) and a **visual** system (mental images). Encode something in both, and you lay down two retrieval routes instead of one.

It's like saving an important file in two different folders. If you can't find it by one path at recall time, the other still turns it up.

The evidence is striking. Paivio and Csapo (1973) found people recalled **more than twice as many pictures as words** — the **picture superiority effect** (pictures are remembered better than words). The clincher: when they *told* people to form a mental image of each word's meaning, the picture advantage vanished. The words caught up because people were now coding them visually too. That's dual coding in action.

### How to actually use it

Dual coding is *not* about decorating notes with clip art. The picture has to carry the *meaning*. Redraw a concept as a labeled diagram, a flowchart, a quick sketch — and crucially, put the visual *right next to* the words it explains, not on a separate page. You want both codes firing together.

```
  DUAL CODING: two routes to one memory

     WORDS ─────────┐
  "arteries have    │
   thick walls"     ▼
                 [ MEMORY ]
                     ▲
     PICTURE ───────┘
   (sketch of a thick
    tube under pressure)

  If one route is blocked at recall,
  the other still reaches the memory.
```

### Two honest cautions

First, don't confuse dual coding with the **learning styles myth** — the popular but false belief that each person is a "visual learner" or "auditory learner" and should be taught only in their style. That idea has repeatedly failed testing. Dual coding makes the *opposite* claim: visuals help *everyone* by adding a second code, regardless of "type."

Second, even *why* pictures win is debated. Some researchers (Ensor, Surprenant et al., 2019; Higdon et al., 2025) argue the real reason isn't a second code at all but **distinctiveness** — pictures simply stand out more. Tellingly, the picture superiority effect even shows up in people with **aphantasia** (who can't form mental images at all). The theory is contested — but the *practical* advice is rock solid: pair words with relevant visuals.

**In short:** Draw the idea and place the picture beside the words — everyone remembers more with two codes, even if scientists still argue about why.

## Concrete examples: from one story to the underlying pattern

Abstract ideas are slippery. **Concrete examples** — specific, familiar instances — give an abstract idea something to hold onto. But there's a twist that trips up most learners, and it's worth getting right.

The key study is Gick and Holyoak (1983). They gave people a tricky puzzle: a doctor must destroy a tumor with radiation, but a strong enough beam would also destroy the healthy tissue it passes through. The solution is to aim several *weak* beams from different directions that converge on the tumor. Most people can't crack it alone. But when learners first read *two* unrelated stories that shared the same "converge from many directions" structure — say, an army splitting up to attack a fortress from all sides — many more solved the tumor problem.

The magic ingredient was **two** examples, not one. With a single story, learners cling to its surface details. With two that share a deep structure, they can *abstract the pattern* — the shared skeleton underneath — and transfer it to a brand-new problem. This ability to carry a skill to a new situation is called **transfer**, and it's the real goal of learning.

### Concrete, but not too concrete

Here's the twist. Kaminski, Sloutsky and Heckler (2008) found that examples that are *too* concrete and vivid can actually *hurt* transfer — learners get so absorbed in the surface details that they miss the abstract principle. Picture teaching fractions only with pizza slices: some kids end up thinking the rule is somehow *about pizza*.

So the recipe is: **start concrete to build intuition, then fade toward the abstract structure, and vary the surface.** Give an idea two or three *different* concrete examples, then ask the powerful question — *"What do these all share?"* That comparison is where the durable, transferable understanding forms.

| Approach | What the learner does | Result |
|---|---|---|
| One vivid example | Fixates on surface details | Poor transfer to new problems |
| Two or three varied examples | Compares them, extracts shared structure | Strong transfer |
| Abstract rule alone | Nothing concrete to grip | Feels hollow, forgotten fast |

**In short:** Give a new idea two or three varied concrete examples, then ask what they share — comparison is what lets you transfer the idea to new situations.

## A worked example: turning a dry fact into a sticky one

Let's put it all together. Take one lifeless fact and rebuild it using everything above.

**Before (dry fact, shallow processing):**

> *"Arteries have thick, elastic walls."*

You could read that ten times and still blank on the exam. Now let's elaborate, dual-code, and ground it.

**After (elaborated, dual-coded, example-rich):**

1. **Elaborate — ask why, and generate the answer yourself first.** *"Why thick and elastic?"* → *"Because blood leaves the heart in high-pressure bursts. The walls must stretch to absorb each surge and spring back to keep blood moving. Thin walls would balloon and burst."* You've now *reasoned* the fact, not memorized it — and by generating the answer before checking, you grabbed the generation effect.

2. **Dual-code — draw it.** Sketch a thick-walled tube with arrows showing a high-pressure surge and the wall stretching, labeled *"stretch + recoil = smooths the pulse."* Put that sketch right next to the sentence.

3. **Concrete examples — give it two.** Think of a **garden hose** that bulges when you kink it and pressure spikes; think of the **springy rubber band** that stretches and snaps back. Now ask: *what do arteries, the hose, and the rubber band share?* → *they all handle pressure by stretching and recoiling.* You've abstracted the principle.

The fact is now anchored by reasoning, a picture, and two familiar analogies. Three roads lead back to it instead of one.

**In short:** To make any fact stick, ask why and answer it yourself, sketch it beside the words, and tie it to two everyday examples.

## Mnemonics and the memory palace: tools for arbitrary lists

Sometimes material has *no* logic to elaborate — a shopping list, the cranial nerves, foreign vocabulary. For pure arbitrary stuff, use **mnemonics**: deliberate tricks that impose structure or vivid imagery onto material that has none. Acronyms, the keyword method, and the famous **method of loci** — the "memory palace" — all fit here.

The **method of loci** (Latin for "places") works by mentally walking through a familiar route — your home — and placing a vivid image for each item at a specific spot. To recall, you take the walk again and "see" each image waiting there. It's the technique behind the book *Moonwalking with Einstein*, in which an ordinary journalist trains for the USA Memory Championship and learns to memorize a shuffled deck of cards by placing wild images along a familiar house.

### A mini memory-palace walkthrough

Say you need to remember three arteries: the **aorta**, the **carotid**, and the **coronary**. Walk your front hall:

```
  YOUR MEMORY PALACE (front hallway)

  [1] Front door  ── a giant OAR (aorta) blocking it,
                     gushing water under huge pressure
        │
        ▼
  [2] Coat rack   ── a CARROT (carotid) wearing a scarf,
                     pointing up toward the head/neck
        │
        ▼
  [3] Staircase   ── a golden CROWN (coronary) resting
                     on the bottom step, circling a heart

  Recall = walk the hall again, "see" each image.
```

The sillier and more vivid the image, the better it sticks.

### Do mnemonics work? Yes — with limits

They work impressively for their narrow job. Dresler et al. (2017), in *Neuron*, trained memory-naïve adults with 40 days of method-of-loci practice: recall jumped from about **26 to 62 words** out of 72. World-class memory athletes hit about **71 of 72**, and — remarkably — trainees' brain connectivity shifted toward the athletes' pattern, an effect still visible **four months** later. For vocabulary, Atkinson and Raugh (1975) found the **keyword method** produced huge immediate gains — in one study roughly **88% versus 28%** for controls.

But be clear-eyed. Mnemonics help you recall *arbitrary facts* — they do **not** build deep understanding or transfer. And their gains can be fragile: keyword-method benefits often *fade faster* over long delays than plain studying. So reserve mnemonics for genuinely arbitrary material, pair keyword images with pictures, and — as always — back them up with spaced retrieval so they don't decay.

**In short:** Mnemonics like the memory palace are superb for memorizing arbitrary lists, but they aid recall of facts, not real understanding — and they still need review to last.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating dual coding as decoration.** Adding cute, irrelevant images splits your attention instead of helping. *Fix:* use a *relevant* diagram that carries the meaning, placed next to the words.
- **Rereading and highlighting.** This is shallow processing that breeds false confidence — you mistake familiarity for learning. *Fix:* close the book and generate the idea from memory instead.
- **Asking "why" then instantly reading the answer.** You throw away the generation effect. *Fix:* struggle to answer first — even a wrong guess helps — *then* check.
- **Studying only one example.** One example traps you in surface details and blocks transfer. *Fix:* study two or three varied examples and ask what they share.
- **Making examples too vivid.** Overly concrete detail (Kaminski et al., 2008) can hide the real principle. *Fix:* build intuition with the concrete, then fade to the abstract structure.
- **Over-trusting mnemonics.** They recall facts but don't create understanding, and they decay. *Fix:* use them for arbitrary lists only, and review with spacing.

## Key Takeaways

- **Elaboration** means asking *why* and *how* a fact is true and tying it to what you already know — it builds many mental roads back to a memory.
- **Self-explanation** matters: Chi et al. (1989) found top physics students generated far more explanations (~52 vs 18) while studying worked examples.
- **Levels of processing** (Craik & Lockhart, 1972): deep, meaning-based processing beats shallow — Craik & Tulving (1975) found semantic judgments roughly doubled recognition versus surface ones.
- **The generation effect** (Slamecka & Graf, 1978): producing an answer beats reading it, with an average effect size of about **d ≈ 0.40** across 86 studies (Bertsch et al., 2007).
- **Dual coding** (Paivio): pair words with a relevant picture. Paivio & Csapo (1973) found pictures recalled more than twice as well as words.
- **Learning styles are a myth**, but dual coding is different — visuals help *everyone*; the "distinctiveness" debate (Higdon et al., 2025) doesn't change the practical advice.
- **Two examples beat one** (Gick & Holyoak, 1983): comparing varied concrete examples lets you extract the shared structure and *transfer* it — but too-vivid examples can hurt (Kaminski et al., 2008).
- **Mnemonics** work for arbitrary material: method-of-loci training took recall from ~26 to ~62 of 72 words (Dresler et al., 2017); the keyword method hit ~88% vs 28% (Atkinson & Raugh, 1975) — but gains can fade without review.
- **These are the supporting cast:** Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated elaboration and self-explanation "moderate," below the "high" utility of practice testing and spacing — use them to *enrich* those two, not replace them.
