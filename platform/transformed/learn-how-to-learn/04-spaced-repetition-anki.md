Two students study for the same exam. Both put in exactly ten hours.

Maya does all ten the night before - one heroic, coffee-fuelled marathon. Leo does one hour a day for ten days, quizzing himself each time. Same material, same total effort. But Maya walks in foggy and drained, remembers a scattering of facts, and forgets almost everything by next month. Leo walks in calm, remembers most of it, and still knows it weeks later.

That gap has nothing to do with talent. It's about *when* the studying happened. Leo used one of the two most powerful learning tools ever discovered - and it costs nothing but a little planning.

**In short:** you can double how much you remember without adding a single minute of study, just by changing the timing. This post shows you exactly how - from a stack of paper cards to the free software millions of doctors and language learners swear by - so you can build your first deck this week.

## Why this matters

Most of what you study leaks straight back out. You read a chapter, feel like you understood it, and a week later it's mostly gone. That's not a personal failing - it's just [how memory works](/blog/learn-how-to-learn/02-how-memory-works). And it means a huge share of the hours you pour into learning are quietly wasted.

Spaced repetition is the fix, and it's the closest thing to a free lunch that learning science offers. Same hours, far more retained. It's why medical students survive brutal board exams, why serious language learners hit fluency, and why the technique keeps landing at the very top when researchers rank study methods.

The best part: you don't need to be smarter or work harder. You just need to move your reviews to the right moments. Let's start with the discovery that made all of this possible.

## The oldest finding in memory science

Back in the 1880s, a German psychologist named **Hermann Ebbinghaus** ran a strange, lonely experiment. He memorised long lists of nonsense syllables - things like "WID" and "ZOF" - then tested himself over and over to measure how fast he forgot them.

What he found is now called the **forgetting curve**: memory fades fast at first, then levels off. Learn something today, and without review you might lose more than half of it within days.

But he found something hopeful too. Each time he *re-studied* the material, the forgetting slowed. The curve got flatter. Memory isn't a bucket that leaks at a fixed rate - it's more like a muscle that toughens a little each time you use it.

That's the **spacing effect**: you remember far more when you spread study out over time instead of cramming it all at once. Psychologists call the spread-out version *distributed practice* and the cram-it-all version *massed practice*.

How strong is the evidence? Unusually strong for psychology. In 2006, a team pooled together **839 comparisons from 317 experiments** - one of the biggest reviews of its kind. Spacing beat cramming in about **96% of the studies**. On average, it lifted recall from roughly **47% to 61%**. Same material, same effort, a huge jump - purely from changing the timing.

## Why spacing beats cramming

So why does spreading study out work so much better? Picture **watering a plant**.

Dump a whole watering can on a seedling at once and most of the water runs straight off the soil, wasted. Water a little at a time, on a schedule that stretches out as the roots grow deeper, and it sinks in. Cramming is the flood; spacing is the steady watering. Your brain, like the soil, can only absorb so much at once.

There's a subtler reason too. When you space reviews out, you give yourself time to *start* forgetting - and that's a good thing. Recalling something that has begun to slip takes effort, and that effort is exactly what tells your brain "this matters, keep it." When you cram, the answer is still sitting in the front of your mind, so pulling it up is easy and teaches your brain almost nothing.

This is where the *other* great memory tool comes in, and we'll pair it with spacing all the way through: **retrieval practice**, also called [**active recall**](/blog/learn-how-to-learn/05-active-recall-study-technique). It means actively pulling an answer *out* of your memory - like with a flashcard - instead of just rereading. Rereading feels productive but is mostly passive. Retrieval is the mental equivalent of a workout.

### How far apart should reviews be?

Researchers answered this with a neat rule of thumb: **the ideal gap between reviews is about 10-20% of how long you want to remember something.**

- Want to remember it for a **year**? Review it roughly **once a month**.
- Want it for a **week**? Review **every day** or so.

The longer you need the memory to last, the wider your spacing should stretch.

The most striking proof comes from a remarkable *nine-year* study of people learning Spanish vocabulary. The headline result: **13 study sessions spaced 56 days apart produced the same long-term recall as 26 sessions spaced only 14 days apart.** Half the total work, equal memory - measured one to five years later. Wider spacing wasn't just as good. It was twice as *efficient*.

Here's the whole contrast at a glance:

| | Cramming (massed) | Spacing (distributed) |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | All at once | Spread over days/weeks |
| Feels like | Smooth, easy, confident | Harder, more effortful |
| Short-term result | Looks good | Also good |
| Long-term result | Forgotten fast | Sticks for months/years |
| Effort per fact remembered | High (wasteful) | Low (efficient) |
| Typical recall | ~47% | ~61% |

Notice one detail in that table: spacing *feels harder*. That's not a bug. The effort is the point - and it's why so many people abandon it for the smooth comfort of cramming, then wonder why nothing stuck.

## The Leitner box: spaced repetition you can hold in your hand

Knowing that you *should* space reviews is one thing. Actually tracking which card to review when is another - and this is where a clever German journalist comes in.

In his 1972 book *So lernt man lernen* ("How to Learn to Learn"), **Sebastian Leitner** described a beautifully simple physical system now called the **Leitner box**. All you need is a stack of flashcards and five boxes (or five labelled dividers in one long box). Here's the entire idea:

- Every new card starts in **Box 1**.
- **Box 1** you review **every day**. Box 2 every few days. Box 3 weekly. Box 4 every two weeks. Box 5 monthly.
- Get a card **right**, and it graduates to the *next* box up - so you'll see it less often.
- Get a card **wrong**, and it falls all the way back to **Box 1** - so you'll drill it daily until it sticks.

Think of it as a **conveyor belt**. Every card you know well rides further down the line and is seen less and less. Every card you stumble on gets yanked back to the start. Over time, your hardest cards cluster in Box 1 where you drill them, and your solid cards drift into Box 5 for a lazy monthly tune-up.

The genius is that the boxes do the scheduling *for* you. You never have to decide what to review - the system decides. Hard cards automatically get more attention; mastered cards automatically get left alone. That's spaced repetition, running on nothing but cardboard.

If you want to start today with zero technology, this is the way. Grab index cards, label five boxes, and go. Plenty of people find the physical, tactile version keeps them motivated in a way apps never do.

## Anki: the Leitner box, automated

The paper box is wonderful, but it has limits. Five boxes means only five possible schedules, and once you have thousands of cards, shuffling paper gets clumsy. Computers can do better - and here's where our next character enters.

In the 1980s, a frustrated biochemistry student in Poznań, Poland, named **Piotr Woźniak** turned himself into a human memory experiment. He logged years of data on his own recall - exactly when he remembered things and when he forgot - and built a scheduling formula from it. In his 1990 master's thesis, he published the algorithm, called **SM-2**, openly and for free. That single act seeded nearly every modern flashcard app, including **Anki**, the free program most learners use today.

SM-2 is really just the Leitner idea with the dial turned up. Instead of five fixed boxes, *each individual card* gets its own personalised schedule. The key piece is the **ease factor** - a per-card multiplier that decides how fast a card's gaps grow. A new card typically comes back after **1 day, then 6 days, and after that the gap multiplies by the ease factor** (which starts at 2.5). So a card might return after 1 day, 6, 15, 38... stretching further each time you get it right. It's Cepeda's "10-20%" rule, calculated automatically for every card.

When you review a card in Anki, you don't sort it into a box. You just tell the app how it went with one of four buttons:

- **Again** - I forgot. (Card resets to a short interval.)
- **Hard** - I got it, but it was a struggle.
- **Good** - I remembered it fine.
- **Easy** - Instant, no effort. (The gap jumps up a lot.)

Based on your honest answer, Anki reschedules the card to reappear at just the right moment - ideally right before you'd otherwise forget it.

One more term worth knowing: a **leech** is a card you keep failing over and over. Anki flags leeches automatically (by default after 8 lapses) and can suspend them, so a handful of troublemakers don't quietly eat all your review time.

A quick honest note on the algorithm. Woźniak's original uses *expanding* gaps, which feels obviously right - but one study found that plain, *equally-spaced* reviews actually beat expanding ones for durable, two-days-later memory. The reassuring conclusion: the exact *shape* of the schedule matters far less than simply spacing at all. Modern Anki has since moved to a smarter default scheduler called **FSRS**, which uses your personal review history to predict when you're about to forget each card. You don't need to understand any of this to benefit. The lesson is simple: **don't agonise over settings. Any real spacing crushes cramming.**

## Writing cards that actually work

Here's the truth that trips up almost every beginner: **spaced repetition only works as well as your cards do.** A perfect schedule can't rescue a badly written card. So this may be the most practical section in the whole post.

The single most important rule comes from Woźniak himself - the **minimum information principle**: put *one* fact on *one* card. Small, simple cards are fast to review, easy to remember, and easy to schedule. Big, stuffed cards - especially long lists - are what Woźniak calls memory poison, because you'll get *part* of them right and *part* wrong every single time, so they never settle.

When you need to learn a list or a sentence, use a **cloze deletion** - a fill-in-the-blank card that hides one word, like "The capital of France is [...]." Cloze cards are quick to make, easy to recall, and let you break a big fact into several small, single-idea cards.

Woźniak's other golden rule is even more basic: **understand it first, then memorise it.** His rule number one is literally "Do not learn if you do not understand." Flashcards are brilliant for *facts and associations* - vocabulary, dates, formulas, definitions - but weak for *understanding, reasoning, and skills*. Memorising an orphaned fact you don't actually grasp is a recipe for a card you'll fail forever.

Here's what good and bad cards look like side by side:

| Bad card | Why it fails | Good card |
|---|---|---|
| Front: "France?" → a paragraph on history, geography, and cuisine | Crams many facts onto one card; you can never get it fully "right" | Front: "Capital of France?" → "Paris" |
| Front: "List all 12 cranial nerves" | A long list means partial failure every time (memory poison) | 12 small cloze cards: "Cranial nerve I is the [...] nerve." → "olfactory" |
| Front: "What is photosynthesis?" (you don't yet understand it) | Memorising before understanding = a permanent leech | Learn the concept first, *then*: "Photosynthesis makes glucose and [...]" → "oxygen" |
| Front: a blurry wall of text | Slow to read, easy to misjudge, boring to review | A card with a clear image or a single cloze deletion |

Two more tips. Use **images** wherever you can - a picture is often faster to make and easier to recall than a sentence. And always **grade yourself honestly.** It's tempting to press "Easy" just to clear your queue faster, but that lies to the scheduler and quietly destroys the timing that makes the whole system work. If you forgot, press "Again." The system only helps you if you tell it the truth.

## Does it really work? The honest limits

Let's ask the fair question: how much does all this actually pay off in the real world?

The broadest verdict comes from a landmark 2013 review of ten popular study techniques - highlighting, rereading, summarising, and the rest. Only *two* earned the top "high utility" rating: **distributed practice** (spacing) and **practice testing** (retrieval). Those are precisely the two ingredients that spaced-repetition flashcards combine. When leading researchers [rank study methods](/blog/learn-how-to-learn/14-learning-techniques-cheat-sheet), the exact thing you've just learned sits at the very top.

There's real-world data too. In medicine, students grinding for the brutal **USMLE Step 1** board exam lean heavily on shared Anki decks. One study found a dose-response link: roughly **every 1,700 flashcards reviewed was associated with about one extra point** on the exam.

But be honest about what that means. It's a *correlation*, not proof. Hard-working, motivated students are the ones who choose to grind thousands of cards in the first place, so some of that boost is just diligence showing up. The effect is real and worth having - but it's modest and tangled with who uses the tool. Anki is a powerful lever, not magic that pours knowledge into your head.

And remember the limits. Spaced repetition is superb for facts and associations, but it will not, by itself, teach you to *reason*, *solve problems*, or *do* a skill. It's the memory scaffolding underneath understanding - not a substitute for it.

## Common misconceptions

A few beliefs quietly wreck people's results. Here's the reality.

- **"Spaced repetition means I can cram it into one big session."** Reviewing a giant batch the night before defeats the entire point. The schedule only works if you do a small amount *every day*.
- **"Bigger cards are more efficient - more facts per card."** The opposite. Long lists and paragraph answers guarantee constant partial failures. One fact per card; split lists into cloze cards.
- **"More new cards means faster progress."** Piling on hundreds of new cards a day builds a review *debt* that snowballs into burnout. Start with 10-20 a day and let the load stabilise first.
- **"I should memorise it now and understand it later."** Orphaned facts you don't grasp become permanent leeches. Learn the concept first, *then* make the card.
- **"I need the perfect algorithm."** Fiddling endlessly with settings is just procrastination. Accept the defaults, write good cards, show up.

## How to use this - start this week

You don't need a system, an app subscription, or a weekend of setup. You need one small deck and seven days.

1. **Build a 20-card deck.** Pick one thing you genuinely want to learn - 20 foreign words, 20 anatomy terms, 20 historical dates. Write each as a single-fact card following the minimum information principle. Paper Leitner box or Anki, your choice.
2. **Review it every day for seven days straight.** Set a fixed time - after breakfast, on the commute. The daily habit matters more than the length; even five minutes counts. Grade yourself honestly, every card.
3. **Turn one messy card into cloze cards.** Find a card crammed with a list or a fat paragraph and split it into three or four fill-in-the-blank cloze cards. Notice how much easier those small cards feel to review.
4. **When a backlog hits, don't panic.** Miss a few days and see 200 cards due? Chip away at a fixed number each day instead of trying to clear it in one sitting. A backlog you nibble at is survivable; a backlog you flee from ends the habit.

## Conclusion

If you remember one thing, remember this: **you don't get more memory by studying harder - you get it by studying at the right moments.** Ebbinghaus found the [forgetting curve](/blog/learn-how-to-learn/03-forgetting-curve-spacing-effect) over a century ago, and every spaced review you do flattens it a little more. Same hours, twice the memory. That's the whole trick.

But notice the quiet catch running through this entire post. Spacing and retrieval build the *memory* underneath knowledge - they don't build the *understanding* on top of it. A perfectly scheduled deck of facts you never truly grasped is just a very efficient way to memorise noise. So the real question isn't only *when* to review. It's *what deserves a card at all* - and how to make sure you understand something before you ever try to remember it. That's the thread we pull next.
