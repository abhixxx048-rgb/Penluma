"We migrated the database in March." That sentence is true, complete, and instantly forgettable. Now try this one: "Our support team was drowning in database timeouts every Monday morning; in March we migrated to a new system, and now Monday mornings are quiet." Same fact. One version disappears the second you stop reading it. The other one sticks.

The difference isn't talent. It's structure. And the good news is you don't have to invent one from scratch every time you need to make a point in a meeting, a pitch, or an interview chair - you can borrow one.

## Why this matters

Most people think [good storytellers](/blog/communication-and-influence/16-why-storytelling-works) are just naturally more interesting. What they actually have is a skeleton they've learned to reach for - a shape that turns "here's what happened" into "here's why you should care." You don't see the skeleton because it's hidden well, but it's always there, holding the story up.

Once you know a handful of these shapes, you stop staring at a blank slide or a blinking cursor wondering how to open. You just ask: is this a thirty-second pitch, or a ten-minute case study? Then you pick the matching template and fill it in. That's the entire skill.

## The three ingredients every story needs

Before the structures themselves, it's worth knowing what they're all built from: a **character** (someone [the audience can follow](/blog/communication-and-influence/04-how-to-know-your-audience) - often "you," a customer, a team, or a user), a **conflict** (a problem or obstacle in that character's way), and a **change** (how things are different at the end).

Take any one of the three away and you're left with a status update, not a story. That's the whole gap between "we migrated the database" and the version above - a character (the support team), a conflict (the timeouts), and a change (quiet Mondays).

Memorize that formula. It's the test you can run on anything you're about to say out loud: does this have a character, a conflict, and a change - or is it just a fact wearing a story's clothes?

## The basic arc: setup, conflict, resolution

The oldest and most useful shape in storytelling comes from Gustav Freytag, a 19th-century novelist who studied classic plays and noticed they all rose and fell in the same pattern. It's now called **Freytag's Pyramid**, and nearly every structure below is a descendant of it. Stripped down for work, it has three moves:

1. **Setup** - introduce the character and what "normal" looks like.
2. **Conflict** - something disrupts that normal and creates tension.
3. **Resolution** - the tension resolves into a new normal.

The full version adds rising action and a climax in the middle, and that middle part matters more than people think. Tension has to build before it can pay off. Jump straight from "we had a problem" to "we fixed it" and the story goes flat, because nobody had time to worry about the outcome.

Think of it like a rollercoaster. The slow click uphill is what makes the drop mean anything - a coaster that starts at the bottom and stays flat isn't a ride, it's a sidewalk.

**Example:** In a project retrospective, instead of "the vendor was late, then we fixed it," a project manager says: "Three weeks before launch, everything was on track. Then our main vendor missed a shipment and we had zero backup plan. For 48 hours we didn't know if we'd hit the date. We found a local supplier overnight, and launched on time." People lean in at the 48-hour mark because the setup made them care first.

## The Story Spine: a fill-in-the-blanks formula

If Freytag's Pyramid is the shape, the **Story Spine** is the sentence template that guarantees you build it correctly every time, without thinking about it. It was created around 1991 by improv teacher Kenn Adams, and it later became famous when a former Pixar artist revealed it as one of the studio's internal writing rules.

- **Once upon a time...** - the character and their normal situation.
- **Every day...** - the routine, before anything changes.
- **Until one day...** - something disrupts it.
- **Because of that...** - the first consequence.
- **Because of that...** - a second consequence, things escalate.
- **Until finally...** - the turning point.
- **And ever since then...** - the new normal.

The repeated "because of that" is the secret ingredient. Most boring workplace updates fail because they list events - "we did A, then B, then C" - without ever showing that one thing *caused* the next. The Story Spine makes skipping that step almost impossible.

**Example - a product update told through the spine:** "Once upon a time, our onboarding flow had eight steps. Every day, about 40% of new users dropped off before finishing. Until one day, we pulled the support tickets and found users were confused at step four. Because of that, we ran five user interviews. Because of that, we realized the confusion was one unclear button label. Until finally, we rewrote that screen. And ever since then, drop-off has fallen to 12%." Notice that's really just a status update - the spine just gave it a pulse.

Keep those seven phrases saved somewhere. Before your next standup or pitch, run your update through them in your head for thirty seconds. You'll almost always find a sharper way to say it.

## The Hero's Journey, simplified

The **Hero's Journey** is the biggest structure in this list, first mapped by mythologist Joseph Campbell in 1949 after he studied hundreds of myths and noticed the same cycle everywhere: a hero leaves the ordinary world, faces trials, and returns transformed. It's since become one of the most borrowed structures in business - especially for customer stories, career narratives, and turnaround stories.

You don't need all of Campbell's original stages. Five cover almost everything at work:

1. **Ordinary world** - who the character is, what normal looks like.
2. **Call to adventure** - a challenge or opportunity that can't be ignored.
3. **Trials** - the character struggles, tries things, sometimes fails.
4. **Transformation** - a breakthrough changes how they see things.
5. **Return, changed** - back to normal life, but wiser or more capable.

Think of it as Freytag's arc stretched out and made personal. Instead of just "tension rose and fell," it asks what this specific person learned and how they're different now - the difference between a weather report and a memoir.

**Example:** Most sales case studies follow this shape without naming it. Ordinary world: the client's finance team closed the books manually every month. Call to adventure: a new regulation cut their filing window by five days. Trials: they tried temps and spreadsheets - both failed. Transformation: they adopted an automation tool and rebuilt the process. Return, changed: they now close in three days instead of ten, and the finance lead got promoted for it.

This is exactly why customer case studies are so persuasive - they're Hero's Journeys where the customer is the hero and the product is the mentor handing them a tool, not the hero itself. The most common way people wreck this structure is making themselves or their company the star of their own pitch. Audiences trust stories where the customer struggles and grows; they get suspicious the moment the vendor becomes the main character.

## Before-After-Bridge: the fastest structure you'll ever need

Where the Hero's Journey takes its time, **Before-After-Bridge (BAB)** is built for speed. It's a three-step structure from [persuasive copywriting](/blog/communication-and-influence/21-persuasive-writing-frameworks-aida-pas-bab), and it's one of the fastest ways to make someone care about a solution in under thirty seconds.

- **Before** - describe the current painful state in vivid, specific terms.
- **After** - paint what life looks like once the problem is gone.
- **Bridge** - introduce the solution that gets someone from Before to After.

**Example - pitching an internal tool in a two-minute meeting slot:** "Before: right now, every invoice error takes someone forty-five minutes to trace through three different systems, and we lose a full day a week to this. After: imagine every invoice error flagged automatically, cause identified in under a minute. Bridge: this dashboard does exactly that - built, tested with finance, ready to roll out next sprint."

Notice how little time gets spent on "how." BAB's power is emotional contrast, not technical detail. Save it for the moments where you genuinely have thirty seconds or less - an elevator pitch, an email's opening line, the first slide of a deck. Save the longer arc structures for when you have room to build real tension.

## In medias res: starting in the middle of the action

**In medias res** is Latin for "into the middle of things." Instead of opening at the calm beginning, you start at a high-tension moment and fill in the backstory after. Homer used it in *The Odyssey* almost 3,000 years ago, and it's the reason so many movies open on an explosion before flashing back to "three days earlier."

For work, this solves a real problem: most audiences have short attention and mentally check out during a slow setup. Opening in the action grabs them immediately - the "why did we get here" backstory becomes something they're now curious about, instead of something they have to sit through first.

**Example:** Compare two openings of the same incident report. Standard order: "Our servers ran well for two years. Last Tuesday we deployed a routine update. At 2am, everything went down." In medias res: "At 2:03am last Tuesday, every customer-facing service went dark at once. Here's what happened, and what we changed so it can't happen again." The second version has already earned the room's attention before explaining anything.

Use this for incident reports, interview answers, and any [presentation opening](/blog/communication-and-influence/10-how-to-structure-a-presentation) where you're worried about losing people in the first sixty seconds.

## STAR: the structure built for interviews

**STAR** is the one structure in this list designed for a single high-stakes moment: answering a behavioral interview question like "Tell me about a time you dealt with conflict on a team." It was introduced in 1974 by the consulting firm DDI as part of a hiring method built on the idea that past behavior predicts future behavior, and it's now the default expectation in most corporate interviews.

- **Situation** - set the scene briefly (roughly a fifth of your answer).
- **Task** - your specific responsibility or goal (a short slice).
- **Action** - what *you* personally did, step by step (the bulk - about 60%).
- **Result** - what happened, ideally with a number attached.

The single biggest mistake candidates make is spending most of their answer on Situation and Task - explaining the whole backstory - and then rushing Action and Result, which is exactly backwards. Interviewers are evaluating what *you* did, not how interesting your situation was.

**Example:** Question: "Tell me about a time you missed a deadline." Situation: "Our team was six weeks into a client project when our lead developer left unexpectedly." Task: "I was asked to absorb his workload while still owning my own deliverables." Action: "I re-scoped my own tasks, delegated two lower-priority items to a teammate with spare capacity, and negotiated a five-day extension by showing the client a revised, realistic timeline." Result: "We delivered five days late with zero quality issues, and the client renewed the contract the next quarter."

Watch your pronouns here too. Answering with "we" instead of "I" is the second most common mistake - if every sentence of your Action section is about the team, the interviewer can't tell what you personally brought to the table.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Storytelling means adding fluff or exaggeration."** Not even close. Every structure above works with true, plain facts - the structure is what makes them memorable, not embellishment.
- **"Longer is more persuasive."** Before-After-Bridge proves the opposite. Some of the most persuasive stories at work are three sentences long.
- **"The Hero's Journey means telling everyone how great my company is."** The most common way to ruin it is making yourself the hero. The customer struggles and grows; you're the guide who hands them a tool.
- **"In an interview, more context makes me look more thorough."** It usually does the opposite - a long Situation and Task section at the expense of Action reads as evasive, not thorough.

## How to use this

1. **Diagnose the moment first.** Ask how much time you actually have - fifteen seconds, ninety seconds, or ten minutes - before picking a structure.
2. **Match the structure to the length:** Before-After-Bridge for pitches and openers, the Story Spine for standups and quick updates, STAR for interview answers, the Hero's Journey for case studies and career stories, in medias res whenever you need to grab a distracted room, and the full arc whenever you have room to build real tension.
3. **Run the three-ingredient test.** Before you speak or write, check: does this have a character, a conflict, and a change? If not, it's a status update - add the missing piece.
4. **For interview prep specifically**, time yourself. Action should eat roughly 60% of your answer - if Situation and Task are running long, cut them.
5. **For pitches and case studies**, make the other person the hero. You're the mentor handing over the tool, never the star of your own story.
6. **Keep a cheat sheet.** Save the table below somewhere you'll actually see it before your next meeting.

| Structure | Best for | Typical length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic Arc (Freytag) | Building and releasing tension | 1-5 minutes |
| Story Spine | Standups, status updates | 30-90 seconds |
| Hero's Journey | Case studies, career and turnaround stories | 3-10 minutes |
| Before-After-Bridge | Pitches, opening lines, emails | 15-45 seconds |
| In medias res | Incident reports, grabbing a distracted room | Any length (it's an opening move) |
| STAR | Interview answers, performance reviews | 60-90 seconds |

## Conclusion

No single structure here is "the best" one - each is a tool sized for a specific job, from a fifteen-second pitch to a ten-minute case study. The moment you stop hunting for the right words from scratch and start reaching for the right shape, your updates, pitches, and interview answers stop sounding like reports and start sounding like something worth remembering.

Knowing the shape is only half the job, though - the other half is having something worth saying inside it. If you've ever wondered why the same well-structured pitch lands with one audience and falls flat with another, the answer usually isn't the story at all. It's what's happening [underneath the conversation](/blog/communication-and-influence/12-body-language-nonverbal-communication) before a single word gets said.
