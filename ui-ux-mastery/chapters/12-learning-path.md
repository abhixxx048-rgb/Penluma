# Chapter 12: Your Learning Path: Books, Courses, Practice, and Mistakes to Avoid

## Why this chapter matters

You have reached the end of the book, but not the end of the learning. That is not a problem. It is the whole point.

Here is the single most important idea in this chapter: **UX is a practice, not a body of knowledge you finish.** It is like cooking or playing guitar. You do not become a cook by reading recipes. You become a cook by making meals, tasting them, and fixing what went wrong. Reading and courses are the scaffolding. Your real skill is the building, and you raise it by shipping work, showing it to people, and revising.

This chapter gives you a realistic plan to keep growing on your own. We will cover the best books (ranked, with what each actually teaches), the best courses for different budgets, the best websites and pattern libraries, how to practice so you actually improve, the quiet mistakes that sabotage beginners, and a month-by-month plan you can follow for 6 to 12 months.

One promise before we start: you can learn UX self-taught. Many working designers did. But only if you practice on real problems and get feedback. Not if you binge tutorials and admire pretty screens. The difference between those two paths is the difference between a hobby and a career.

Let's build your path.

## The Books, Ranked for a Beginner

There are hundreds of design books. You do not need most of them. Below are eight that matter, ranked by "read-first value" for someone starting out.

A quick note on why old books still top the list. Design tools change constantly. The software everyone uses today (Figma) will one day be replaced, just as the tools before it were. But the *principles* do not change. A law about how fast a human can move a mouse to a button is as true today as it was 70 years ago. So the classics endure. Learn the "why," not just the "where to click."

Here is the ranked list at a glance, then details on each.

| # | Book | Author | What it teaches | Best for |
|---|------|--------|-----------------|----------|
| 1 | The Design of Everyday Things | Don Norman | The "why" of design; core mental models | Building the mindset |
| 2 | Don't Make Me Think | Steve Krug | Everyday usability judgment | The friendliest start |
| 3 | Refactoring UI | Wathan & Schoger | Making screens look good | Visual polish |
| 4 | Laws of UX | Jon Yablonski | Psychology as design laws | A reference while designing |
| 5 | About Face | Alan Cooper | Deep interaction design | Intermediate reference |
| 6 | Hooked | Nir Eyal | Habit-forming products (and ethics) | Product thinking |
| 7 | 100 Things... About People | Susan Weinschenk | Behavioral science, bite-sized | Dip-in reference |
| 8 | Articulating Design Decisions | Tom Greever | Defending your work to others | Career survival |

### 1. The Design of Everyday Things - Don Norman

This is the foundational "why" of the whole field. Don Norman is often called the godfather of UX. He actually coined the term "user experience" while working at Apple in the early 1990s.

The book teaches the mental frameworks every designer uses forever. A few plain-language definitions:

- **Affordance**: what an object lets you do. A flat plate on a door affords pushing. A handle affords pulling.
- **Signifier**: a cue that tells you about the affordance. The word "PUSH" printed on the door is a signifier.
- **Mapping**: the relationship between a control and its effect. On a stove, if the front-left knob controls the front-left burner, that is good mapping.
- **Feedback**: the system telling you something happened. A button that changes color when tapped gives feedback.

Norman's big lesson: when someone struggles with a door, a stove, or a microwave, that is a *design* failure, not a user failure. There is even a nickname for badly designed doors that you push when you should pull: "Norman doors." Once you learn this, you cannot unsee it. You will notice bad design everywhere, and that noticing is the start of thinking like a designer.

This book is denser than the others. Read it slowly.

### 2. Don't Make Me Think - Steve Krug

The friendliest possible starting point. It is short, funny, full of pictures, and you can read it in a weekend. It is still the number-one usability best seller on Amazon after more than 20 years.

The whole book hangs on one law: **"Don't make me think."** A good design is self-evident. You should not have to puzzle out how to use it. Krug shows how people actually behave online: they do not read pages, they scan them. They do not find the *best* option, they grab the first one that seems to work (this is called "satisficing"). They muddle through.

The second half is a superb, plain guide to cheap do-it-yourself usability testing (watching real people try to use your design). Krug's famous line: **"Testing one user is 100 percent better than testing none."** Keep that sentence in your pocket forever.

### 3. Refactoring UI - Adam Wathan & Steve Schoger

This is the bridge from "I understand UX principles" to "my screens actually look good." It fixes the beginner's most visible weakness: interfaces that look amateur.

It is refreshingly practical, giving tweak-by-tweak advice: how to use spacing, how to build hierarchy with size and weight and color, why you should limit your color palette, how to use shadows to make things feel raised, and the golden rule to **work in grayscale first** (design in black, white, and gray before adding any color, so you are forced to get the layout right). It pairs with a companion website and video series.

One honest note: this is UI polish, not UX research. It makes things pretty and clear. It does not teach you to find the right problem. You need both.

### 4. Laws of UX - Jon Yablonski

This book turns cognitive psychology (the science of how the mind works) into about 10 memorable design laws you can name and cite. It has a free companion website that catalogs 30 principles. If you like structure, you will love this.

The highest-value laws to know by name:

- **Hick's Law**: the more choices you offer, the longer a decision takes. So simplify menus.
- **Fitts's Law**: the time to hit a target depends on its size and distance. So make tap targets big and close.
- **Jakob's Law**: users spend most of their time on *other* sites, so they expect yours to work the same way. Respect conventions.
- **Miller's Law**: working memory holds only about 7 items (give or take 2). So chunk information into groups.
- **Peak-End Rule**: people judge an experience by its most intense moment and its ending, not the average.
- **Aesthetic-Usability Effect**: attractive interfaces are *perceived* as easier to use, and people forgive their flaws.
- **Doherty Threshold**: keep the system's response under **400 milliseconds** to keep people engaged and productive.
- **Von Restorff Effect**: the item that looks different is the one people remember. Use it for your main button.
- **Serial Position Effect**: people best remember the first and last items in a list. Put key navigation there.
- **Goal-Gradient Effect**: motivation rises as people get closer to a goal. This is why progress bars work.

Keep this one open while you design.

### 5. About Face - Alan Cooper

The heavyweight, around 700 pages. Think of it as the interaction-design bible. It introduces **goal-directed design** (designing around what the user is trying to achieve) and popularized **personas** (fictional but research-based profiles of your typical users). It goes deep on designing for how people actually work, and puts behavior before features.

This is not a first read. Come back to it once your fundamentals are solid, and use it as a reference.

### 6. Hooked - Nir Eyal

This teaches the **Hook Model**, a four-step loop that builds habits: **Trigger → Action → Variable Reward → Investment.** A notification triggers you, you open the app (action), you get something unpredictable and pleasing (variable reward), and you put a little of yourself in, like a post or a follow (investment), which makes you come back. It explains how apps like Instagram and Slack keep you engaged.

Read this one critically. The same techniques that build healthy engagement can cross into manipulation, which is where "dark patterns" (tricks that push users into things they did not want) begin. Eyal's follow-up book, *Indistractable*, is the ethical counterweight. Learn the model, but always ask whether you are helping the user or exploiting them.

### 7. 100 Things Every Designer Needs to Know About People - Susan Weinschenk

A behavioral-science reference organized as 100 short, evidence-backed principles about how people see, read, remember, decide, and get motivated. Things like how peripheral vision works, how the brain recognizes patterns, why multitasking fails, and how culture changes the meaning of a color. There is a follow-up called *100 More Things*.

This is a dip-in book. Read a principle when you need it. It grounds your design choices in psychology instead of taste.

### 8. Articulating Design Decisions - Tom Greever

The most underrated book on this list, and maybe the most important for your career. It teaches a *soft* skill that decides whether your good design ever ships: how to explain and defend your decisions to stakeholders (the bosses, clients, and teammates who approve or block your work).

It covers listening, building relationships, handling the classic "make the logo bigger" request, rescuing meetings that fall apart, and getting buy-in. The blunt truth it delivers: this skill is what separates employed designers from talented but unemployed ones. Read it once you are collaborating with clients, product managers, or engineers.

**Honorable mentions** worth knowing by name: *Lean UX* (Gothelf & Seiden) for process, *Sprint* (Jake Knapp) for fast design sprints, *The Elements of User Experience* (Jesse James Garrett) for the classic 5-plane model of how a product is built up in layers, and *Universal Principles of Design* as a general reference.

**In short:** Start with Krug and Norman for the mindset, add Refactoring UI to make your work look good, keep Laws of UX open as you design, and save the rest for when you need them.

## Courses and Platforms

Books build your thinking. Courses give you structure, deadlines, and sometimes a credential. Match the course to your budget and goal. Prices below are current as of 2025 to 2026, so always verify before you buy.

### Google UX Design Professional Certificate (Coursera)

- **Cost**: about $49 per month. Most people finish in 3 to 6 months, so roughly $150 to $300 total.
- **Format**: around 6 months at about 10 hours a week, 7 courses, taught by Google designers, no prerequisites.
- **Results**: Google reports that **75 percent of graduates have a positive career outcome** (a new job, raise, or promotion) within six months. Graduates get one-on-one career coaching and access to an employer network of more than 150 companies.
- **The honest caveat**: reviewers rate it modestly for making you truly job-ready. One review scored it 5 out of 10 on job-market readiness and noted weak personal mentoring. The **portfolio you build during it matters far more than the certificate itself.**

Best for: complete beginners who want an affordable, structured, credible first step. It will not, by itself, guarantee a job.

### Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g) UX Certification

- **Cost**: expensive. The foundational certificate runs roughly $3,200 to $6,000 in tuition plus about $400 per exam. The advanced "UX Master" certification runs $9,600 to $18,000.
- **Format**: complete about 5 full-day courses and pass a roughly 1-hour exam per course, online or in person.
- **Recognition**: the most authoritative credential in the field, from the firm founded by Jakob Nielsen and Don Norman. A strong signal for senior and research roles.

Best for: working practitioners (roughly 3 or more years in) leveling up or aiming at senior roles. It is overkill and over-budget for a newcomer.

### Interaction Design Foundation (IxDF)

- **Cost**: a membership model, about $16 per month for students up to about $22 per month for professionals, billed annually (roughly $180 to $264 a year). That unlocks 35+ courses.
- **Scale**: more than 130,000 graduates, positioned as the largest online design school. Includes certificates, masterclasses, local meetups, and tool discounts.

Best for: self-paced, budget-conscious learners who want breadth and a steady library. The content is text-heavy and academic, with less hand-holding than a bootcamp.

### Designlab - UX Academy

- **Cost**: the UX Academy bootcamp is about $8,499. A shorter Foundations prep course is about $499 and earns a $500 credit. Interest-free payment plans exist (with a roughly $400 servicing fee).
- **Format**: a one-on-one vetted working-designer mentor, weekly critiques with peers, a structured schedule, plus a UI module and real client capstone projects. Frequently rated a top UX bootcamp for satisfaction.

Best for: career-changers who need structure, accountability, mentorship, and a deadline, and can afford it. The real mentor and real feedback are what you are paying for over free self-study.

### Other platforms worth naming

**Uxcel** (cheap, gamified microlearning and skill tests), **CareerFoundry** and **Springboard** (mentored bootcamps, some with job guarantees), university courses on **Coursera and edX**, and **Memorisely** or **DesignerUp** for focused Figma learning.

### Free YouTube channels (high-signal)

You can learn an enormous amount for free. Good channels:

- **AJ&Smart** - design sprints, running workshops, decision-making.
- **Femke (@Femke.design)** - a senior product designer on research, prototyping, and process.
- **CareerFoundry** - structured method: framing research questions, running interviews, synthesizing findings.
- **The Futur** - the business and career side of design.
- **Flux Academy, DesignCourse (Gary Simon), Jesse Showalter** - Figma and UI tutorials.
- **UX Tools (Taylor Palmer)** - tools, usability studies, tutorials.

A warning that we will repeat later: watching videos feels like progress but is not, by itself, learning. Use these to unblock a specific problem, then go make something.

**In short:** Beginners on a budget should start with the Google Certificate or IxDF plus free YouTube. Career-changers who can invest should consider a mentored bootcamp. Skip NN/g until you are experienced.

## Websites, Newsletters, and Pattern Libraries

Split your online sources into two buckets: places that teach you to *think correctly*, and places where you *study real patterns*.

### Learn the principles (read these to think right)

- **Nielsen Norman Group (nngroup.com)** - the gold standard for research-backed usability articles. Home of Jakob Nielsen's famous **10 Usability Heuristics** (10 general rules of thumb for good interface design). Free articles, paid deep reports.
- **Baymard Institute (baymard.com)** - large-scale research on e-commerce, especially checkout. Their numbers are worth citing. Baymard reviewed more than 41,000 checkout performance scores and found that **65 percent of leading e-commerce sites score "mediocre" or worse** on checkout. The average site could gain roughly **35 percent more conversions** just from a better checkout. The average checkout has about 15 form fields (nearly 24 form elements) when it could have far fewer. And across 50 studies, the average shopping cart abandonment rate is about **70 percent**. Baymard estimates about **$260 billion** in recoverable revenue in the US and EU from fixing checkout alone. Numbers like these are gold in a portfolio case study.
- **lawsofux.com** - Jon Yablonski's free catalog of 30 psychological design laws.
- **Smashing Magazine** - practical front-end and UX articles, plus a strong weekly newsletter.
- **The primary source documents**: the WCAG accessibility guidelines from the W3C, Apple's Human Interface Guidelines, and Google's Material Design. Read the originals, not other people's summaries.
- **Company design blogs** - Stripe, Figma, Airbnb, Notion, and Shopify's "Polaris" system show how mature teams reason.

### Newsletters worth subscribing to

Pick two or three and read them weekly:

- **NN/g UX Newsletter** - research-backed.
- **UX Design Weekly** (36,000+ subscribers) - curated links.
- **Smashing Magazine newsletter**, **Really Good UX**, **UX Collective**, and **Growth.Design** (which teaches through case studies drawn as comics).

### Study real patterns (steal the how, not the pixels)

- **Mobbin** - the largest curated library of real, shipped mobile and web app screens and flows. Filter by app, by element, or by whole flow. This is the single best answer to "how do real apps actually solve this?"
- **Page Flows** - screen-*recording videos* of real user flows like onboarding, checkout, and cancellation. Video shows you motion and sequence, which static screens cannot.
- **UI Sources, Appshots, SaaSFrame** - annotated real-app flows and component collections. (Appshots alone cites more than 120,000 screens from 400-plus apps.)
- **Refactoring UI site** - before-and-after examples of UI polish.

### Dribbble and Behance - with a big caveat

**Dribbble** and **Behance** are galleries of beautiful design work. Here is the trap: much of it is *aspirational art, not usable product.* A lot of Dribbble is "concept" work made to collect likes, and much of it could never actually be built or would frustrate real users. It optimizes for looking cool in a thumbnail, not for helping a person finish a task.

Use these sites for a spark of visual inspiration. **Never copy a Dribbble flow into a real product.** When you want to learn how real interfaces work, go to shipped products through Mobbin or Page Flows. This distinction (beauty contest versus real product) is one of the most important habits you can build.

**In short:** Learn to think from NN/g, Baymard, and lawsofux.com. Learn real patterns from Mobbin and Page Flows. Treat Dribbble as an art gallery, not a manual.

## How to Practice (the part that actually builds skill)

Reading and watching are *input*. Skill comes from *output plus feedback*. Here are the practice methods, ranked by how much they actually teach you.

```
  MOST LEARNING
       ^
       |   Real projects (real users, real constraints)
       |   Redesign critiques (problem stated first)
       |   Portfolio + case-study writing
       |   Daily UI challenge (early only)
       |   Watching tutorials
       v
  LEAST LEARNING
```

### 1. Real projects (highest value)

Design something for an actual person or organization with actual constraints: a friend's small business, a local nonprofit, an open-source tool, a volunteer gig. Real users, real feedback, real tradeoffs. This is exactly what portfolios and interviews reward, because it proves you can work inside the messy limits of the real world.

### 2. Redesign critiques

Pick a real app you use. Find one *specific* usability problem. Research why it might exist. Then design the fix, and write down your reasoning. The discipline is stating the problem *before* you touch a single pixel.

There is a classic naive mistake to avoid here. Do not "redesign Spotify's home screen" just to make it prettier. That teaches you almost nothing, because you have no idea about their business goals, their data, or the reasons behind their choices. Instead, define a real problem, solve that, and honestly admit what you do not know (like their internal metrics). A focused redesign of one real problem beats a from-scratch fantasy app, because the constraints are real.

### 3. The Daily UI challenge - useful, but with big caveats

Daily UI (dailyui.co) emails you a UI prompt every day for 100 days.

**The good**: it builds tool speed in Figma, creates a daily habit, gives you a stockpile of screens, and helps absolute beginners beat the fear of a blank canvas.

**The bad, which you must understand**: it teaches UI *decoration*, not UX. You are not studying users, running tests, or handling real constraints. Because the prompts jump around (a calculator one day, a music player the next), it can build shallow, inconsistent habits. Many experienced designers advise skipping it entirely.

**The verdict**: it is fine for your first month or two to build Figma fluency and momentum. Then graduate to real projects. If you do it, make it count: add a real problem, reflect on your choices, and ask for critique.

### 4. Portfolio and case-study writing

A **case study** is a written story of one project: the problem, what you did, and the result. This is the deliverable that gets you hired. Some hard data from hiring research:

- Hiring managers want **2 or 3 strong case studies, not ten** mediocre ones.
- They spend about **60 seconds per case study**, and maybe 5 minutes on your whole portfolio. So **lead with the outcome and results first**, then explain the process.
- They care about your **thinking and tradeoffs, not just polished pictures.** As one manager put it, any junior can follow a process. Show your reasoning, your specific role, and how you worked with others.
- **Structure**: Problem → your role → research → ideation (sketches, wireframes, user flows) → final design → results and impact.
- Aim for roughly **60 to 80 percent text and 20 to 40 percent visuals** in each section. A portfolio is a story, not just a gallery.
- As a junior, your "impact" can be validated learning, improved usability, or a clarified direction. It does **not** need a revenue number.
- **Hypothetical projects are fine** as long as you clearly label them as such.

### 5. Community and feedback loops

Post your work for critique. Good places: the r/UXDesign community on Reddit, Designer Hangout, **ADPList** (free mentorship), IxDF meetups, and Discord communities. Learning to *give* critique sharpens your own eye as much as receiving it. Feedback is the mechanism that turns practice into skill. Without it, you just repeat your mistakes with more confidence.

### The one cadence rule

Spend **at least as many hours producing and revising as you spend consuming.** If you are 90 percent watching tutorials, you are not learning UX. You are entertaining yourself. Designers even have a name for this trap: "tutorial hell."

**In short:** Real projects and thoughtful redesigns teach the most. Ship finished, documented work, and always get it in front of other people.

## A Realistic 6 to 12 Month Self-Study Plan

This assumes about 10 hours a week. Treat it as a flexible scaffold, not a law. The shape follows the common roadmap: foundations in months 1 to 3, tools and projects in 4 to 6, portfolio and job-readiness after that.

```
Month:  1     2     3     4     5     6     7-9      10-12
        |-----FOUNDATIONS----|--PROJECTS--|--DEPTH--|--JOB READY--|
        tools  process visual  build  case   real    depth &     portfolio
        basics research design  E2E    study  project spec.       + apply
```

**Month 1 - Foundations and tool basics.**
Read *Don't Make Me Think* and start *The Design of Everyday Things*. Begin the Google UX Certificate (or IxDF). Learn Figma fundamentals: frames, components, **Auto Layout** (a feature that makes elements resize and space themselves automatically), variants, and basic prototyping. Warm up with the Daily UI challenge to beat the blank canvas. *Deliverable:* 8 to 10 Daily UI screens and a personal Figma cheat sheet.

**Month 2 - UX process and research.**
Learn the double-diamond (a model of design as two phases: explore wide, then narrow down, twice), user interviews, personas, journey maps, information architecture (how you organize and label content), and card sorting (a method for learning how users group things). Read *Laws of UX*. *Deliverable:* run 3 to 5 short user interviews with friends on a real problem, then write up your insights and an affinity map (grouped sticky notes of what you learned).

**Month 3 - Interaction and visual design.**
Read *Refactoring UI* and use it to redo your Month 1 screens, keeping a before-and-after. Dip into Weinschenk's *100 Things*. Study real patterns on Mobbin and Page Flows. *Deliverable:* a small component library plus one redesign critique of a real app, with the problem stated first.

**Month 4 - First end-to-end project.**
Take one product (real, or clearly labeled hypothetical) all the way through: problem → research → information architecture → wireframes → high-fidelity prototype in Figma. Test it with about 5 users and iterate. (Nielsen's rule of thumb: around 5 users per round of testing surfaces most usability problems.) *Deliverable:* a working prototype and documented test findings.

**Month 5 - Case study #1, plus accessibility.**
Write up your Month 4 project as a portfolio case study: outcome-first, mostly text, tradeoffs shown. Learn WCAG basics and audit and fix your project for accessibility. Read *Hooked* critically for product and retention thinking. *Deliverable:* one polished case study.

**Month 6 - Second real project and portfolio site.**
Do a *real* project for an actual person or organization. Build your portfolio website and write case study #2. Get both critiqued on ADPList or in communities. *Deliverable:* two case studies and a live portfolio.

**Months 7 to 9 - Depth, feedback, specialization.**
Add a third project, ideally collaborative (team up with a developer or product manager). Read *About Face* and *Articulating Design Decisions*. Give and receive structured critique regularly. Explore a specialty: research, design systems, or product design. If you are aiming at research or senior roles, you could start NN/g courses. *Deliverable:* case study #3 and a clear specialization angle.

**Months 10 to 12 - Job readiness (or client work).**
Refine your 2 or 3 strongest case studies and tighten the portfolio to lead with impact. Practice presenting your work out loud, which is real interview prep. Build a network through meetups, ADPList mentors, and LinkedIn. Apply for junior roles, internships, or freelance gigs. Keep a steady input habit: one NN/g or Smashing article plus your newsletters each week. *Deliverable:* an interview-ready portfolio and active applications.

**Ongoing, forever.**
Subscribe to two or three newsletters. Keep a "swipe file" of good patterns (Mobbin boards work well). Do one redesign critique a month. And always be shipping. UX mastery compounds. The people who get good are simply the ones who never stopped making and sharing work.

**In short:** Foundations first, then projects, then a portfolio that leads with impact. Ten hours a week for a year turns a beginner into someone employable.

## Common Mistakes

These traps are common, quietly damaging, and completely fixable. Watch for each one in your own work.

- **Starting with visuals instead of the problem.** The number-one trap. New designers open Figma and pick fonts and colors before they understand the user's task. No amount of polish saves a design that solves the wrong problem. *Fix:* always begin with "who is this for, and what are they trying to do?" Work in grayscale first.

- **Designing for yourself.** Choosing layouts and words based on your own taste. Your preferences are not data. *Fix:* define the real target user and validate with real people. Remember: you are not the user.

- **Skipping research.** Assuming you already know what users need. This leads to solving imaginary problems. *Fix:* even light research beats none. Five interviews, a look at competitors, or one usability test. Testing one user is 100 percent better than testing none.

- **Ignoring or fearing feedback.** Getting defensive in critiques, or hiding your work until it is "perfect." Design only improves through reactions from others. *Fix:* show work early and often. Treat critique as free quality-checking, not judgment. (This is exactly why Greever's book matters.)

- **Copying without understanding.** Lifting a pattern from Dribbble or a competitor without knowing *why* it works, or whether it even works. *Fix:* understand the principle behind a pattern before you reuse it, and borrow from *shipped* products, not beauty contests.

- **Neglecting accessibility.** Treating it as an afterthought. *Fix:* bake in color contrast, big enough tap targets, and keyboard and screen-reader support from the very start.

- **Chasing tools over fundamentals.** Believing that mastering Figma makes you a designer. Tools change; thinking endures. *Fix:* treat tool fluency as the price of entry, not the skill itself.

- **Tutorial hell.** Endlessly consuming, never finishing a documented case study. *Fix:* ship, document, repeat.

- **Doing too much, too fast.** Jumping between unrelated challenges with no depth (the Daily UI failure mode). *Fix:* fewer, deeper projects taken all the way through.

## Best Practices Checklist

- [ ] Read *Don't Make Me Think* and *The Design of Everyday Things* first, for the mindset.
- [ ] Keep *Refactoring UI* and *Laws of UX* nearby as you design.
- [ ] Pick one structured course that fits your budget (Google Certificate or IxDF for most beginners).
- [ ] Subscribe to 2 or 3 newsletters and read them weekly.
- [ ] Study real, shipped patterns on Mobbin and Page Flows, not Dribbble concept art.
- [ ] Start every design by naming the user and the problem, before opening any tool.
- [ ] Work in grayscale before you add color.
- [ ] Test with about 5 real users each round, and iterate on what you see.
- [ ] Spend at least as many hours producing as consuming.
- [ ] Build 2 or 3 strong case studies, each leading with the result.
- [ ] Write mostly in words (60 to 80 percent text): show your thinking and tradeoffs, not just screens.
- [ ] Post work for critique regularly (ADPList, communities) and learn to give critique too.
- [ ] Do one real project for an actual person or organization.
- [ ] Redesign-critique one real thing a month, stating the problem first.
- [ ] Keep response times feeling instant (under 400ms) wherever you can.

## Key Takeaways

- **UX is a practice, not a finished body of knowledge.** You learn it by shipping work, showing it to people, and revising, not by binging tutorials.
- **Principles endure; tools expire.** Learn the "why" behind design. The classics (Norman, Krug) stay true across every tool change.
- **Read to think, watch to unblock, but practice to improve.** Reading and video are input. Skill comes from output plus feedback.
- **Match the course to your budget and goal.** Google Certificate or IxDF for beginners, a mentored bootcamp for funded career-changers, NN/g only once you are experienced.
- **Study real products, not beauty contests.** Learn how apps actually work on Mobbin and Page Flows. Treat Dribbble as an art gallery.
- **Real projects and focused redesigns teach the most.** Design for a real person with real constraints, and always state the problem before touching pixels.
- **Your portfolio is the deliverable that gets you hired.** Two or three strong, outcome-first case studies that show your thinking beat ten polished but shallow ones.
- **Get feedback constantly.** Show work early, treat critique as free quality-checking, and learn to give critique to sharpen your own eye.
- **Follow a plan, but stay flexible.** Ten hours a week for 6 to 12 months, moving from foundations to projects to a job-ready portfolio, is enough to change your career.
- **Never stop shipping.** UX mastery compounds. The people who get good are simply the ones who kept making and sharing work.
