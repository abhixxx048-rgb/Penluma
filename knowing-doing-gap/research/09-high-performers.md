## 1. Grit — the claim, the numbers, and the correction

### The original claim (Duckworth 2007)
- Angela Duckworth (University of Pennsylvania), "Grit: Perseverance and Passion for Long-Term Goals," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2007. Defined grit as perseverance + passion for long-term goals; built the 12-item Grit Scale (Grit-O) and later the 8-item Grit-S (Duckworth & Quinn, 2009).
- **West Point "Beast Barracks"**: across cohorts totaling 2,500+ cadets, grit was the single best predictor of surviving the brutal 6.5-week summer initiation — beating the academy's own Whole Candidate Score (SAT, class rank, leadership, physical fitness). Grittier cadets were roughly 60% more likely to complete Beast Barracks than less gritty peers.
- **National Spelling Bee**: grittier kids advanced to later rounds — not because they were smarter spellers, but because grit predicted more hours of solitary deliberate practice, which in turn predicted advancement (Duckworth, Kirby, Tsukayama, Berstein & Ericsson, 2011).
- Duckworth's own data: grit explained on the order of **~4% of the variance** in success outcomes across her samples — she stated this herself in the 2007 paper. (Useful teaching point: even the researcher's own headline number is modest.)
- Duckworth won a MacArthur "genius" grant (2013); *Grit* the book appeared in 2016.

### The meta-analytic correction (Credé, Tynan & Harms 2017)
- Marcus Credé, Michael Tynan & Peter Harms, "Much Ado About Grit: A Meta-Analytic Synthesis," *JPSP*, 2017: **88 independent samples, 66,807 people, 584 effect sizes**.
- Findings: grit's correlation with academic performance is modest (ρ ≈ .18 overall; the perseverance-of-effort facet does better, ~.26). Grit correlates with conscientiousness at **ρ ≈ .84 — with correction, approaching unity (.80–.98 across studies)**. Credé's verdict: grit is largely a repackaging of conscientiousness — a "jangle fallacy" (two different labels, same construct; term from Kelley, 1927).
- The "consistency of interest" (passion) facet adds almost nothing predictive; the **perseverance facet remains significantly associated with performance even after controlling for conscientiousness** — so the useful core survives, the branding doesn't.
- Duckworth's response (NPR, 2016): she accepts grit is "a member of the conscientiousness family" but argues it has independent predictive power for stamina-type outcomes (retention, completion) — and indeed the meta-analysis found grit predicts *retention* better than *grades*.
- Follow-up: Schmidt et al. (2020, *Journal of Research in Personality*) — "Grit and conscientiousness: Another jangle fallacy" — replicated the near-redundancy.

**Honest chapter framing**: grit is real but small, mostly old wine (conscientiousness/persistence) in a new bottle; the perseverance component is the active ingredient. It is NOT a substitute for ability, opportunity, or good strategy.

Story material: Duckworth's origin story — her father repeatedly told her "you're no genius"; she later won the "genius grant" and opened her book with that irony. West Point's Beast Barracks (cadets dropping out in week one despite stellar entrance scores) is the vivid anchor scene.

## 2. Deliberate practice and the 10,000-hour myth

### The original research (Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer 1993)
- K. Anders Ericsson and colleagues studied violinists at the Berlin Academy of Music (published in *Psychological Review*, 1993). By age 20: future elite soloists had accumulated **~10,000 hours** of practice on average; "good" violinists ~8,000; future music teachers ~4,000.
- **Deliberate practice ≠ repetition.** Ericsson's requirements: (1) specific, well-defined improvement goals; (2) full concentration; (3) immediate, informative feedback (usually a coach); (4) working at the edge of current ability, not inside the comfort zone; (5) building refined "mental representations" of correct performance. Ericsson's book *Peak* (with Robert Pool, 2016) is the accessible source.

### What Gladwell got wrong (per Ericsson himself, Salon, 2016)
- Malcolm Gladwell coined "the 10,000-Hour Rule" in *Outliers* (2008). Ericsson's corrections:
  1. **10,000 was arbitrary** — the average at age 20; at 18 it was ~7,400 hours. Gladwell picked the round number.
  2. **It was an average, not a threshold** — half of the best violinists had NOT hit 10,000 hours by 20.
  3. **It's about deliberate practice, not hours** — the Beatles' Hamburg gigs (Gladwell's example) were performances, not deliberate practice.
  4. **No promise of universality** — the study never showed anyone can become elite with 10,000 hours.

### The meta-analytic correction (Macnamara)
- Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald, *Psychological Science*, 2014 (88 studies): deliberate practice explained **26% of performance variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education, <1% in professions**. Overall ~12%. Conclusion: "important, but not as important as has been argued."
- Macnamara, Moreau & Hambrick, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 2016 (52 sports datasets): practice explains **18% of variance in sports overall, but only ~1% among elite-level performers** — at the top, everyone has practiced enormously, so practice stops differentiating; genetics, starting age, coaching quality, and psychology take over.
- Ericsson's rebuttal (e.g., Frontiers in Psychology, 2019): most studies in the meta-analysis measured mere "practice hours," not deliberate practice as originally defined; loose definitions dilute the effect. The debate remains genuinely unresolved — but nobody credible now defends "10,000 hours makes anyone an expert."

### Stories
- **The Polgár sisters**: Hungarian educational psychologist László Polgár announced before his children were born that he would raise geniuses; home-schooled daughters Susan, Sofia, and Judit in chess for hours daily. Judit became a grandmaster at 15 years 4 months (1991), breaking Bobby Fischer's record, and remains the strongest female player in history. Cited as practice's triumph — but scholars note it's an uncontrolled n=3 family study (Howard, 2011, debunking analysis in *Cognitive Development*).
- **The Dan Plan**: photographer Dan McLaughlin quit his job in 2010 to log 10,000 hours of deliberate golf practice and reach the PGA Tour, with Ericsson advising. He reached a very respectable ~2.6 handicap but abandoned the project around hour ~6,000 (2015) after a chronic back injury — a living illustration that the "rule" is not a formula.
- Useful contrast: 10,000 hours of *driving* doesn't make you a Formula 1 driver — hours without designed difficulty plateau (Ericsson's "OK plateau," popularized by Joshua Foer in *Moonwalking with Einstein*, 2011).

## 3. Why intelligence is not enough

### Terman's "Termites" (Genetic Studies of Genius, started 1921)
- Lewis Terman (Stanford) tracked **1,528 California children with IQs ≥ ~140** (average ~151) across their lifetimes — the longest-running longitudinal study in psychology.
- The group did well on average (professors, doctors, lawyers, ~2,000+ scientific papers, patents) — but produced **no Nobel laureates**, while **two children Terman rejected for insufficient IQ — William Shockley and Luis Alvarez — later won Nobel Prizes in physics**. (Warne, *Intelligence*, 2020, shows statistically this is largely a base-rate artifact — but it remains the canonical illustration that IQ screening misses greatness.)
- **The A/C comparison (Melita Oden, 1968)**: comparing the **100 most successful ("A") vs. 100 least successful ("C") men** in the sample — success defined as using one's intellectual gifts professionally — average IQ barely differed. The A's differed in **persistence ("perseverance toward goals"), self-confidence, integration of goals, and early parental encouragement**. Oden: "High but not the highest intelligence, combined with the greatest degree of persistence, will achieve greater eminence than the highest degree of intelligence with somewhat less persistence."
- Terman's own conclusion: "intellect and achievement are far from perfectly correlated." Some Termites ended up as police officers, seamen, typists, pool cleaners.
- Caveat for honesty: Terman meddled in subjects' lives (letters of recommendation, interventions), and the sample was demographically narrow (mostly white, middle-class Californians).

### The threshold hypothesis — and its limits
- Original idea (Guilford, 1967): above ~IQ 120, more IQ adds little to creativity/achievement — other traits dominate.
- Support: Jauk, Benedek, Dunst & Neubauer (2013, *Intelligence*) used empirical breakpoint detection: a threshold around **IQ ~85–100 for basic creative ideation, ~120 for creative *potential*** — but **no threshold at all for real-world creative *achievement***, where personality (openness) mattered above the line.
- Against a hard threshold: the **Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth (SMPY; Lubinski & Benbow, Vanderbilt, tracking 5,000+ people since 1971)** finds that even *within the top 1%* of ability, the top 0.01% out-achieve the merely top 1% in patents, publications, income, and tenure — "more ability matters" with no ceiling.
- Synthesis for the chapter: IQ helps and keeps helping, but it functions as an *entry ticket*, not a differentiator among the already-able; within any elite pool, the variance in outcomes is driven by persistence, strategy, opportunity, and luck — exactly Terman's A/C finding.

## 4. Mental models top performers use

- **Inversion** — Charlie Munger, channeling mathematician Carl Jacobi ("Invert, always invert"): instead of asking "How do I succeed?", ask "What would guarantee failure?" and avoid it. Munger: "It is remarkable how much long-term advantage people like us have gotten by trying to be consistently not stupid, instead of trying to be very intelligent." (See also Peter Bevelin, *Seeking Wisdom*, 2007; Shane Parrish, *The Great Mental Models*.) Practical form: the "pre-mortem" (Gary Klein, HBR 2007) — imagine the project has already failed, then explain why; Klein reports prospective hindsight boosts ability to identify reasons for future outcomes (~30%, per Mitchell, Russo & Pennington, 1989).
- **Second-order thinking** — Howard Marks (*The Most Important Thing*, 2011): first-order thinking asks "what happens next?"; second-order asks "and then what?" Everyone can do first-order; edge lives in consequences-of-consequences.
- **Expected-value thinking / decoupling decisions from outcomes** — Annie Duke (*Thinking in Bets*, 2018) calls judging a decision by its result "resulting": a good decision can have a bad outcome and vice versa. High performers grade the process, not the single outcome.
- **Decision journals** — Michael Mauboussin says the single best advice he got from Daniel Kahneman: whenever you make a consequential decision, write down what you decided, why, what you expect to happen, and how you feel — in real time. It "immunizes yourself against hindsight bias" and reveals when you were right for the wrong reasons (Farnam Street interviews with Mauboussin).
- **Systems over goals** — Scott Adams (*How to Fail at Almost Everything and Still Win Big*, 2013): "Goal-oriented people exist in a state of continuous pre-success failure... systems people succeed every time they apply their system." James Clear (*Atomic Habits*, 2018): "You do not rise to the level of your goals. You fall to the level of your systems." Winners and losers often have the *same* goals — the goal can't be what differentiates them.

## 5. Performing under pressure vs. choking — Sian Beilock

- Sian Beilock (cognitive scientist, University of Chicago; now president of Dartmouth; book *Choke*, 2010). Definition: "Choking isn't just poor performance. It is worse performance than you are capable of, precisely because there is a lot on the line."
- Two mechanisms (Beilock & Carr, *JEP: General*, 2001):
  1. **Explicit monitoring / paralysis by analysis** — for well-practiced sensorimotor skills (golf putting, free throws), pressure makes experts consciously monitor movements that run best on autopilot, dismantling proceduralized skill. Experimental evidence: expert golfers putt *worse* when told to attend to their swing mechanics; giving them a distracting secondary task (counting tones) *prevents* choking — the opposite pattern from novices.
  2. **Distraction / working-memory capture** — for cognitive tasks (math, exams), pressure-fueled worries consume working memory. Ironically it's the *highest*-working-memory people who choke hardest, because their usual strategies depend on the resource that anxiety steals (Beilock & Carr, 2005).
- **Antidotes with evidence**:
  - Practice under mild pressure ("pressure training"): golfers who practiced with an audience or small stakes choked less later.
  - Speed up pre-shot routines slightly; focus on a holistic cue word or an external target rather than mechanics.
  - **Expressive writing**: Ramirez & Beilock, *Science*, 2011 — ninth-graders who wrote for 10 minutes about their worries immediately before a final biology exam (two cohorts, n=51 and n=55) eliminated the anxiety–performance gap; anxious writers performed on par with calm students (roughly B+ vs. B− for anxious controls). Mechanism: offloading worries frees working memory. **Replication caveat**: the Camerer et al. many-labs style replication effort (2018) and later classroom studies (e.g., 2021, PMC8192598) failed to find the benefit — record the original AND the doubt.
- Vivid stories: Jana Novotná's 1993 Wimbledon final collapse against Steffi Graf (serving at 4–1, 40–30 in the third set, then double-faulting her way to defeat, weeping on the Duchess of Kent's shoulder — and winning Wimbledon five years later); Greg Norman's 1996 Masters six-shot final-round collapse; Beilock's own origin story as a soccer goalkeeper choking in a big showcase game.

## 6. Stress reappraisal — threat vs. challenge

### Biopsychosocial model (Blascovich & Mendes)
- **Challenge state**: appraised resources ≥ demands → heart pumps more blood, blood vessels *dilate* (higher cardiac output, lower peripheral resistance) — physiology resembling aerobic exercise; better performance.
- **Threat state**: demands > resources → vessels *constrict*, blood pressure rises inefficiently; impaired decision-making. Same racing heart, different vascular signature — the interpretation is the fork in the road.

### Jamieson's arousal-reappraisal studies
- Jamieson, Mendes, Blackstock & Schmader (*Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 2010): GRE practice test in the lab; half were told "arousal helps performance — if you feel anxious, remind yourself your arousal could be helping you." Reappraisal group showed **higher salivary alpha-amylase** (they weren't calmer — they used the arousal) and **outperformed controls on the quantitative section** — and on the **real GRE taken 1–3 months later** (reappraisal group ~65 points higher on quant in the original report).
- Jamieson, Peters, Greenwood & Altose (*Social Psychological and Personality Science*, 2016): reappraisal instructions raised math exam performance and lowered test anxiety in community-college classrooms — a cheap, scalable field intervention.
- Jamieson et al. also showed reappraisal shifts cardiovascular response from threat pattern toward challenge pattern (2012, *JEP: General*).

### Crum's stress mindsets
- Alia Crum, Peter Salovey & Shawn Achor, "Rethinking Stress" (*JPSP*, 2013): **388 UBS employees during the post-2008 layoffs**. Randomized to watch three ~3-minute videos: stress-is-enhancing (n=163), stress-is-debilitating (n=164), or no video (n=61). The stress-is-enhancing group reported **better work performance and fewer health symptoms** two weeks later; dispositional stress-is-enhancing mindset predicted higher life satisfaction and lower anxiety/depression.
- Crum's Stress Mindset Measure; her framing: the goal isn't to reduce stress but to change what you believe stress does. Related large-scale correlational anchor: Keller et al. (2012, *Health Psychology*) — in a US sample of ~29,000, high stress predicted higher mortality **only among people who believed stress was harmful**; heavy stress + no such belief showed no elevated risk (correlational, but memorable).
- Honest note: mindset-intervention effect sizes in this literature are small-to-moderate and context-dependent; treat as promising, not miraculous (cf. broader mindset debates around Dweck — large meta-analyses like Sisk et al., 2018, found small average effects for growth-mindset interventions, d ≈ .08, larger for at-risk students).

## 7. Failure as data — intelligent failure

- Term coined by **Sim Sitkin (1992)**, "Learning Through Failure: The Strategy of Small Losses."
- **Amy Edmondson** (Harvard Business School; *Right Kind of Wrong: The Science of Failing Well*, 2023 — Financial Times Business Book of the Year). Three failure types:
  1. **Basic failures** — preventable slips in known territory (blameworthy-ish; reduce them).
  2. **Complex failures** — multi-cause system breakdowns (learn and buffer).
  3. **Intelligent failures** — the "right kind of wrong": undesired but informative.
- **Four criteria for an intelligent failure**: (1) occurs in genuinely *new territory* (no playbook exists); (2) pursues a *meaningful opportunity*, not a lark; (3) is *hypothesis-driven* — informed by the best available knowledge; (4) is *as small as possible* while still informative.
- Edmondson's origin finding (1990s hospital study): the *better* nursing teams appeared to make *more* medication errors — actually, they *reported* more because they felt safe; this birthed her psychological-safety research program (Edmondson, 1999, "Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams").
- High-performer behavior: treat outcomes as information about the process (links to decision journals and Duke's "resulting" above); separate the *person* from the *error*; run cheap experiments at the frontier.
- Stories: **James Dyson's 5,127 prototypes** (5,126 "failures") over ~15 years before the working cyclonic vacuum; **Eli Lilly's "failure parties"** (from the 1990s) honoring scientifically excellent trials that failed; Thomas Edison: "I have not failed. I've just found 10,000 ways that won't work"; SpaceX's early Falcon 1 failures (three consecutive launch failures 2006–2008 before success on flight 4) as institutionalized intelligent failure.

## 8. Routines and energy management of elite performers

- Back to Ericsson's Berlin violinists (1993): the best practiced **~3.5 hours/day split into two-to-three sessions of ≤90 minutes with breaks**, mostly in the morning; middling performers practiced ~1.4 hours/day without structured rest. Top two groups slept **8.6 hours/day vs. 7.8** for the least skilled group, and napped nearly **3 hours/week vs. <1 hour**. Punchline: elites don't just work more — they *rest more, deliberately*.
- **Loehr & Schwartz, "The Making of a Corporate Athlete" (Harvard Business Review, 2001; book *The Power of Full Engagement*, 2003)**: manage *energy*, not time; performance rides on rhythmic **oscillation** between stress and recovery ("rituals"), across four levels — physical, emotional, mental, spiritual. Drawn from Loehr's work with tennis pros: top players' between-point rituals (16–20 seconds of walking, breathing, ritualized routines) dropped their heart rates and differentiated them from journeymen with equal strokes.
- Ultradian rhythm evidence: ~90-minute work blocks map to basic rest-activity cycles (Kleitman); supports the "sprint and recover" architecture.
- Anecdote bank: Roger Federer's reported ~11–12 hours of sleep per day; LeBron James's reported seven-figure annual spend on body maintenance and 8–10 hours of sleep; writer anecdotes (Darwin's three focused 90-minute work blocks per day; Stephen King's fixed 2,000-words-every-morning ritual) — high output from protected, repeatable routines rather than heroic marathons.

## 9. Process goals vs. outcome goals

- Definitions (sport psychology, Hardy et al.): **outcome goals** = results relative to others (win the race); **performance goals** = self-referenced standards (run 3:59); **process goals** = execution behaviors fully under your control (hold cadence, breathe on stroke three).
- **Williamson, Swann et al., systematic review & meta-analysis of goal setting in sport (*International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology*, 2022)**: process goals showed the largest performance effect (**d = 1.36**) vs. performance goals (**d = 0.44**) and outcome goals (**d = 0.09**, essentially nil); process goals also boosted self-efficacy (d ≈ 1.11) and reduced anxiety.
- Damon Burton (1989, competitive swimmers): goal *type*, not just difficulty, drives performance and anxiety; performance goals raised self-confidence vs. no goals (d = 1.35 males, 0.78 females).
- Backdrop: Locke & Latham's goal-setting theory (2002 synthesis of 35 years, 400+ studies): specific, difficult goals beat "do your best" — but in complex skill acquisition, outcome pressure can backfire, which is where process goals win.
- Bridges to earlier sections: process goals are the goal-setting version of "systems over goals," and they reduce choking because they direct attention to controllable execution instead of stakes.
- Story: British Cycling under Dave Brailsford — "marginal gains" as pure process orientation (aggregating 1% improvements in sleep, hygiene, equipment), from zero Tour de France wins in a century to dominating 2012–2017 (also a caution: the story is partly mythologized and the team later faced medical-ethics controversy — use with a light touch).

## 10. Replication-honesty ledger (for the chapter's credibility box)

| Claim | Original | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| Grit predicts success | Duckworth 2007 | Real but modest (ρ ≈ .18); ~redundant with conscientiousness (Credé 2017, 88 samples / 66,807 people); perseverance facet is the active ingredient |
| 10,000-hour rule | Gladwell 2008 (from Ericsson 1993) | Number arbitrary/average; deliberate practice explains 26%→<1% of variance by domain (Macnamara 2014); only ~1% among elites (Macnamara 2016); Ericsson disputes measurement |
| IQ = destiny | Terman 1921– | High IQ helps but A/C groups differed in persistence, not IQ (Oden 1968); SMPY shows no ability ceiling, so "threshold" is only partly right |
| Expressive writing before exams | Ramirez & Beilock, *Science* 2011 | Failed in Camerer et al. 2018 replication project and some classroom replications — flag as uncertain |
| Stress mindset videos | Crum 2013 (n=388) | Promising; effect sizes small-moderate; growth-mindset cousin literature shows small average effects (Sisk 2018, d ≈ .08) |
| Marshmallow test / ego depletion (adjacent chapters) | Mischel 1972; Baumeister 1998 | Watts et al. 2018: delay-of-gratification effect shrinks ~2/3 with controls; ego depletion failed multi-lab replications (Hagger 2016) — mention as pattern context |
