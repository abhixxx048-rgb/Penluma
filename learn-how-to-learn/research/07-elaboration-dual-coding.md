## Key concepts (plain definitions)

- **Elaboration / elaborative interrogation:** Deepening a memory by asking *why* and *how* a fact is true and tying it to what you already know. The classic prompt is "Why does this make sense?" or "Why would this be true?" — you generate the explanation for a stated fact yourself.
- **Self-explanation (Chi):** Explaining new material to yourself as you study — how a step follows, how it connects to prior knowledge, what a worked example is *doing* — rather than passively reading.
- **Dual coding (Paivio):** Information can be encoded in two systems, verbal and visual (imagery). Combining words with a relevant picture/diagram lays down two retrieval routes, so recall improves. Related: the **picture superiority effect** (pictures remembered better than words) and **concreteness effect** (concrete, imageable words beat abstract ones).
- **Concrete examples & analogies:** Grounding an abstract idea in a specific, familiar instance; using *multiple* varied examples so learners abstract the shared underlying structure and can transfer it.
- **Generation effect:** Producing an answer yourself beats reading the same answer.
- **Levels of processing (Craik & Lockhart):** Deep semantic processing (meaning) produces stronger memory than shallow processing (looks, sound).
- **Mnemonics:** Deliberate encoding tricks — method of loci (memory palace), acronyms, keyword method — that impose structure/imagery on arbitrary material.

## Studies & evidence (author, year, finding, numbers)

- **Slamecka & Graf (1978)** coined the *generation effect* across five experiments: subjects who generated a word from a rule + cue (e.g., synonym, "rapid–f__" → "fast") recalled and recognized far better than those who just read "rapid–fast." Robust across free/cued recall, recognition, and confidence. A later meta-analysis (Bertsch et al., 2007; 86 studies) found an average effect size of **d ≈ 0.40**.
- **Craik & Lockhart (1972)** proposed levels of processing; **Craik & Tulving (1975)** showed the mechanism: judging a word's *meaning* (semantic) yielded far higher recognition than judging its case/typeface (visual) or rhyme (phonetic) — often roughly double the deep-vs-shallow recall.
- **Paivio & Csapo (1973):** people recalled more than twice as many pictures as words. Tellingly, instructing people to *image* the referent of each word erased the picture advantage — supporting dual coding.
- **Chi, Bassok, Lewis, Reimann & Glaser (1989):** "Good" physics students spontaneously generated far more self-explanations while studying worked examples (≈52 vs 18 idea statements; ≈13 vs 7 minutes) and tied them to physics principles. **Chi et al. (1994):** prompting self-explanation of a biology text caused larger learning gains than not prompting.
- **Gick & Holyoak (1983):** Studying *two* analogous stories (vs one) before the "radiation/tumor" problem let learners induce an abstract "convergence" schema and transfer it — foundational evidence that multiple examples plus comparison drive transfer.
- **Dresler et al. (2017, *Neuron*):** 40 days of method-of-loci training took memory-naïve adults from ~26 to ~62 words recalled (of 72); memory athletes hit ~71/72, and trainees' brain connectivity shifted toward the athletes' pattern, lasting 4 months.
- **Atkinson & Raugh (1975):** the keyword method produced dramatic immediate gains for foreign vocabulary (in one study ~88% vs 28% control).
- **Dunlosky et al. (2013)** rated elaborative interrogation and self-explanation as **"moderate utility,"** below practice testing and distributed practice ("high").

## Myths / contested points (with honest nuance)

- **"Learning styles" (visual/auditory learners) is a myth** — but dual coding is *not* the same claim. Dual coding says visuals help *everyone* by adding a second code; it does not say match modality to a learner "type."
- **Dual coding's mechanism is contested.** Ensor, Surprenant et al. (2019) and Higdon et al. (2025) argue **distinctiveness**, not a second code, best explains picture superiority; picture superiority even survives in aphantasia (no mental imagery). The *practical* advice (pair words with relevant visuals) holds; the theory is debated.
- **Elaborative interrogation / self-explanation are "moderate," not magic:** they need prior knowledge to hook onto, are mostly tested on lab/short retention, and haven't been well validated in real classrooms (Dunlosky).
- **Mnemonics have real limits:** superb for arbitrary lists, but keyword-method gains often *fade faster* over long delays than plain study, and mnemonics aid recall of facts, not deep *understanding* or transfer.
- **Concrete vs abstract examples:** Kaminski, Sloutsky & Heckler (2008) found overly concrete/"perceptually rich" examples can *hurt* transfer because learners fixate on surface detail. Nuance: use concrete examples to build intuition, then fade toward the abstract structure and vary the surface.

## Real-world examples & analogies

- **Two memory routes analogy:** a fact stored as words + a picture is like saving a file in two folders — if one path is blocked at recall, the other still finds it.
- **Elaborative interrogation in action:** learning "arteries have thick walls" — instead of rereading, ask *why?* → "because blood leaves the heart under high pressure." Now it's reasoned, not memorized.
- **Memory palace:** the *Moonwalking with Einstein* / "USA Memory Championship" story — ordinary people memorize card decks by mentally walking a familiar house and placing vivid images at each spot.
- **Generation effect analogy:** trying (and even failing) to recall an answer before seeing it is like a muscle contracting — the effort itself strengthens the memory.

## Practical how-to points

- Turn notes into *why/how* questions; answer from prior knowledge before checking.
- Cover the answer and **generate** it first (flashcards, blank-page recall) rather than rereading.
- Redraw the concept as a labeled diagram or sketch; put the visual *next* to the words (dual coding), not on a separate page.
- Explain each step of a worked example aloud, including how it links to what you already know.
- Give an abstract idea **two or three different concrete examples**, then ask what they share.
- Reserve mnemonics (loci, acronyms, keyword) for arbitrary lists/vocabulary; pair keyword mnemonics with pictures and later retrieval to fight decay.

## Common mistakes learners make on this topic

- Treating dual coding as decoration — adding irrelevant/"cute" images that split attention instead of *relevant* diagrams.
- Rereading and highlighting (shallow processing) and mistaking familiarity for learning.
- Asking "why" but immediately reading the answer instead of generating it first (losing the generation effect).
- Studying only **one** example, then failing to transfer to new problems.
- Over-relying on mnemonics for material that needs understanding, and expecting keyword-method gains to last without review.
- Making examples so vivid/concrete that surface details block the abstract principle.
