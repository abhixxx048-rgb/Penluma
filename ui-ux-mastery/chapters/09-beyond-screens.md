# Chapter 09: UI/UX Beyond Screens: Presentations, Documents, Marketing, Video, Packaging

## Why this chapter matters

Everything you have learned so far in this book was about screens: apps, websites, buttons, forms. But here is a secret that separates good designers from great ones. The rules you learned do not live inside a screen. They live inside the human brain.

Your eyes and your working memory do not know or care whether you are looking at a phone, a slide on a wall, a report on paper, a box on a shelf, or a sign in an airport. The same perceptual machinery is running in every case. So the same five design principles apply everywhere.

In this chapter we will take five ideas you already know and prove they work far outside software:

- **Hierarchy** — decide what the viewer sees first, second, and third.
- **Contrast** — make the important thing stand out from everything else.
- **Cognitive load** — never ask the brain to hold more than it can.
- **Feedback** — confirm the person's action so they know it worked.
- **Consistency** — repeat patterns so the brain learns once and reuses.

A quick definition before we begin. A **"user"** is not only someone tapping a screen. A user is **anyone whose attention and understanding you are trying to guide**. The person in the back row of your presentation is a user. The shopper glancing at a shelf is a user. The reader skimming your report is a user. Once you see it this way, everything becomes an interface.

Let us prove it, medium by medium.

---

## The one big idea: every artifact is a communication interface

Before we go section by section, hold on to one sentence: **the medium changes, but the human perceptual system does not.**

Here is why that is true, in plain terms. Your brain has a special region called the **fusiform face area** (a patch of the brain that recognizes faces faster than almost anything else). It fires whether the face is on a video thumbnail or a cereal box. Your **working memory** (the small mental "desk" that holds what you are thinking about right now) holds only a few items at a time, whether those items are slide bullets or shelf products. The **picture superiority effect** (the fact that people remember images better than words) is true on a poster and in a slide deck alike.

There is also a family of rules called the **Gestalt principles** (German for "shape" or "whole"). These describe how the brain automatically groups things it sees: things that are close together seem related (proximity), things that look alike seem related (similarity), and the brain separates a "figure" from its "background" (figure-ground). Designers use these grouping rules to build hierarchy. And crucially, as design firms Eleken and Toptal both put it, "the perceptual grouping rules don't change with the medium. A poster and a mobile app both rely on the same underlying visual perception principles."

That is the thread running through every section below.

**In short:** Design is guiding a human eye and brain with the least friction — and the brain works the same no matter what you put in front of it.

## 1. Presentations and slides

### Slides are a "glance medium"

Think about a billboard on a highway. You have maybe two or three seconds to read it while driving past at speed. If it needs study, it has failed.

Presentation guru Garr Reynolds says a slide is exactly like this: "Presentations are a 'glance media' — more closely related to billboards than other media." So design every slide for **three-second comprehension**. If your audience has to read and decode a slide, they stop listening to you. This is the billboard principle, and it is really just *hierarchy plus contrast under a time limit*.

### One idea per slide

Nancy Duarte wrote the classic book *slide:ology* (2008). Her single most repeated rule is simple: **one idea per slide**.

The traps she warns against are the traps everyone falls into:

- Overusing bullet points.
- Cramming multiple ideas onto one slide.
- Using too many colors, shapes, and fonts.

Duarte's mindset shift is powerful: slide-making is **design, not decoration**. Decoration adds stuff to look pretty. Design removes stuff to communicate. She estimates a serious presentation deck deserves **36 to 90 hours** of work. (Her firm built the deck behind Al Gore's film *An Inconvenient Truth*, so she knows what serious effort looks like.) The point is not that you must spend 90 hours. The point is that great slides are crafted, not decorated.

This is nothing but **cognitive load management**. One idea per slide means the audience's working memory only ever holds one thing at a time.

### Assertion-Evidence: the research-backed structure

Here is the most important idea in this whole section, because it is backed by hard data.

In the 1980s, Michael Alley at Penn State studied how people learn and built a slide structure called **Assertion-Evidence**. Each slide has two parts:

- **Assertion** — a full-sentence *headline* that states the slide's single main message, no more than about two lines. NOT a topic phrase like "Results." A real claim like "Sales grew fastest in regions we ignored."
- **Evidence** — visual support: a photo, chart, or diagram. NOT a list of bullet points.

```
  TRADITIONAL SLIDE            ASSERTION-EVIDENCE SLIDE
  +------------------+         +--------------------------+
  | Results          |         | Cases fell 17% after     |
  |  - point one     |         | the new policy launched  |
  |  - point two     |   vs.   |                          |
  |  - point three   |         |     [ clear chart ]      |
  |  - point four    |         |                          |
  +------------------+         +--------------------------+
   topic phrase + text          sentence claim + one image
```

Now the study result. Researchers showed two audiences the *same spoken words*. One group saw traditional bulleted slides; the other saw assertion-evidence slides. The assertion-evidence audience **understood and remembered the technical content significantly better** — a difference that was **statistically significant at p < .01** (published by Alley, Garner, Neeley and colleagues in *Technical Communication* and the *International Journal of Engineering Education*). "Statistically significant at p < .01" means, in plain English, there is less than a 1% chance the improvement was random luck.

This is the empirical backbone of the whole chapter: **a claim plus a picture beats a bullet list.** It is the picture superiority effect proven in a meeting room.

### Garr Reynolds and *Presentation Zen*

Reynolds adds a toolkit that maps perfectly onto our five principles:

- **Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)** — the ratio of useful information to clutter. His rule: "Every element should be expressed to the extent necessary, but not beyond." If removing something does not change the meaning, remove it. Kill footers, logos, clip-art; thin out heavy table gridlines; delete chart junk. This is pure contrast: less noise makes the signal louder.
- **White space** — the empty areas of a slide. Do not fill them. Empty space increases impact and clarity. Emptiness is a design choice, not wasted room.
- **Contrast** — create *one* clearly dominant element. Avoid designs where everything has equal weight, because then nothing leads the eye.
- **Picture Superiority Effect** — use full-bleed images (images that run to the very edges), and make one big image rather than many small ones. Especially after 30 seconds, people recall pictures far better than words.
- **Charts** — Restrain, Reduce, Emphasize. Replace generic chart titles with **declarative statements**: "Cases decreased 17%" instead of "Reported cases 2021." Same idea as an assertion headline.
- **Animation** — use no more than two or three transition types across the whole deck. Do not animate bullets flying in on every slide.
- **Segmenting** — if a slide is dense, break it into two or three simpler slides.

### Rules of thumb (they are heuristics, not laws)

- **Guy Kawasaki's 10-20-30 rule** — 10 slides, 20 minutes, no font smaller than 30pt. His reasoning: "a normal human being cannot comprehend more than 10 concepts in a meeting" (cognitive load), and a 30pt floor *forces* you to use less text because big words leave no room to ramble.
- **The 6x6 rule** — at most 6 bullets per slide, at most 6 words per bullet. A simple load-limiter. (Assertion-Evidence fans would go further and cut bullets entirely.)
- **Slidedocs vs slides** — Duarte's distinction. If a deck must be read alone, with no speaker, build a proper **slidedoc** (a visual document meant to be read). Do not make a "slideument," a mush that fails as both a slide and a document.

**In short:** A slide is a billboard for one idea — a sentence claim, one image, and nothing you could delete without losing meaning.

## 2. Documents and reports

### People scan, they do not read

Here is a fact that should change how you write everything. In a landmark **1997 study**, the Nielsen Norman Group (led by Jakob Nielsen) found that **79% of users scan** a new page. Only 16% read word by word. The **average page visit lasts under a minute**, and people read only about **20 to 28% of the words** on a page.

So if you write a report as a wall of paragraphs, four out of five readers will never read it as written. They will hunt for the parts that matter. Your job is to make those parts easy to find.

### The F-pattern

NN/g used eye-tracking (cameras that record exactly where a person looks) and discovered the **F-shaped reading pattern**. On a text-heavy page, eyes read the top line across, drop down and read a shorter line across, then scan straight down the left edge — tracing the shape of the letter F.

```
  ================>     (full sweep across the top)
  ==========>          (shorter sweep lower)
  ||
  ||                   (eyes run down the left edge)
  ||
```

The lesson: **front-load meaning.** Put your keywords first, at the start of headings and first sentences and along the left side. (For sparse, visual layouts there is a related **Z-pattern**, where the eye zig-zags corner to corner.) Practically: meaningful subheads, real bulleted lists, and **bold** key phrases so a scanner's eye catches them.

### Barbara Minto and the Pyramid Principle

Barbara Minto was Harvard Business School's **first female MBA (1963)** and McKinsey's first female consultant. She codified a way of structuring ideas called the **Pyramid Principle** (her firm was founded in 1973).

The core idea is often called **BLUF — Bottom Line Up Front.** State your answer first, then support it. This is the opposite of how school taught you to write (build up slowly to a conclusion at the end). Busy readers want the conclusion immediately.

Structure your thinking as a pyramid under one governing thought:

```
              [ Main answer / recommendation ]
             /            |            \
      [ Argument 1 ] [ Argument 2 ] [ Argument 3 ]
        |    |          |    |          |    |
      data data       data data       data data
```

- **Answer or recommendation at the very top, first.**
- Supported by **2 to 4 grouped arguments**, which should be **MECE** (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive — meaning the groups do not overlap, and together they cover everything).
- Detailed evidence and data at the base.

To introduce the pyramid, Minto uses a four-part story frame called **SCQA**:

- **Situation** — facts the reader already accepts. No objection here; you are setting the baseline.
- **Complication** — what changed, or the problem, or the tension.
- **Question** — the question that naturally arises from that tension.
- **Answer** — your recommendation, which is the top of the pyramid.

Notice this is just **visual hierarchy applied to ideas.** The conclusion is the dominant element, placed first; supporting detail is subordinated below. A well-structured document has the same shape as a well-designed slide.

### The report scannability toolkit

- **Executive summary** — the report's "above the fold." The whole argument in one paragraph for readers who read nothing else.
- **Descriptive headings and subheads** every few paragraphs — waypoints for F-pattern scanners.
- **Tables** for comparison data. Keep gridlines light (SNR again), align numbers to the right so digits line up, and put one idea per column.
- **Bulleted and numbered lists**, **bold** keywords, short paragraphs, and generous margins and white space.
- **Chunking** — break long text into labeled sections. (This is the segmenting principle from slides, wearing a document's clothes.)

**In short:** Because four in five readers scan, write for the scanner — answer first, headings as signposts, keywords front-loaded, and the whole point in the executive summary.

## 3. Marketing creatives and social media

### The one-second thumb-stop

Picture someone scrolling a feed with their thumb. Your post gets about **one second** to stop that thumb. That is it.

This is the exact same challenge as the billboard slide and (as we will see) the package on a shelf. You win a glance with two things only: **instant figure-ground contrast** and **one dominant focal point**. Everything else is decided later, if you even earn a "later."

### Hook, Value, CTA

Strong posts follow a three-part structure: **Hook → Value → CTA.**

- **Hook** — the first line or first frame. A pattern interrupt (something unexpected that breaks the scroll), a curiosity gap, a bold claim, or an emotional face. Its only job is to earn the next two seconds.
- **Value** — the payoff. The substance that justifies the attention you just grabbed.
- **CTA** — Call To Action. One clear next step: follow, save, click, or buy. **One** primary CTA, exactly like a slide's single dominant element or an email's single button.

### Platform image sizes (2025 to 2026)

This part is not glamorous, but get it wrong and the platform's auto-crop will chop the head off your subject and destroy your hierarchy. Design to the right size:

| Platform / format | Aspect ratio | Recommended pixels |
|---|---|---|
| Instagram feed (square) | 1:1 | 1080 x 1080 |
| Instagram feed (portrait) | 4:5 | 1080 x 1350 |
| Instagram tall grid (Jan 2025) | 3:4 | 1080 x 1440 |
| Stories / Reels / TikTok | 9:16 | 1080 x 1920 |

Two things to remember. First, as of **January 2025** Instagram moved to a **3:4 "tall grid,"** so vertical uploads (1080 x 1350 or 1080 x 1440) are safest. Second, the broad trend is that **vertical, mobile-first ratios (4:5 and 9:16) now outperform square** on most networks, simply because they fill more of the screen. Always design at **1080px wide minimum**. (For a full per-network table, the Buffer, Hootsuite, and Sprout Social cheat-sheets stay current.)

### The principles carry straight over

- **Hierarchy** — one dominant message per creative.
- **Contrast** — thumb-stopping power against a busy feed.
- **Cognitive load** — one idea per creative, never a cluttered collage.
- **Consistency** — a brand template or kit so a follower recognizes you mid-scroll before they even read your name.

And text on a creative must be legible at **mobile thumbnail size**. This is the same "back of the room" legibility test Reynolds applies to slides — just shrunk to a phone.

**In short:** You get one second and one focal point — hook the thumb with contrast, deliver value, ask for one action, and size it so the crop does not betray you.

## 4. Video and thumbnails (YouTube)

### The thumbnail is a UX decision

A YouTube thumbnail is a tiny interface with one job: earn the click. Years of A/B testing (showing different versions to different viewers to see which wins) give us surprisingly precise numbers.

- **Faces and emotion.** Thumbnails with human faces outperform object-only thumbnails by roughly **25 to 30%**. Why? Your **fusiform face area** processes faces faster than almost any other input. Add a clear emotion and click-through rate (CTR) rises **up to 30%** (VidIQ data). The best-performing emotions are **surprise, excitement, and curiosity** — we mirror the emotion we see.
- **Contrast.** A 2023 Vidooly study found high-contrast, contrasting-color thumbnails get about **30% higher CTR**. That is figure-ground separation again, deciding money.
- **Text — the 3-element rule.** Add **1 to 3 words** at most. Past about four words, CTR *drops*, because the text becomes unreadable at mobile size. Keep the whole thumbnail to roughly **three visual elements** — for example, a face, an object, and one to three words — so it reads in a single glance.
- **Curiosity gap.** The thumbnail should open an information gap that only the video can close. That itch is the psychological trigger of the click.
- **Before / after.** For how-to and educational content, "before then after" thumbnails get about **35% higher CTR** than showing only the finished result, because they promise a transformation.

```
  3-ELEMENT THUMBNAIL
  +-----------------------------+
  |  [FACE]      "I QUIT"        |   1) emotional face
  |  surprised    (2 words)      |   2) 1-3 words
  |            [ object ]         |   3) one object
  +-----------------------------+
```

### Retention, hooks, and pacing (2025 data)

Winning the click is half the battle. Now you must keep the viewer. **Retention** means the percentage of the video an average viewer watches.

- **The first 15 seconds decide everything.** Videos that state a clear value proposition in the first 15 seconds see about **18% higher retention at the one-minute mark.**
- A **steep drop in the first 15 to 30 seconds, then a flat line** is the signature of a failed hook — you lost most viewers before the substance began.
- **Below 50% retention** signals a structural hook or pacing problem. Holding **70% or more in the first 30 seconds** is strong and tends to trigger the algorithm to promote you.
- **Slow intros cost casual viewers.** With a slow intro, casual audiences drop about **60% in the first 30 seconds**, versus about **35%** for dedicated learners who came on purpose.
- **Match pacing to intent.** Entertainment audiences respond to varied pace and surprise; learners respond to timestamps and clear structure (which lifts completion by about 15%). In about 35% of analyzed videos, a pace or style that fought the audience's intent caused steep mid-video drop-offs.

Now watch the principles map on cleanly:

- The **thumbnail** is a hierarchy and contrast problem — win the glance.
- The **hook** is a feedback problem — instantly reward the click so the viewer's silent question ("was this worth it?") gets an immediate yes.
- **Pacing** is cognitive load management stretched over time — never overload, never bore.

**In short:** Faces, contrast, and three elements win the click; a value-packed first 15 seconds wins the watch — thumbnail is contrast, hook is feedback, pacing is load control.

## 5. Physical products and packaging

### Shelf impact is the retail thumb-stop

A package on a crowded shelf has about **one second** to win a shopper's glance. Sound familiar? It is the feed and the billboard again, now in a store.

Apple's answer is **radical minimalism**: no extra words, no bright colors, no busy graphics. Clean lines that mirror the clean device inside. Customers link that visual simplicity to **reliability and elegance**. And there is a deeper move here — the consistency between the package and the product builds trust. The box looks like the phone feels. When outside matches inside, the brain reads it as honest.

### The unboxing experience — Apple as the classic case

Steve Jobs and Jony Ive treated packaging as **"theater."** They designed a **"ritual of unpacking"** to make the product feel special, investing **thousands of hours** per box. (Apple reportedly keeps a dedicated packaging room.) Here is what they engineered, translated into our principles:

- **Designed slowness.** The iPhone box lid descends slowly, because of a precisely engineered air-cushion friction fit. That slowness is deliberate — it builds anticipation. It is a **pacing** choice, exactly like a video reveal.
- **Multi-sensory feedback (synesthesia).** Humans respond positively when several senses are engaged at once — weight, friction, sound, texture. The box *clicking* and *sliding* is **feedback**, confirming your action, just as a button animates when tapped.
- **Curiosity gap.** The box stages a reveal that closes the gap between "what I bought" and "what I now hold" — the same curiosity gap that drives a thumbnail click.
- **Endowment effect.** The endowment effect is our tendency to value something more once we own it. High-quality packaging raises the *perceived* value of what you now hold and feels worth keeping. That is also why millions of people post **unboxing videos** — the packaging becomes free marketing.
- **Materials as message.** Molded fiber and pulp trays hold the device securely and signal "premium yet responsible."

### Affordances and signifiers — the Norman door revisited

We met Don Norman earlier in this book. His masterpiece *The Design of Everyday Things* gives us two words worth locking in:

- **Affordance** — a possible action an object offers. A flat plate *affords* pushing. A vertical bar *affords* pulling or grabbing.
- **Signifier** — a design property that *announces* where and how to act: an arrow, a label, a handle shape. Norman coined "signifier" because designers kept misusing the word "affordance." The affordance is the possibility; the signifier is the signal.

Now the famous **Norman door.** A Norman door is a door whose signifiers contradict its mechanism — it has a pull handle but you must push it. The usual "fix" is to slap a **PUSH** sign on it.

Here is the punchline, and it applies to every design you will ever make: **the need for an instructional sign is itself proof of bad design.** As Norman says, "you shouldn't need a sign on a door." If the affordances and signifiers are right, the correct action is obvious without words. Every time you find yourself adding a "PUSH," a "swipe up," or a "read the manual," a design has failed.

Norman's other everyday-things principles — **natural mapping** (the layout of stove knobs matching the layout of burners), **immediate feedback**, and **conspicuity** (making the right control obvious) — apply to remotes, faucets, and appliances exactly as they apply to a UI.

### Wayfinding and signage

Signifiers, Norman notes, "communicate where the action should take place" — a doorway, a stop sign, an Uber sticker in a window. They can even be sounds: a phone ring, a notification ding, a bell.

Good **wayfinding** (helping people find their way through airports, hospitals, and transit systems) uses the identical toolkit:

- A clear **visual hierarchy** of signs — big for major decisions, small for details.
- High **figure-ground contrast** so signs pop from the wall.
- **Consistent** iconography and color-coding along the whole route.
- **Progressive disclosure** — revealing information step by step along the path so travelers are never overloaded at once.

When people get lost in a building, it is almost always a hierarchy, contrast, or consistency failure — the physical twin of a confusing navigation menu.

**In short:** A box, a door, and an airport are all interfaces — win the glance, reward the senses with feedback, make the right action obvious, and if you need a sign to explain it, redesign it.

## 6. Email design

Email is a constrained, mobile-first interface — think of it as a mini landing page living inside an inbox. Every rule you know applies, tightened by the small screen.

- **Single-column layout.** One column reinforces hierarchy and scannability and survives tiny screens. Multi-column emails feel cramped and break on mobile. This is the email version of "one idea per slide."
- **Visual hierarchy.** **One primary CTA** drives the main goal. Secondary actions are made visually smaller and quieter through size and color — the "one dominant element" rule again.
- **Preheader text.** The little preview snippet next to or under the subject line. Aim for about **30 to 55 characters** and use it to support the subject line. (Note: AI inbox summaries can now override your preview text, so front-load the core message where it counts.)
- **Tap targets.** Make buttons at least **44 x 44 pixels.** Fingers are far bigger than mouse cursors — this is Apple's Human Interface Guidelines minimum touch size. Place the primary CTA in **thumb-reach zones** (the middle and lower half of a phone screen) and keep the key message and CTA **near the top.**
- **White space and scannability.** Cut clutter; make it skimmable in the F-pattern.
- **Accessibility.** Enough contrast between text and background, real text instead of text baked into images, and a logical reading order — the same WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) concerns as any screen UI.

**In short:** An email is a one-column, one-CTA mini page — put the message up top, make buttons thumb-sized, and design it to be skimmed, not read.

## 7. The cross-media principle matrix

Here is the whole chapter on one page. Read it down each column to see one medium; read it across each row to watch a single principle change costume while staying the same idea.

| Principle | Slides | Documents | Social / Marketing | Video / Thumbnail | Packaging / Physical | Email |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hierarchy** | One assertion headline per slide | BLUF / Minto pyramid, exec summary | One dominant message | Face + 1-3 words; hook first | Logo / brand dominant on shelf | One primary CTA |
| **Contrast** | One dominant element; SNR | Bold keywords, light gridlines | Thumb-stop vs busy feed | High-contrast thumbnail (+30% CTR) | Minimalist figure-ground | Button color pops |
| **Cognitive load** | 6x6, 10-20-30, chunking | Scanning, chunked sections | One idea per creative | Pacing; 3-element rule | Simple, few elements | Single column |
| **Feedback** | Speaker reveals next point | Clear next-step signposting | CTA response | Hook rewards the click | Box clicks / reveals; unboxing | Confirmation, clear CTA |
| **Consistency** | Slide master, 2-3 fonts | Heading styles | Brand kit / template | Channel branding | Packaging family, brand-device match | Email template |

**In short:** Five principles, six media, one brain — learn the principles once and you can design in any medium.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the artifact as storage, not communication.** Dumping everything you know onto a slide, page, or box. *Fix:* decide the single most important message first, make it dominant, and subordinate or cut the rest.
- **No single focal point (equal-weight layouts).** When everything shouts, nothing is heard. *Fix:* pick one dominant element per view and use size, color, and space to prove it.
- **Low contrast, so the message drowns in noise.** *Fix:* raise figure-ground contrast — thin the gridlines, brighten the button, simplify the background until the signal is loud.
- **Too many fonts, colors, and ideas.** Cognitive overload. *Fix:* cap yourself — 2 to 3 fonts, one idea per unit, a fixed brand palette.
- **Illegibility at real viewing size.** Slide text nobody in the back can read; thumbnail text lost at phone size; shelf copy invisible from a step away. *Fix:* test at the real distance — back of the room, phone thumbnail, arm's length on a shelf.
- **Requiring an instruction to fix a design.** A "PUSH" sign, a "swipe up," a "read the manual." *Fix:* if you need words to explain the action, redesign the affordances and signifiers so the action is obvious.
- **Weak or slow hooks.** A video intro that meanders, an email subject with no pull, a slide that opens with "Agenda." *Fix:* lead with value in the first line, first frame, or first sentence.
- **Inconsistency that forces the brain to relearn.** Every slide, email, or post looking different. *Fix:* build and reuse a template, master slide, or brand kit.

## Best Practices Checklist

- [ ] I chose the ONE message before designing anything.
- [ ] There is a single dominant element in every view.
- [ ] Slides use a sentence assertion headline plus one visual, not bullets.
- [ ] Documents lead with the answer (BLUF) and open with an executive summary.
- [ ] Content is written for scanners: headings, bold keywords, short chunks, front-loaded meaning.
- [ ] Social creatives stop the thumb in one second and end with one clear CTA.
- [ ] Images are exported at the correct platform size (1080px+ wide; 4:5 or 9:16 for vertical).
- [ ] Thumbnails use a face, high contrast, and 1 to 3 words — three elements max.
- [ ] Videos deliver a clear value proposition in the first 15 seconds.
- [ ] Packaging and physical objects make the right action obvious without an instruction sign.
- [ ] Emails are single-column, one primary CTA, with 44x44px tap targets near the top.
- [ ] Fonts capped at 2 to 3; colors follow a consistent brand palette everywhere.
- [ ] I tested legibility at the real viewing size and distance.
- [ ] Nothing remains that I could delete without losing meaning (SNR check).

## Key Takeaways

- **The same five principles — hierarchy, contrast, cognitive load, feedback, consistency — govern every medium.** The screen changes; the human perceptual system does not.
- **Every artifact is a communication interface, and everyone whose attention you guide is a "user"** — the back-row listener, the shopper, the scanner, the scroller.
- **A claim plus a picture beats a bullet list, and it is proven.** Alley's Assertion-Evidence slides improved comprehension and recall at p < .01. Make the same move everywhere: declarative headlines and chart titles.
- **People scan, they do not read.** Nielsen's data: 79% scan, visits last under a minute, only about 20 to 28% of words get read. Design for the F-pattern and front-load meaning.
- **Answer first.** Minto's Pyramid Principle and BLUF put the conclusion at the top with 2 to 4 MECE supports below — visual hierarchy applied to ideas.
- **You get about one second to win a glance** on a feed, a shelf, or a billboard slide — won with instant contrast and one focal point.
- **Faces, contrast, and three elements win clicks; a strong first 15 seconds wins watch time.** Thumbnail is contrast, hook is feedback, pacing is load control.
- **If you need a sign to explain it, the design failed.** Norman's affordances and signifiers make the right action self-evident — in doors, packages, and interfaces alike.
- **Consistency is a gift to the brain:** build a system once (slide master, brand kit, email template) and the viewer learns it once and reuses it forever.
