# Chapter 3: The Forgetting Curve and the Spacing Effect

## Why this chapter matters

Picture this. It's the night before a big test. You sit down at 9 p.m. with a mountain of notes, a pot of coffee, and grim determination. You read, and re-read, and re-read. By 2 a.m. your brain feels stuffed full. You walk into the exam the next morning and — it works. The facts are right there. You pass.

Then, three weeks later, someone asks you one question from that same test. And it's gone. Not "a little fuzzy" — *gone*, as if you never studied at all. Where did it go?

This chapter is about that vanishing act. You're going to meet the **forgetting curve** — the well-documented pattern showing how fast newly learned information leaks out of your head — and its opposite, the **spacing effect**, one of the most reliably proven tricks for making memories last. By the end, you'll understand why cramming feels so effective yet fails you, and you'll have a concrete schedule you can start using this week.

Here's the good news up front: forgetting is not a personal flaw. It's a predictable, measurable curve. And once you know its shape, you can outsmart it.

---

## Section 1: The curve that started it all

Back in the 1880s, a German psychologist named **Hermann Ebbinghaus** did something slightly mad. He decided to study memory using himself as the only subject. To keep things "pure," he invented lists of **nonsense syllables** — meaningless three-letter combinations like *WID*, *ZOF*, *LAJ* — so that no prior knowledge could help him. Then he memorized list after list, and carefully measured how much he forgot over time.

His clever trick was the **savings method** — his way of measuring memory. Instead of asking "how many do you remember?", he *relearned* an old list and counted how much *faster* it went the second time. If learning a list the first time took 25 repetitions, and relearning it later took only 20, he had saved 5 repetitions — that's 5 out of 25, or **20% savings**. This is a beautifully sneaky measure, because it catches memory that's still lurking below the surface even when you can't consciously recall a thing.

What he found became famous. Memory doesn't fade at a steady, straight-line pace. It drops off a cliff at first, then levels out. In his 1885 self-experiments, his savings looked roughly like this: about **58% after 20 minutes, 44% after 1 hour, 34% after 1 day, 25% after 6 days, and 21% after 31 days**. The steepest loss happens in the very first hours.

Here's the shape drawn out:

```
Memory
retained
100% |*
     | *
     |  *
     |   *
     |    *
     |      *
     |        *
 50% |          * *
     |              * *  *
     |                    *  *  *  *
     |                              *  *  *  *  *
  0% +--------------------------------------------------->
     0   20min  1hr    1day     6days           31days
                     Time since learning
```

Notice how the line plunges early, then flattens into a long tail. That flattening matters — it means the stuff that survives the first day tends to stick around a good while longer.

One honest warning: those exact percentages are *illustrative*, not gospel. They came from one man memorizing gibberish. Meaningful material — a story, a concept you understand — decays much more slowly. Treat the **shape** of the curve as rock-solid, and the exact numbers as rough.

Does the curve still hold up over a century later? Yes. In 2015, researchers **Murre and Dros** carefully replicated Ebbinghaus's method and got the same shape: roughly 42% savings at 20 minutes, 34% at 1 hour, 31% at 1 day, 17% at 6 days, and 9% at 31 days. They even spotted a small upward "bump" near 24 hours — a hint that a night's sleep helps *lock in* memories, a process called **consolidation**.

**In short:** Ebbinghaus showed in 1885 — and Murre and Dros confirmed in 2015 — that forgetting is fast at first and then slow, a curved decline you can actually predict.

## Section 2: Why we forget (it's not just fading)

It's tempting to think of forgetting as a memory simply *evaporating* over time, like a puddle drying up. Psychologists call this idea **decay** — the notion that memory traces weaken with time if unused. Decay is real, but on its own it's a weak explanation. Two other culprits do a lot of the damage.

The first is **interference** — when other memories crowd out the one you want. There are two flavors. **Proactive interference** is when *old* learning blocks *new* — like your old password stubbornly popping up when you try to recall the new one. **Retroactive interference** is the reverse: *new* learning scrambles *old*, like starting Spanish lessons and suddenly finding your rusty French coming out all wrong.

The second culprit is **retrieval failure**, also called **cue-dependent forgetting**. Here the memory is safely stored — it hasn't decayed or been overwritten — but you can't *reach* it because the right trigger is missing. You know it's in there ("it's on the tip of my tongue!"). The psychologist **Endel Tulving** captured this with his **encoding specificity principle**: the cues that were present when you *learned* something need to be present again when you try to *recall* it. This is why walking into the room where you studied can make a "forgotten" fact snap right back.

Why does this matter for you? Because if forgetting were pure decay, there'd be nothing to do but relearn from scratch. But since so much forgetting is really *retrieval failure*, the fix is to **practice retrieving** — to keep the paths to your memories well-worn and easy to travel.

**In short:** We forget partly from decay, but mostly from interference and lost retrieval cues — which means much of what feels "gone" is actually still stored and reachable.

## Section 3: Storage strength vs. retrieval strength

Here's the single idea that explains the whole mystery of the disappearing exam knowledge. Two researchers, **Robert and Elizabeth Bjork**, proposed it in their 1992 **New Theory of Disuse**. They said every memory has *two* different strengths.

**Storage strength** is how *deeply* something is learned — how well it's built into the foundations of your mind. The remarkable claim is that storage strength **never decreases**. Once something is genuinely learned, it stays learned at some level, essentially forever.

**Retrieval strength** is how *accessible* a memory is *right now* — how easily it comes to mind today. Unlike storage strength, retrieval strength *fluctuates* and *fades*.

Think of it like a library. **Storage strength** is whether a book is actually *in* the building. **Retrieval strength** is whether you can *find* it — is it on the front desk, or buried in a dusty basement with no label?

Now the cramming mystery solves itself. When you cram the night before, you send **retrieval strength** shooting sky-high — the books are all piled on the front desk, easy to grab. That's why the exam feels easy. But cramming adds almost *nothing* to **storage strength** — the books never get properly shelved into the permanent collection. So within days, retrieval strength drains away, and there's little storage strength underneath to catch it. The knowledge feels like it vanished because, in a way, it was never truly filed.

This also explains a strange fact you'll meet later: **effortful, slightly difficult recall builds storage strength**, while easy, fluent review does not. Struggling a little to remember is the *signal* that deep learning is happening.

**In short:** Cramming spikes retrieval strength (accessible now) but barely touches storage strength (learned deeply) — which is exactly why crammed knowledge feels solid then evaporates.

## Section 4: The spacing effect — the honest fix

If cramming loads it all into one session, the fix is almost embarrassingly simple: *spread the same study time out*. Psychologists call this the **spacing effect** — the finding that you remember far more when study is broken into separated sessions over time. Studying it all in one block is called **massed practice** (cramming); spreading it out is **distributed practice**.

The evidence here is not thin. In 2006, **Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted, and Rohrer** published a landmark meta-analysis — a study that pools together many other studies — synthesizing **839 assessments across 317 experiments in 184 articles**. Spaced practice reliably beat massed practice. In fact, distributed practice is considered one of psychology's most replicated findings.

A garden analogy makes it click. Imagine watering a plant. If you dump a whole month's water on it in one go, most of it floods the pot and runs off the sides — wasted. But small amounts across many days soak gently into the soil. Your brain works the same way: it can only absorb so much per session, and the rest runs off.

Here's a side-by-side comparison:

| Feature | Massed practice (cramming) | Distributed practice (spacing) |
|---|---|---|
| **How** | One long block, all at once | Short sessions spread over days/weeks |
| **How it feels** | Smooth, fluent, "I've got this" | Effortful, a bit forgetful, harder |
| **Retrieval strength** | Spikes high, briefly | Rises steadily, stays up |
| **Storage strength** | Barely grows | Grows deeply |
| **Next-day test** | Often fine | Fine |
| **Test weeks later** | Mostly forgotten | Well retained |
| **Total time spent** | Same | Same |
| **Best for** | An exam tomorrow morning | Actually keeping the knowledge |

Read that last column carefully, because here's the fair, honest truth many people get wrong: **cramming is not useless.** For a test the very next morning, cramming genuinely works — that's *why* it feels so effective and why students keep doing it. It only fails when you need the material to *last*. The honest framing is that cramming trades long-term memory for short-term performance. If your goal is a grade tomorrow and you'll never need it again, cram away. If your goal is to actually *know* something, space it.

**In short:** Spacing the same study time across separate sessions produces dramatically better long-term memory — proven across 317 experiments — while cramming only wins for tests you take the very next day.

## Section 5: How far apart? Building your schedule

So spacing wins — but *how far apart* should the sessions be? This is where a second study gives us a genuinely useful rule.

In 2008, **Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted, and Pashler** had about **1,350 people** learn facts, review them after various gaps, and then get tested anywhere up to a *year* later. Their key finding: **the best gap is roughly 10–20% of how long you need to remember the material.**

Let that sink in, because it kills a popular myth. There is *no* single magic interval — not 24 hours, not any fixed number. The right gap is **relative to your goal**. Concretely, from their data:

- Need it for about **1 week**? Review after roughly **1 day**.
- Need it for about **70 days**? The best gap was around **21 days**.
- Need it for a **whole year**? The optimal gap actually *shrank* to about 5% — a few weeks between reviews.

The longer you need to hold the memory, the longer (in absolute terms) your ideal gaps become. This is closely related to the **lag effect** — the finding that among spaced schedules, *longer* gaps generally beat shorter ones for lasting retention. And it points back to the 2006 meta-analysis's central law: the ideal spacing grows as your target retention grows.

Now here's the diagram that ties the whole chapter together. Each time you review *just as you're starting to forget*, you "reset" your memory back toward the top — and each reset makes the curve fall more *slowly* the next time. The curve flattens:

```
Memory
retained
100% |R........        R.............        R..................  R
     |         `..    /              `..    /                  `.
     |            `../                   `../
     |            /  `.                  /
     |           /     `.               /
     |    review resets              review
     |    & flattens                 again
  0% +--R1------R2-----------R3-------------------R4------------->
        1 day   +3 days      +1 week             +3 weeks
                        Time  ->
     ('R' = a review;  each review lifts memory back up and
      the next decline is shallower than the one before)
```

See how each review not only lifts the line back up, but makes the *following* slope gentler? That's storage strength growing. After a few well-timed reviews, the material barely decays at all.

Here's a concrete **worked schedule** for a typical goal — say, remembering something for a test about a month out, then keeping it beyond:

| Review | When | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Learn it | Day 0 | First encounter; retrieval strength high but fragile |
| Review 1 | +1 day (Day 1) | Catch it before the steep first-day drop |
| Review 2 | +3 days (Day 4) | Gap widens as memory strengthens |
| Review 3 | +1 week (Day 11) | You'll have partly forgotten — that effort is the point |
| Review 4 | +3 weeks (Day 32) | Long gap now that it's well-rooted |

Notice the gaps get *bigger* each time: 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 3 weeks. That's called **expanding spacing**, and it mirrors how each review makes the memory more durable, so it can survive a longer wait before the next one.

One more crucial detail: what should you *do* during each review? Not passively reread. In their sweeping 2013 review of ten study techniques, **Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan, and Willingham** found that only **two** earned a top "high utility" rating: **distributed practice** (spacing) and **practice testing** (quizzing yourself). They work best *together*. So at each review, close the book and try to *recall* — that effortful retrieval is what builds the storage strength.

**In short:** Set your gaps to about 10–20% of how long you need the material — expanding gaps like 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 3 weeks — and *test yourself* at each one rather than rereading.

## Section 6: The overlearning trap, and when spacing wobbles

One last habit to break. When you finally recall something perfectly, it's tempting to keep drilling it over and over that same night to be *really* sure. Continuing to study past the point of one perfect recall is called **overlearning** — and in one sitting, it's mostly wasted effort.

**Rohrer and Taylor** showed this in 2005 with 218 students learning geography facts and word definitions. Overlearning gave a small boost at 1 week, but that advantage shrank sharply over the following weeks. They flatly called overlearning an **"inefficient strategy"** for lasting retention: those extra same-session repetitions buy very little durable memory. The lesson is clear — once you've got it right, *stop*, and bank that time for a later spaced session instead.

Now, in the spirit of honesty this book promised you: the spacing effect is one of psychology's most replicated findings, but it isn't magic dust that works identically everywhere. Recent classroom studies — especially on complex math procedures — show the benefit can be smaller or less consistent for complicated skills than the clean lab experiments suggest. The lab effect doesn't always transfer perfectly to messy real-world learning. Spacing is still one of your best bets by far. Just don't expect it to be equally dramatic for every kind of material.

**In short:** Don't over-drill the same night (Rohrer and Taylor, 2005, called it inefficient) — save that effort for a later session — and know that spacing's power, while robust, can be gentler for very complex skills.

## Common Mistakes

- **Judging learning by how it *feels*.** Cramming feels fluent and smooth, so it feels like it's working — but that fluency is just retrieval strength, which fades. *Fix:* trust *effort*, not ease. If recall feels a little hard, storage strength is growing.
- **Treating one perfect recall as "done."** Then over-drilling it the same night. *Fix:* stop after you get it right once, and schedule a review for a later day instead.
- **Using tiny within-day gaps.** Reviewing something an hour later and expecting it to stick for months. *Fix:* for durable memory, space reviews at least a *day* apart.
- **Massing all your review right before the test.** That's cramming in disguise. *Fix:* start early and spread reviews across the weeks leading up to it.
- **Expecting spacing to feel easy.** The forgetting *between* sessions makes recall effortful — and beginners assume that means it's not working. *Fix:* remember the difficulty *is* the mechanism.
- **Rereading instead of self-testing.** Passive review wastes the spaced session. *Fix:* close the book and quiz yourself.

---

## Try This

1. **Pick one thing you're learning and build a 4-review schedule this week.** Learn it today, then review after 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, and 3 weeks. Write the dates in your calendar right now so you don't have to remember.
2. **Turn every review into a self-test.** At each scheduled review, put your notes away and try to recall the material *before* you check. Notice how the effortful ones stick best.
3. **Catch yourself over-drilling.** The next time you nail something perfectly, deliberately *stop* studying it that session — and instead jot down a date a few days out to revisit it.

## Key Takeaways

- **Ebbinghaus (1885)** first charted the **forgetting curve**: memory drops fast at first (roughly 58% savings at 20 min, 34% after 1 day) then flattens — and **Murre and Dros (2015)** confirmed the same shape ~130 years later.
- Forgetting isn't just **decay** — much of it is **interference** (old and new memories competing) and **retrieval failure**, where a memory is stored but you've lost the cue to reach it (Tulving's encoding specificity).
- The **Bjorks' New Theory of Disuse (1992)** splits memory into **storage strength** (how deeply learned; never decreases) and **retrieval strength** (how accessible now; fades) — cramming spikes the second and barely touches the first.
- The **spacing effect** — distributing study across sessions — reliably beats **massed practice** (cramming); the **Cepeda et al. (2006)** meta-analysis pooled 839 assessments across 317 experiments to prove it.
- **Cramming isn't useless:** it works for a test the next morning. It only fails for *lasting* retention — it trades long-term memory for short-term performance.
- The optimal review gap is about **10–20% of how long you need the material** (Cepeda et al., 2008, ~1,350 people): ~1 day for a 1-week goal, ~21 days for a 70-day goal.
- **Expanding gaps** work well — for example 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 3 weeks — and each timely review "resets" and flattens the forgetting curve.
- **Overlearning** in one sitting is inefficient (**Rohrer and Taylor, 2005**): stop after one perfect recall and bank the time for a later spaced session.
- **Dunlosky et al. (2013)** rated only two of ten techniques "high utility" — **distributed practice** and **practice testing** — so space your reviews *and* quiz yourself at each one.
