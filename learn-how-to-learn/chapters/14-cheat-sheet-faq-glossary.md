## The one-page cheat sheet

Print this page. Everything else in the book is footnotes to it.

### Techniques ranked by evidence

This ranking comes straight from the research, especially Dunlosky et al.'s 2013 review "Improving Students' Learning" (which rated ten common study techniques) and the wider body of memory science. The two winners are the ones almost nobody uses; the three losers are the ones almost everybody does.

| Rank | Technique | How to do it (one line) | Evidence |
|------|-----------|------------------------|----------|
| ⭐ Highest | **Practice testing** (active recall / retrieval practice) | Close the book and force yourself to recall or answer - flashcards, past papers, blank-page recall. | Very strong (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006) |
| ⭐ Highest | **Spaced practice** (distributed practice) | Spread study across days/weeks; review just as you're about to forget, not all at once. | Very strong (Cepeda et al., 2006) |
| ✅ Strong | **Interleaving** | Mix related topics/problem types in one session instead of doing all of one kind in a block. | Strong (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007) |
| ✅ Strong | **Elaboration / elaborative interrogation** | Keep asking "why is this true?" and "how does it connect to what I know?" | Strong |
| ✅ Strong | **Self-explanation** | Talk yourself through *why* each step works as you study, in your own words. | Strong (Chi et al.) |
| ✅ Strong | **Dual coding** | Pair words with a picture, diagram or sketch so the idea has two routes into memory. | Strong (Paivio) |
| ✅ Strong | **The Feynman technique** | Explain it in plain words as if to a 12-year-old; find the gaps; go back and fix them. | Strong (based on retrieval + elaboration) |
| ⚠️ Moderate | **Summarising / note-making** | Works *if* you rephrase in your own words; useless if you copy verbatim. | Mixed |
| ❌ Weak | **Highlighting / underlining** | Marking text feels productive but adds almost nothing on its own. | Weak (Dunlosky, 2013) |
| ❌ Weak | **Rereading** | Passive rereading breeds the **illusion of competence** - feels fluent, isn't learned. | Weak |
| ❌ Weak | **Cramming** (massed practice) | Great for a test tomorrow, gone by next week. Fails long-term retention. | Weak for lasting memory |

**The 30-second version:** *Test yourself, space it out, mix it up, explain it simply, and get enough sleep.* If you only ever remember one sentence from this book, remember that one.

### The supporting conditions

Techniques don't work in a vacuum. These are the settings that let them work.

| Lever | The move |
|-------|----------|
| **Focus** | One task at a time. Multitasking isn't real - it's fast switching that leaks **attention residue** (Leroy, 2009). Try a **Pomodoro** (25 min on, 5 off). |
| **Sleep** | 7–9 hours. Sleep *consolidates* memories; a sleepless night can cut the hippocampus's ability to form new memories by ~40% (Walker). Sleep *before* and *after* learning. |
| **Exercise** | Aerobic movement raises **BDNF**, a protein that helps grow and connect neurons. Even a walk before studying helps. |
| **Effort** | If it feels a bit hard, it's probably working. These are **desirable difficulties** (Bjork): struggle that builds durable memory. |
| **Practice** | To improve a skill, use **deliberate practice** - work at the edge of your ability, on your weak spots, with immediate feedback and correction (Ericsson). |
| **Motivation** | Set specific goals, track progress, and use **implementation intentions**: "When X happens, I will do Y." |

## The 30-day quick-start plan

You don't need to adopt everything at once. Here's a gentle ramp that layers one habit at a time.

```
WEEK 1  - Capture & test
  Day 1   Pick ONE subject. Study a small chunk normally.
  Day 2+  After every study block, close the book and write down
          what you remember (blank-page recall). That's it.
  Goal: replace rereading with retrieval.

WEEK 2  - Space it
  Start flashcards (Anki or paper Leitner box) for the facts.
  Review yesterday's + last week's cards, not just today's.
  Goal: let intervals stretch as recall gets easier.

WEEK 3  - Mix & deepen
  Interleave: shuffle problem types / topics in one session.
  For each hard idea, do a 5-min Feynman explanation out loud.
  Goal: harder sessions, deeper connections.

WEEK 4  - Systematise & sustain
  Set a weekly review slot. Protect sleep (fixed bedtime).
  Add one Pomodoro of deep, phone-away focus per day.
  Goal: a repeatable system you'll still use in month 6.
```

Keep it small. A habit you actually repeat beats a perfect system you abandon in a week.

## Master list of common mistakes

Every trap the book warned about, in one place. Each line is a mistake → the fix.

- **Rereading and highlighting and calling it studying** → Close the book and test yourself instead. Recognition isn't recall.
- **Cramming the night before** → Space the same hours across days. Same effort, far more retained.
- **Studying until it feels easy, then stopping** → "Feels easy" is the **illusion of competence**. Stop when you can *explain it*, not when it *feels familiar*.
- **Blocking practice** (all of topic A, then all of B) → Interleave. Mixing feels worse and works better.
- **Making study comfortable** → Comfortable often means ineffective. Welcome desirable difficulty.
- **Passive notes** (transcribing the lecture) → Note-*making*: rephrase, question, connect. Cornell or Zettelkasten over stenography.
- **Multitasking** → Single-task. Every switch costs you attention residue and time.
- **Skipping sleep to study more** → You're trading the consolidation that turns study into memory. Bad deal.
- **Practising what you're already good at** → Deliberate practice targets weaknesses at the edge of your ability.
- **Waiting to feel motivated** → Motivation follows action. Use tiny starts and implementation intentions.
- **Trusting your gut about how well you've learned** → Judgments of learning are badly miscalibrated. Test to know.
- **Chasing "learning styles"** → Matching to a "visual/auditory" style has no reliable evidence. Match the *material*, not the myth.

## FAQ

Real questions beginners ask, answered plainly.

**Q: How many hours a day should I study?**
There's no magic number - quality beats quantity. Two or three focused, tested, single-tasked hours beat eight distracted ones. Because of the spacing effect, *when* you study matters more than *how long*: four one-hour sessions across a week crush one four-hour marathon.

**Q: Is cramming ever OK?**
Yes - for one purpose. If you have a test tomorrow and haven't studied, cramming (**massed practice**) will get information into short-term memory for a few hours. Just know the cost: most of it will be gone within days. For anything you actually need to keep, space it out.

**Q: Are learning styles real?**
No - not in the way people mean. The idea that you're a "visual learner" or "auditory learner" and should be taught in that style is one of education's most popular myths, and controlled studies have failed to support it. What's true is that most material is best learned in the format that fits *the content* (a map is visual, a poem is verbal) and that combining words with visuals (**dual coding**) helps everyone.

**Q: How do I stop forgetting things?**
You can't stop forgetting - it's how the brain works (Ebbinghaus mapped the **forgetting curve** in 1885). But you can beat it: every time you retrieve something just as you're about to forget it, you flatten the curve and stretch the next interval. That's spaced repetition. Forgetting a little, then recalling, is the mechanism, not the failure.

**Q: Does rereading work?**
Barely. Rereading is the most common study method and one of the weakest. It creates fluency - the words feel familiar - which fools you into thinking you've learned them (the illusion of competence). Swap most of your rereading time for self-testing.

**Q: What about handwriting vs typing notes?**
Handwriting is usually better for *learning*, not because of the pen but because you can't write fast enough to transcribe - so you're forced to summarise and rephrase, which is elaboration. Typing tempts you into verbatim capture, which is nearly useless. If you type, deliberately paraphrase. The goal is note-*making*, not note-*taking*.

**Q: How do I stay motivated?**
Stop waiting to feel motivated; motivation usually arrives *after* you start, not before. Lower the bar to starting (just five minutes), use **implementation intentions** ("After dinner, I do 20 flashcards at my desk"), track visible progress, and connect the work to a goal you actually care about (intrinsic motivation lasts longer than rewards or fear).

**Q: Is multitasking really that bad?**
Yes. The brain doesn't do two demanding things at once; it switches, and each switch leaves **attention residue** that drags on the next task (Leroy, 2009). Studying with your phone buzzing means you're paying a constant switching tax. Put it in another room.

**Q: How long until I see results?**
The uncomfortable truth: the methods that work best (testing, spacing, interleaving) feel *worse* and *slower* in the moment - that's why they're called desirable difficulties. Trust the delayed payoff. On a test a week or a month later, these methods routinely beat the "easy" ones by wide margins.

**Q: Is the 10,000-hour rule true?**
No, not as popularly stated. It's a catchy simplification of Ericsson's violinist research. The real finding is that it's not raw hours but *deliberate practice* - focused, effortful, feedback-driven work on your weaknesses - that builds expertise. Ten thousand hours of mindless repetition makes you experienced, not expert.

**Q: Do I need Anki, or are paper flashcards fine?**
Both work; the principle (spaced retrieval) matters, not the tool. A physical **Leitner box** - cards you move between boxes reviewed at different frequencies - captures the same idea with zero software. Use whatever you'll actually keep using.

**Q: What if a topic is boring or I keep procrastinating?**
Shrink it. Procrastination is often about the discomfort of *starting*, not the task itself. Commit to a single Pomodoro. Interleaving also helps - variety fights boredom. And connect the boring bit to something you want, so there's a "why" behind it.

**Q: Is highlighting a waste of time?**
Mostly, when it's your main method. On its own, highlighting is passive and near-useless. It can be a *first step* - marking what to turn into questions or flashcards - but the highlighting itself isn't the learning.

**Q: Does teaching others really help me learn?**
Yes - it's one of the best tricks in the book (the **protégé effect**). Preparing to teach and explaining out loud forces retrieval, exposes your gaps, and makes you organise the idea. You don't even need a real student; explaining to an empty room, a rubber duck, or a page works. That's the Feynman technique.

**Q: What's the single highest-return habit?**
Sleep, closely followed by self-testing. Nothing else you do matters much if you're not consolidating it. Protect your sleep first, then test yourself relentlessly.

## Glossary

Every key term in the book, one plain line each.

- **Active recall** - Pulling information out of your memory (vs putting it in); the core of practice testing. See *retrieval practice*.
- **Anki** - Free, open-source spaced-repetition flashcard software that schedules reviews for you.
- **Attention residue** - The mental "smear" left behind when you switch tasks, hurting the next task (Leroy, 2009).
- **BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor)** - A protein, boosted by exercise, that helps neurons grow and connect; often called "fertiliser for the brain".
- **Chunking** - Grouping small bits of information into one meaningful unit so working memory can hold more.
- **Cognitive load** - The total mental effort a task puts on your limited working memory; overload blocks learning.
- **Consolidation** - The process, especially during sleep, by which fragile new memories become stable long-term ones.
- **Cornell method** - A note-making layout splitting the page into cues, notes, and a summary.
- **Cramming** - See *massed practice*.
- **Deliberate practice** - Focused, effortful practice at the edge of your ability, on your weaknesses, with immediate feedback (Ericsson).
- **Desirable difficulties** - Struggles introduced on purpose (spacing, testing, interleaving) that feel harder but build stronger memory (Bjork).
- **Diffuse mode** - The relaxed, wandering brain state (walking, showering) that makes loose connections and creative leaps (Oakley).
- **Distributed practice** - See *spacing effect*.
- **Dual coding** - Combining words with visuals so an idea gets two routes into memory (Paivio).
- **Dunlosky et al. (2013)** - The review that ranked ten study techniques; crowned practice testing and distributed practice.
- **Ebbinghaus (1885)** - The psychologist who first measured memory and drew the forgetting curve.
- **Elaboration / elaborative interrogation** - Deepening memory by asking "why?" and "how?" and linking new material to what you know.
- **Encoding** - The first stage of memory: getting information *in*. You can't remember what you never encoded.
- **Feynman technique** - Learn by explaining a concept in the simplest possible words, spotting your gaps, and fixing them.
- **Flow** - The absorbed state of full engagement where a challenge matches your skill and time seems to vanish (Csikszentmihalyi).
- **Focused mode** - The concentrated, deliberate brain state used for tackling a specific problem head-on (Oakley).
- **Forgetting curve** - Ebbinghaus's finding that memory drops steeply at first, then levels off.
- **Generation effect** - You remember things better when you generate the answer yourself rather than reading it.
- **Growth mindset** - The belief that ability can be developed through effort (Dweck); real but its effect sizes are debated.
- **Illusion of competence** - Mistaking familiarity (it *feels* easy) for actual mastery; the trap of passive rereading.
- **Implementation intention** - A specific "When X, I will do Y" plan that makes good habits automatic (Gollwitzer).
- **Interference** - Forgetting caused by other memories crowding in (proactive = old blocks new; retroactive = new blocks old).
- **Interleaving** - Mixing different topics or problem types within a session instead of blocking them.
- **Judgment of learning** - Your self-estimate of how well you've learned something; usually overconfident and miscalibrated.
- **Leitner box** - A physical spaced-repetition system: flashcards move between boxes reviewed at different intervals.
- **Long-term memory** - The brain's vast, durable store of information.
- **Massed practice (cramming)** - Packing all study into one block; fine for tomorrow, poor for lasting memory.
- **Metacognition** - "Thinking about your own thinking" - knowing what you do and don't understand (Flavell).
- **Metalearning** - Learning how to learn; the master skill this whole book is about.
- **Miller's 7 ± 2** - The classic (1956) estimate of working memory capacity; later revised down to ~4 chunks (Cowan).
- **Mind map** - A visual, branching diagram of ideas around a central topic (Buzan).
- **Personal knowledge management (PKM)** - Systems and tools (e.g. Obsidian) for capturing and connecting what you learn.
- **Pomodoro technique** - Work in timed sprints (classically 25 min) with short breaks to sustain focus.
- **Practice testing** - Studying by testing yourself; the highest-evidence technique in the book.
- **Protégé effect** - You learn material more deeply when you prepare to teach it, or actually teach it, to others.
- **Retrieval practice** - See *active recall* and *testing effect*.
- **Self-explanation** - Explaining to yourself *why* each step or fact makes sense as you study (Chi et al.).
- **Self-regulated learning** - Managing your own learning through a cycle of planning → doing → reflecting (Zimmerman).
- **Sensory memory** - The split-second buffer holding raw sights and sounds before they fade or get attended to.
- **SM-2** - The spaced-repetition algorithm behind SuperMemo and Anki that expands intervals based on recall quality (Wozniak).
- **Spacing effect** - You remember more when study is spread over time than when it's crammed (Cepeda et al., 2006).
- **SQ3R** - A reading method: Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review.
- **Storage** - The middle stage of memory: holding encoded information over time.
- **Testing effect** - The finding that retrieving information strengthens memory more than restudying it (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006).
- **Working memory** - The tiny mental "desk" that holds and manipulates a few items right now (Baddeley & Hitch).
- **Zettelkasten (slip-box)** - A linked network of atomic notes in your own words that grows into a thinking tool (Luhmann; Ahrens).

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating this chapter as reading, not reference** → Don't just skim it once. Bookmark it and return whenever you plan a session or hit a doubt.
- **Grabbing every technique at once** → You'll drop them all. Add one habit at a time, as in the 30-day plan.
- **Keeping the "feels good" methods because they're comfortable** → Rereading and highlighting feel productive precisely because they're easy and shallow. Trust the harder, higher-evidence moves.
- **Memorising the glossary as trivia** → The words are hooks back to the ideas. If a definition feels thin, reread the chapter it came from.
- **Forgetting that the conditions matter as much as the techniques** → Perfect flashcards on four hours of sleep still fail. Sleep, focus and exercise are not optional extras.

## Key Takeaways

- The two highest-evidence techniques are **practice testing** and **spaced practice**; almost everything else is support around them (Dunlosky et al., 2013).
- **Interleaving, elaboration, self-explanation and dual coding** are strong seconds; **highlighting, rereading and cramming** are weak - yet the most popular.
- The best methods feel *harder* in the moment - that's the mark of a **desirable difficulty**, not a sign you're doing it wrong.
- **Sleep is the silent multiplier**: it consolidates memory, and losing it can cut new-memory formation by roughly 40% (Walker).
- **Learning styles are a myth**; match the format to the *material*, and pair words with visuals for everyone.
- **Motivation follows action**, not the reverse - shrink the start and use implementation intentions.
- **Expertise comes from deliberate practice** on your weaknesses with feedback, not from raw hours or a mythical 10,000-hour rule (Ericsson).
- Your **gut sense of how well you've learned is unreliable** - only self-testing tells you the truth.
- This chapter is a tool, not a chapter: keep it, and let the loop of *forget → look up → use again* do its quiet work.
