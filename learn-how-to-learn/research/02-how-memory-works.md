# Research Brief - Chapter 2: How Your Memory Actually Works

## Key concepts (plain definitions)
- **Encoding** - getting information *in*: converting what you see/hear into a form the brain can store. Deeper, more meaningful processing encodes better than shallow repetition.
- **Storage** - holding the encoded information over time (seconds to a lifetime).
- **Retrieval** - getting information *back out*: finding and reactivating a stored memory. Retrieval is itself a reconstruction, not a playback (see below).
- **Sensory memory** - a very brief, high-capacity buffer (fractions of a second) holding raw sights/sounds before attention selects some for further processing.
- **Short-term / working memory** - the small, temporary "mental workbench" where you consciously hold and manipulate a few items right now.
- **Long-term memory (LTM)** - effectively unlimited, durable store. Split into **declarative/explicit** (facts and events you can state) - subdivided into **episodic** (personal experiences) and **semantic** (general knowledge) - and **procedural/implicit** (skills like cycling, done without conscious recall).
- **Chunk** - a single meaningful unit built from smaller pieces (e.g. "IBM" as one chunk, not three letters).
- **Schema** - an organized framework of prior knowledge that makes new, related information faster to encode and easier to retrieve.
- **Consolidation** - the process that turns a fragile new memory into a stable, lasting one over hours to years (much of it during sleep).
- **Cognitive load** - the total demand placed on working memory during a task.

## Studies & evidence (author, year, finding, numbers)
- **Miller (1956)**, *The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two* - immediate/short-term memory holds roughly **7 ± 2 (5–9) chunks**. Miller's key insight: capacity is limited by *number of chunks*, not amount of raw information, so chunking expands effective capacity.
- **Cowan (2001)**, *The Magical Number 4* - when rehearsal and long-term-memory support are blocked, the real limit is closer to **about 4 chunks** (often cited as 3–5). This is now the more widely accepted "pure" capacity estimate.
- **Atkinson & Shiffrin (1968)** - the "multi-store"/modal model: sensory → short-term → long-term. Sensory memory lasts ~250 ms–2 s; unrehearsed STM decays in roughly **18–20 seconds**; rehearsal transfers items to LTM.
- **Baddeley & Hitch (1974)** - replaced passive STM with **working memory**: a *central executive* directing a *phonological loop* (verbal/auditory) and *visuospatial sketchpad* (visual/spatial). Baddeley added the *episodic buffer* in 2000.
- **Chase & Simon (1973)** (building on **de Groot, 1965**) - chess masters reconstructed real game positions after a **5-second** glance far better than novices, but were **no better than novices on random positions**. Expertise is stored chunks/patterns in LTM, not a bigger raw memory.
- **Chase & Ericsson (SF study, 1980–82)** - an ordinary student, over ~230+ hours of practice, raised his digit span from **7 to 79 digits** by recoding numbers into familiar running times - a dramatic demonstration of chunking.
- **Hebb (1949)** - "neurons that fire together wire together" (Hebbian learning): co-active neurons strengthen their connection.
- **Lømo (1966) / Bliss & Lømo (1973)** - first experimental demonstration of **long-term potentiation (LTP)** in the rabbit hippocampus: the leading cellular mechanism of learning, confirming Hebb's prediction.
- **Patient H.M. (Henry Molaison; Scoville & Milner, 1957)** - after bilateral hippocampal removal he could form **no new declarative memories**, yet learned new *procedural* skills (mirror-drawing) while denying he'd ever done them. Proved the hippocampus is required for new explicit memories and that memory systems are separable.
- **Tulving (1972)** - distinguished **episodic** from **semantic** memory within LTM.
- **Sweller (1980s–present)** - **Cognitive Load Theory**: working memory can process only a few elements at once, so learning fails when total load exceeds it. Three loads: **intrinsic** (inherent difficulty of the material), **extraneous** (wasteful demands from poor presentation), **germane** (effort that builds schemas). Goal: cut extraneous, manage intrinsic, foster germane.
- **Bartlett (1932)**, *War of the Ghosts* - British readers retelling an unfamiliar folktale systematically distorted it to fit their own culture (**assimilation, leveling, sharpening**). Memory is *reconstructive*, shaped by schemas.
- **Loftus & Palmer (1974)** - 45 students judged car speed; the verb "**smashed**" produced ~**40.8 mph** vs "**hit**" ~**34 mph**; a week later, "smashed" viewers were more than twice as likely to falsely "remember" broken glass. The **misinformation effect** shows retrieval rewrites memory.

## Myths / contested points (with honest nuance)
- **"7 is the magic number."** Popular but outdated; Cowan's **~4** is the better estimate under controlled conditions. Real span depends heavily on chunking and material, so no single number is exact.
- **"Memory is a video recording."** False. Every retrieval partly reconstructs the memory and can alter it (Bartlett, Loftus). This is well replicated.
- **The Atkinson–Shiffrin model** is a useful teaching scaffold but oversimplified - Baddeley's working memory replaced its passive STM box.
- **"Germane load is a separate resource"** is now debated; many researchers fold it into intrinsic load. Sweller himself revised the framework. The three-load teaching split is still useful pedagogically.
- **"We use 10% of our brain"** - myth; unrelated to real memory limits.
- **LTP = memory** is a strong candidate mechanism, not absolute proof; the LTP-to-behavior link is still active research.

## Real-world examples & analogies
- **Phone numbers** are chunked (555-8213, not 5-5-5-8-2-1-3) - everyday proof of Miller's principle.
- **Chess masters** see "familiar patterns," not 32 pieces - one glance, whole board (Chase & Simon).
- **Learning to drive**: at first every action floods working memory (intrinsic + extraneous load); with practice it becomes procedural and nearly effortless.
- **Analogy - the workbench:** working memory is a tiny desk; LTM is a vast warehouse. You can only work on a few items on the desk at once, but storage is nearly limitless.
- **Analogy - retrieval as re-cooking:** recalling isn't reheating a saved dish; it's re-cooking from a recipe each time, so it can come out slightly different.
- **Analogy - schema as coat-hooks:** prior knowledge gives new facts a place to hang, so they don't fall on the floor.

## Practical how-to points
- **Chunk** big information into meaningful groups to beat the ~4-item limit.
- **Build schemas first**: learn the big-picture framework before details so new facts attach to something.
- **Cut extraneous load**: remove clutter, redundant text, and split attention; study one clean stream at a time.
- **Match load to skill**: sequence from simple to complex; don't overload beginners.
- **Protect consolidation**: sleep after studying; space learning over days rather than cramming.
- **Use retrieval practice** (self-testing) - retrieval strengthens memory even though (and partly because) it is effortful reconstruction.

## Common mistakes learners make on this topic
- Treating short-term memory as *storage* rather than a tiny active *workspace* - then trying to hold too much at once.
- Assuming rereading = learning; passive repetition encodes shallowly.
- Believing recall is accurate playback, so trusting confident but distorted memories.
- Learning isolated facts with no schema to hang them on.
- Overloading working memory (multitasking, cluttered materials) and blaming "bad memory" instead of load.
- Cramming, which skips sleep-based consolidation.
- Thinking experts have "photographic memory" rather than domain-specific chunks built over years.
