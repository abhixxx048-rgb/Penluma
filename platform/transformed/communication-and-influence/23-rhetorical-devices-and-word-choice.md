Julius Caesar used one. So did Martin Luther King Jr. So did whoever wrote the subject line of the last marketing email you actually opened. The tool is the same age-old trick, and it isn't complicated: **choose your words on purpose instead of on autopilot.**

Most people write the way they talk - fast, unedited, first-draft. The people whose emails get replied to, whose pitches get funded, whose slides get remembered, are making a handful of these word choices deliberately. This post hands you their toolbox.

## Why this matters

Every sentence you write is a stack of small decisions - which word, how long, ending on a question or a statement. You're making these choices whether you notice or not. The only question is whether you're making the good ones by accident or on purpose.

The devices below aren't about sounding clever. They're about making an idea easier to picture, easier to remember, and easier to trust - which is exactly what determines whether your email gets acted on, your pitch gets funded, or your point gets forgotten by lunchtime. None of them require talent. They require noticing.

## The one swap that changes everything: concrete language

The single biggest lever in persuasive writing is the choice between abstract and concrete words.

**Abstract language** names ideas or qualities you can't see, touch, or picture - "quality," "efficiency," "innovative," "value." **Concrete language** names things you can picture, hear, or check - "arrives in 48 hours," "cuts your invoice time from three days to twenty minutes," "the screen cracked on the first drop."

The nuance matters here. When people talk about a product to friends, broad praise like "it's high quality" can actually travel further, because it implies general goodness. But in direct interactions - a salesperson with a customer, a manager writing to a client - concrete language wins decisively. Customers rate concrete-speaking employees as more helpful, and go on to spend meaningfully more time and money with the company. So when you're talking directly to the person you want to persuade, trade your abstractions for specifics.

| Abstract (weak) | Concrete (strong) |
| --- | --- |
| We are committed to customer satisfaction. | We answer every support ticket within four hours. |
| The proposal offers significant savings. | The proposal saves $42,000 a year - one full salary. |
| Our team has deep expertise. | Our team has shipped this exact migration eleven times. |

Inside concrete language sit two smaller tools worth knowing by name. **Sensory words** trigger sight, sound, touch, taste, or smell - "sharp," "quiet," "gritty," "warm." **Power words** are short, high-impact words that carry emotional weight - "free," "proven," "instantly," "because," "guaranteed." They work partly through emotion and partly through familiarity: the brain has learned to associate them with reward or urgency, so they get processed faster and stick around longer.

Use them sparingly. "Amazing, guaranteed, instant results, act now!" burns the reader's trust and the words' effect in the same sentence. Power words work because they're rare and true.

## Borrow a picture the reader already owns: metaphor and analogy

A **metaphor** calls one thing by the name of another to transfer its qualities ("our roadmap is a minefield"). An **analogy** explains something unfamiliar by linking it to something familiar ("onboarding a client is like training for a marathon - the slow weeks matter more than race day"). Both work for the same reason: the brain understands new things by relating them to things it already knows. Instead of building your reader a brand-new mental model, you hand them one they already own and just relabel it.

A product manager explaining technical debt to non-technical executives puts it this way: *"Right now we're paying interest on a loan we took out two years ago to ship faster. If we don't pay down the principal this quarter, the interest - the bug count - eats the whole engineering budget."* No one needs a computer science degree to follow that.

Metaphor does three jobs at once. It simplifies (a complex system becomes "a leaky bucket"). It sticks (people forget statistics but remember pictures). And it carries emotional tone - calling a budget cut "trimming fat" feels very different from calling it "amputating a limb," even though both describe the same action.

The catch is George Orwell's old warning against "dying metaphors" - comparisons so overused they've lost their picture entirely: "low-hanging fruit," "move the needle," "boil the ocean." Nobody sees fruit anymore when they hear that phrase; it's become filler. The fix isn't to avoid metaphor. It's to build one from something [your specific audience](/blog/communication-and-influence/04-how-to-know-your-audience) actually lives inside - sports for a competitive sales team, cooking for a hospitality client - and to pick one image and stay inside it, rather than mixing three in one breath ("let's circle back and open the kimono to move the needle").

## Why three feels complete: the rule of three and parallelism

"Veni, vidi, vici." "Life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness." "Blood, sweat, and tears." "Snap, Crackle, Pop." These have survived for a reason that has nothing to do with the specific words: **three is the smallest number that reads as a pattern instead of a coincidence.** Two items feel like a pair. Four starts to feel like a list you have to work at. Three feels complete and rhythmic - easy to hold in your head.

This works at every scale of professional writing: three bullets on a slide, three reasons in a pitch, three examples in an interview answer. Compare "I'm organized, I communicate well, I also have good technical skills, plus I'm a fast learner" (four vague traits blurring together) with "I plan carefully, I communicate clearly, and I deliver on time." Three, parallel, memorable.

That second version works because of **parallelism** - giving matching ideas matching grammatical form. "We listen. We learn. We deliver." is parallel: three short, same-shaped statements. "We do a lot of listening. Learning happens too. And delivery is the result." is the identical content, wrecked by mismatched grammar. Parallelism isn't just tidy - it cuts the reader's mental effort, because once they've decoded the shape of the first item, the rest are free.

One warning: don't pad a three-part list (sometimes called a **tricolon**) with a weak third item just to hit three. "Fast, reliable, and good" - "good" adds nothing. A weak third point is worse than stopping at two; it tells the reader you ran out of real ideas.

## Let contrast do the persuading: antithesis and rhetorical questions

**Antithesis** places two opposite ideas side by side, usually in matching grammar. It works because contrast is how the brain notices things - a steady gray wall is invisible, a single red door on it is not. King's "I Have a Dream" speech calls the Emancipation Proclamation "a great beacon light of hope" arriving after "a long night of captivity." Armstrong compressed it into eleven words: "one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind" - small versus giant, one man versus all mankind.

In professional writing, antithesis is a clean way to state a trade-off honestly: "This plan will cost us headcount today so it can save the company tomorrow." Naming both sides of the trade signals you've actually weighed the alternative, which builds more credibility than burying the bad news in qualifiers.

A **rhetorical question** is asked not to get an answer, but to make the reader answer it in their own head - which makes them feel like they arrived at your conclusion themselves. "How much longer can we afford to lose customers to a worse product?" does more work than "We are losing customers to a worse competitor," because the reader has to actively process an answer instead of passively receiving a fact. This is sometimes called the [self-persuasion effect](/blog/communication-and-influence/06-cognitive-biases-in-persuasion): conclusions we generate ourselves are trusted more than conclusions we're handed. Use it once, to open a section you're about to answer in detail. Stack three in a row ("Isn't it time we changed? Don't you want better results? Wouldn't you rather succeed?") and it starts to feel like pressure - readers tune it out.

## The drumbeat: repetition, anaphora, and cadence

Not all repetition is a mistake. **Anaphora** - repeating the same word or phrase at the start of successive sentences - is the engine behind some of history's most quoted lines. King's "I have a dream" opens clause after clause. Churchill's wartime speech repeats "we shall fight" seven times. The repeated phrase becomes a drumbeat the listener's mind locks onto, so each new ending lands with more force because the opening is already familiar.

A founder rallying a team after a hard quarter might say: *"We built this company when nobody believed in the idea. We built this company when we had no funding. We built this company when our first product failed. We can build our way through this too."* Four facts, turned into one rising argument.

Underneath all of this sits **cadence** - the rhythm created by sentence length. "The plan works. It is simple. It ships on time." Three short, clipped sentences feel confident and certain. Now stretch the same information into one long, hedging sentence full of qualifiers, and it sounds unsure of itself even though nothing factual changed. Varying sentence length on purpose - a run of short sentences to build tension, then one longer sentence to release it - is one of the most underused tools in business writing, because most professional prose defaults to a flat, medium-length monotone.

The test for whether repetition is a device or a tic: does it build toward a climax? Using the same weak transition word ("Also… Also… Also…") throughout a document isn't anaphora - it's just monotonous.

## Frame the gain, center the reader: framing and "you" focus

**Framing** is choosing which true angle of a fact to present. The classic demonstration: a food product labeled "85% fat-free" is chosen far more often than the identical product labeled "15% fat," even though the numbers say exactly the same thing. [Positive framing](/blog/communication-and-influence/07-framing-in-communication) - stating what's gained rather than what's lost - tends to feel more motivating, especially when the audience doesn't already have a strong opinion about the thing being described.

"This process eliminates 90% of manual errors" lands better than "This process is only 10% error-prone," even though both describe the identical result. The first frames the change as a gain. The second frames it as a residual flaw. Framing isn't lying - the underlying fact has to stay true - but it is a real choice about which true angle leads.

The second lever here is the **"you" focus**: writing from the reader's point of view instead of the writer's. Compare "We are proud to announce our new dashboard" with "You can now see your whole week at a glance." The first centers the company. The second centers the reader, and answers the question every reader is silently asking: *what does this mean for me?*

| Writer-centered ("we") | Reader-centered ("you") |
| --- | --- |
| We built this feature to save time. | You'll get an extra two hours back every week. |
| Our team reduced the error rate. | You'll see fewer mistakes on every order. |
| We are excited to launch this update. | You now have full control over your dashboard. |

After drafting anything persuasive, count how many sentences start with "we" or "I" versus "you" or "your." If "we" wins by a wide margin, rewrite a few openings to put the reader first.

## Rhetoric is salt, not the meal

Every device in this toolbox shares one danger: overuse. A sentence carrying a metaphor, a tricolon, an antithesis, and a rhetorical question all at once doesn't sound powerful - it sounds like it's trying too hard, and readers notice performance faster than they notice sincerity. Rhetoric is a spice. The strongest professional writing is [plain writing](/blog/communication-and-influence/20-how-to-write-with-clarity) with two or three well-placed devices, not a page where every line does a trick.

There's also an [ethical line](/blog/communication-and-influence/08-persuasion-vs-manipulation) running through all of it. Concrete language, metaphor, and framing are tools of clarity when they make a true thing easier to picture. They become tools of manipulation the moment they're used to make a false thing feel true - a vague risk framed as a guaranteed gain, a real loss hidden inside a cheerful metaphor. The test is simple: *would the device still feel fair to the reader if they could see exactly what you were doing and why?* Ethical rhetoric survives that inspection. Manipulative rhetoric depends on the reader not noticing.

A vendor saying "our solution has helped teams like yours cut costs" is vague and unverifiable - technically true of almost anything. "Three of your closest competitors cut onboarding costs by 30% using this in the last year" is concrete, specific, and checkable. Both are persuasive. Only the second respects the reader enough to be falsifiable.

## Common misconceptions

- **"More devices make writing more persuasive."** The opposite is usually true. One or two well-placed devices land harder than five stacked into a paragraph, which reads as performance.
- **"Rhetoric is inherently manipulative."** It's manipulative only when it's used to make something false feel true. Used on a true claim, it's just clarity with better delivery.
- **"Metaphors and framing are basically the same as spin."** Spin hides a real cost. Framing highlights a real gain. The underlying fact has to stay true either way - the difference is honesty, not technique.
- **"If the idea is good, the words don't matter."** A weak [argument](/blog/communication-and-influence/24-building-arguments-and-evidence) dressed in rhetorical polish just becomes a more noticeable weak argument once the reader sees past it. Rhetoric can't rescue a bad idea - but a good idea told plainly, with zero devices, routinely loses to the same idea told with two well-placed ones.

## How to use this

1. **Draft plainly first.** Write your message with no devices at all - just get the argument down. This keeps you from spreading rhetorical effort thin.
2. **Scan for abstract nouns.** Look for words ending in "-tion," "-ity," or "-ness" (implementation, scalability, effectiveness) and swap at least one for a concrete number or image.
3. **Add exactly one or two devices, at the moments that matter most** - your opening line and your closing ask. Not every sentence.
4. **Build one analogy from your specific audience's world** - sports, cooking, road trips, whatever they already live inside - instead of reaching for a generic or overused one.
5. **Count your "we" versus "you."** If "we" dominates, rewrite a few sentence openings to center the reader.
6. **Read the draft aloud.** If every sentence takes the same breath to say, break the pattern deliberately - vary the length somewhere on purpose.
7. **Run the straight-face test before sending:** Is the underlying claim true? Would you explain the word choice openly if asked? Does removing a device weaken the sentence, or just shorten it? If a device fails that test, cut it.

## Conclusion

None of these devices make a false thing true or a weak argument strong. What they do is make a true, strong idea easier to picture, easier to remember, and easier to believe - which is the entire job of persuasive writing. Pick one or two, place them where they'll be noticed, and let the rest of your sentence stay plain.

The words you choose are only half the equation, though. The other half is timing - when you say something can matter as much as how you say it. That's the next tool worth adding to your kit.
