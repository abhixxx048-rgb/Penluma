Two people give the same quarterly update. One says: "Churn dropped 12%. Tickets fell 30%. NPS rose four points." The other says: "Six months ago, a customer named Maria almost cancelled after three brutal days. Here's what we changed - and what it means for every account like hers." Same underlying facts. By Friday, only one of these updates is still living in anyone's head.

That's not a matter of charisma or personality. It's biology - and once you see the mechanism, you can use it on purpose.

## Why this matters

Most of us were trained to lead with the evidence: the number, the chart, the bullet point. It feels rigorous. It also gets forgotten almost instantly, because the human brain was never built to hold onto facts floating in isolation.

If you [present ideas](/blog/communication-and-influence/10-how-to-structure-a-presentation), pitch projects, manage a team, sell anything, or just want people to actually remember and act on what you tell them, understanding *why* stories stick - and facts usually don't - turns storytelling from a "nice to have" soft skill into a tool you can deploy on purpose, in a specific order, for a specific effect.

## Your brain didn't evolve to store facts - it evolved to follow narrative

Here's an uncomfortable truth for anyone who loves data: your brain didn't evolve to warehouse disconnected information. It evolved to track cause and effect - who did what to whom, what happened next, what's dangerous, what's safe. That's **narrative**: a sequence of connected events with a beginning, a problem, and a resolution. A story is exactly the shape your brain is hungry for. A fact with no story around it is food your brain doesn't quite know how to digest.

Neuroscientist **Uri Hasson** at Princeton has spent years putting people in brain scanners while they listen to real, unscripted stories. What he found is remarkable: when a listener genuinely connects with a story, their brain activity starts to physically mirror the storyteller's. Researchers call this **neural coupling** - the listener's and speaker's brains rising and falling together, like two instruments playing in sync.

The stronger the coupling, the better the listener understands and later recalls what was said. And this syncing barely happens with a list of disconnected facts - it shows up specifically with narrative.

Think of two guitars in the same room. Pluck a string on one, and if the other is tuned the same way, its matching string vibrates too, untouched. A well-told story does this to a listener's brain - it doesn't just inform them, it makes their mental strings hum in sympathy with yours.

You've probably felt the professional version of this already. A list of product features doesn't get anyone's brain vibrating. But say, "Our lead engineer was mid-flight when the outage hit, and by the time she landed, the fix was already live because the on-call system worked exactly as designed," and the listener stops receiving information - they start running a small simulation of the event, as if it happened to them.

## Stories change your brain chemistry, not just your attention

Stories don't just sync activity between two brains - they change the chemical mix inside one. Neuroeconomist **Paul Zak** ran experiments showing that an emotionally engaging story - one with a character, real tension, and a resolution - triggers a release of **oxytocin**, a hormone linked to trust, empathy, and connection.

In Zak's studies, people whose oxytocin rose after watching a character-driven story became measurably more generous and more willing to trust strangers afterward. A dry list of facts doesn't reliably do this. A story about one specific person facing one specific struggle does.

This is the actual mechanism behind why a good story can calm an angry client, move a skeptical stakeholder, or pull a divided team back together in a way pure logic often can't. Facts persuade the analytical part of the mind. Stories additionally recruit the emotional and social parts - and decisions, decades of psychology confirm, [are rarely made by logic alone](/blog/psychology-of-decisions/03-how-the-brain-actually-decides-emotion-memory-attention).

Stories are also just easier to hold onto. Stanford marketing professor **Jennifer Aaker** ran a classroom exercise where students gave one-minute pitches - nine out of ten leaned on statistics, one told a human story. Afterward, only about 5% of the audience could recall a specific number from the fact-heavy pitches. **63% could recall the story.**

That's not a small gap. That's the difference between an idea that survives the meeting and one that evaporates the moment everyone stands up.

Before your next presentation, ask yourself one question: what's the one moment, person, or scene in this material that someone could retell to a colleague tomorrow? If you can't answer that, your audience won't be able to either, no matter how airtight your data is.

One caution: treating [emotion as unprofessional or manipulative](/blog/communication-and-influence/08-persuasion-vs-manipulation) is a mistake in the other direction. Used honestly - grounded in a real event, a real customer, a real risk - emotional resonance isn't a trick. It's simply how human memory and trust actually work. The problem is faking it or overusing it until it curdles, not using it at all.

## The curse of knowledge - and why story is the cure

Here's a trap almost every expert falls into. Once you know something deeply - your product, your industry, your data - it becomes almost impossible to remember what it's like *not* to know it. Researchers call this the **curse of knowledge**: the difficulty an expert has imagining what a non-expert doesn't already understand.

It's why so many presentations and reports are stuffed with unexplained jargon that makes perfect sense to the person who wrote them and means almost nothing to everyone else.

Researcher **Elizabeth Newton** demonstrated this with a now-famous experiment. She asked one group of people to tap out the rhythm of a well-known song, like "Happy Birthday," using only a finger on a table. Before tapping, she asked them to guess: what percentage of listeners would correctly name the song? Tappers guessed around 50%. In reality, listeners guessed correctly only about **2.5% of the time** - roughly 1 in 40.

Why such a huge gap? Because the tappers could hear the tune in their head as they tapped, so the connection felt obvious. Listeners heard only disconnected taps. The tapper's own knowledge made it impossible to imagine what it sounded like to someone without it.

This happens constantly at work. An engineer says "we just need to refactor the auth layer" and assumes everyone follows. A finance lead says "the variance is driven by FX headwinds" and assumes it's obvious. To the speaker, the meaning is self-evident. To the room, it's disconnected taps on a table.

Story is the antidote, almost by accident. A story only works if the listener can follow a sequence of events from the outside, with no prior knowledge required - so telling one *forces* you to rebuild the context you'd otherwise skip.

Say "our latency dropped due to edge caching optimization" and most people are lost. Say instead: "Imagine you're a customer in Singapore loading your dashboard. Before, that request traveled all the way to servers in Virginia and back - about half a second, every time. Now it's answered by a server just down the road." The client with zero idea what "edge caching" means understands completely, because the story supplied the setting, the character, and the stakes a technical statement skipped.

## What makes an idea stick: the SUCCESs formula

If stories are this powerful, why do some stories and ideas stick while others are forgotten by lunchtime? Authors **Chip Heath** and **Dan Heath** studied why certain ideas survive - from urban legends to safety campaigns to great teaching moments - in their book *Made to Stick*, and distilled the pattern into six ingredients, packaged as the acronym **SUCCESs**:

| Principle | Plain-English meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | Strip the idea to one core message, not ten | "Our one job this quarter is to cut onboarding time in half." |
| **Unexpected** | Break an assumption to earn attention | "Our biggest churn driver wasn't price. It was a confusing welcome email." |
| **Concrete** | Specific, sensory detail over abstraction | Not "improve efficiency" but "cut approval from 9 emails to 1 click." |
| **Credible** | Give a reason to believe you | "Ask any of our 40 support agents - they'll tell you the same thing." |
| **Emotional** | Connect to something people already value | "This delay isn't costing us $12,000. It's costing Maria her Saturday." |
| **Stories** | Wrap the idea in a real sequence of events | Telling Maria's story, rather than just stating the $12,000. |

Notice "Story" is only one of six letters. The Heaths aren't saying facts don't matter - "Credible" and "Concrete" both lean directly on solid evidence and specific numbers.

The real insight is that facts and story aren't rivals. A sticky idea usually needs a simple, surprising, [concrete, credible message](/blog/communication-and-influence/24-building-arguments-and-evidence) *carried inside* a story that makes people feel something.

One common mistake: forcing every element into a message regardless of fit. Not every idea needs a surprise twist, and not every story needs a statistic bolted onto the end. Aim for a natural, honest fit between the principles - not a checklist ticked by force.

## When to use story, when to use data - and why it's usually both

None of this means throw out your spreadsheets. Facts still matter enormously - they build credibility, satisfy analytical decision-makers, and protect you when someone challenges your claims. The real skill is knowing which one leads, and when.

Reach for **data first** when your goal is to prove something precisely, defend a decision, or compare exact options - especially with a highly analytical, skeptical audience (an auditor, a scientist, a rigorous finance committee). Reach for **story first** when your goal is to make people care, help them remember your message next month, make an abstract risk feel real, or move people to act rather than just agree.

In practice, the most persuasive communicators use a simple sequence: open with a short, concrete story to earn attention and emotional buy-in, then immediately follow with the data that proves the story represents a real, repeatable pattern - not a cherry-picked exception.

The order matters. Data-first, story-second often loses the room before the story even arrives, because the numbers have already triggered a silent "so what?" Story-first, data-second uses the story to open the door, then the data to make sure people trust what walked through it.

Picture a sales leader making the case for a new pricing model. She opens: "Last quarter, we almost lost our biggest account because their new procurement lead couldn't understand our invoice. It took three calls and a week of back-and-forth just to get paid." Then the data lands: "That wasn't a one-off - since we simplified invoicing, on-time payments are up 22% across all 340 accounts like theirs." The story creates the *why*. The number proves the *how big* and *how repeatable*.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Storytelling is a soft skill, not a real one."** It's closer to a precision tool. Neural coupling and oxytocin release are measurable, physical effects - not vibes.
- **"Using emotion in a pitch is manipulative."** Manipulation is faking or overusing emotion. A true, specific story about a real customer or risk isn't a trick - it's just how memory and trust actually work.
- **"If the data is strong enough, people will remember it."** Jennifer Aaker's classroom study found 5% statistic recall versus 63% story recall. Strong data with no story around it is still forgettable.
- **"A single powerful anecdote is proof of a trend."** It isn't, and treating it that way damages your credibility with a sharp audience. A story should open the door; data should confirm it's not a one-off.
- **"Being an expert makes you a clear communicator."** Often the opposite - the more you know, the harder it is to imagine what your listener doesn't. That's the curse of knowledge, and it needs a deliberate fix.

## How to use this

1. **Before any pitch, presentation, or update, find the one scene.** Ask: what's the single moment, person, or event someone could retell to a colleague tomorrow? If you can't name one, find one before you write another slide.
2. **Open with the story, not the number.** Thirty to ninety seconds of a specific, real situation - then follow with the data that shows it's a pattern, not an exception.
3. **Make the story concrete, not general.** Name a real person, a real moment, a real detail. "A customer in Singapore" beats "our international users."
4. **Run big messages through SUCCESs.** Can you state the one simple core idea, the one unexpected hook, and the one concrete scene? If not, it's not ready yet.
5. **Assume your listener doesn't have your context.** Before you explain something technical, ask what a story about it would need to include - the setting, the character, the stakes - and lead with that instead of the jargon.
6. **[Keep a story that proves your point at hand](/blog/communication-and-influence/19-build-your-personal-story-bank).** Actively collect small, specific moments from your own work - a customer email, an outage, a near-miss - so you have real material ready when the pitch comes.

## Conclusion

Facts prove something is true. Stories make people feel it's true, remember it, and act on it - and your brain is built to do exactly that: sync with a storyteller, release trust chemicals for a good character-driven moment, and quietly discard a stray statistic by Friday. The skill isn't choosing story over data. It's opening with the story that earns attention, then backing it with the data that earns trust.

Getting the story right is only half the job, though - the other half is landing it with the right person in the right way. That's a very different set of levers, and it's worth understanding [Cialdini's seven principles of persuasion](/blog/communication-and-influence/05-cialdini-7-principles-of-persuasion) before your next big pitch.
