# Chapter 6: Interleaving and Desirable Difficulties

## Why this chapter matters

Imagine two young batters at a baseball practice. The first steps into the cage and faces twenty fastballs in a row. By the tenth swing he's crushing them - clean contact, confident, smiling. The second batter faces a jumbled mix: fastball, curveball, changeup, fastball, curveball, in no predictable order. He looks awful. He mistimes swings, fouls balls off, and walks away frustrated while the first kid looks like a natural.

Now fast-forward to the real game, weeks later. A pitcher stands sixty feet away and he is *not* going to announce which pitch is coming. Who hits better? The frustrated kid. This isn't a made-up story - it's roughly what researchers Hall, Domingues, and Cavazos found in a 1994 study of real batters. The messy, error-filled practice built the skill that actually mattered: reading a pitch you weren't expecting.

This is one of the strangest and most useful truths in the science of learning. The practice that *feels* the best is often the practice that *works* the worst. And the practice that feels clumsy, slow, and mistake-ridden is frequently the one building durable skill.

In this chapter you'll meet two ideas that explain the batting-cage mystery: **interleaving** (mixing up what you practice) and **desirable difficulties** (the counter-intuitive rule that some struggle is good for you). By the end, you'll know why "feels worse, works better" is a signal to trust - and how to use it without overdoing it.

---

## Blocking versus interleaving: two ways to practice

Let's name the two strategies clearly.

**Blocking** (also called **massed practice**) means studying one type of problem or one category over and over before moving to the next. You do all your fractions, *then* all your decimals, *then* all your percentages. It feels smooth, organized, and confident.

**Interleaving** means shuffling different but related types together in the same session. A fraction problem, then a percentage problem, then a decimal, then back to a fraction - mixed up. It feels choppy, harder, and more error-prone in the moment.

Here's the contrast at a glance, imagining three problem types called A, B, and C:

```
BLOCKED practice:        A A A   B B B   C C C
                         └─────┘ └─────┘ └─────┘
                          all A   all B   all C
                        (smooth, feels easy)

INTERLEAVED practice:    A B C   B C A   C A B
                         └───────────────────┘
                         types mixed together
                        (choppy, feels hard)
```

| | **Blocked (AAABBBCCC)** | **Interleaved (ABCBCACAB)** |
|---|---|---|
| How it feels | Smooth, fluent, confident | Effortful, bumpy, error-prone |
| Errors during practice | Few | More |
| What you practice | Executing one method | *Choosing* which method fits |
| Short-term performance | Looks great | Looks worse |
| Long-term retention | Weaker | Stronger |
| Transfer to new problems | Poorer | Better |

Notice the pattern in that last column. Almost everything that makes blocking *feel* good in the moment is exactly what makes it *weaker* for the long run.

**In short:** Blocking drills one thing at a time and feels easy; interleaving mixes related things together, feels harder, and builds more lasting skill.

## The "feels worse, works better" paradox

Why would harder practice produce better learning? To understand this, we need to return to an idea from Chapter 3: the difference between **storage strength** and **retrieval strength**, a distinction developed by psychologists Robert and Elizabeth Bjork.

**Storage strength** is how deeply and durably something is wired into your long-term memory. **Retrieval strength** is how easily you can pull it up *right now*. These two are not the same, and that gap is the key to everything in this chapter.

When you block your practice, you keep the same method loaded and ready. Retrieval strength shoots up - the answer is right on the tip of your brain - so performance looks fantastic. But because you never have to *rebuild* the method or *decide* whether it's the right one, you're not adding much storage strength. You're coasting on a signal that will fade the moment you leave the room.

When you interleave, every new problem forces your brain to let go of the last method and reconstruct the right one from scratch. That reconstruction is effortful and you'll make mistakes. But the act of retrieving and rebuilding is precisely what deepens storage strength. The struggle isn't a side effect - it *is* the learning.

Think of it like the batting cage. Facing twenty fastballs, your swing gets grooved for that one pitch (high retrieval strength for fastballs). But mixing pitches forces you to *read and decide* every single time - and reading-and-deciding is the skill the real game demands.

This is why researchers call these methods **desirable difficulties** - a term coined by Robert Bjork in 1994. Desirable difficulties are learning conditions that *slow you down and increase your errors during practice* but produce stronger, more durable, and more transferable learning in the long run. The family includes spacing (Chapter 5), retrieval practice or self-testing (Chapter 4), interleaving, varying your study conditions, and even reducing feedback. What unites them is a signature feeling: they all feel *worse* while you do them.

**In short:** Interleaving feels hard because it lowers your in-the-moment retrieval strength, but that very struggle builds the long-term storage strength that lasts.

## The evidence: what the studies actually show

This isn't a nice theory with no proof behind it. The research is unusually clear.

**Rohrer and Taylor (2007)** taught college students to compute the volumes of four obscure solids - shapes like a wedge and a spheroid. One group practiced blocked (all of one shape, then all of the next); another practiced the same problems mixed together. *During* practice, the blocked group solved problems more accurately - blocking looked like the winner. But on a surprise test **one week later, the mixed group scored 63% versus just 20% for the blocked group.** That's roughly triple the score, from the exact same problems, arranged differently.

**Kornell and Bjork (2008)** ran a beautiful study with paintings. Participants studied six paintings by each of twelve artists, either blocked (all six of one artist together) or interleaved (shuffled across artists). Then they were shown *brand-new* paintings they'd never seen and asked to name the artist. Interleaving won clearly - mixing the artists helped people learn each painter's *style*. Here's the twist that matters most: afterward, about **78–80% of participants still believed blocking had helped them more.** Only around one in five correctly judged that interleaving worked better. People experienced the superior method and *still* trusted the worse one.

That gap between what works and what we *think* works has a name: a **metacognitive illusion** - a systematic error in judging our own learning. Blocking feels fluent, and we mistake fluency for progress.

The classroom evidence is just as strong. **Rohrer, Dedrick, and Stershic (2015)** followed 126 seventh-graders over about three months of real math class; interleaving beat blocking on both an immediate and a delayed surprise test - by *Scientific American*'s summary, about 25% higher after one day and roughly 76% higher after one month. Then **Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig, and Cheung (2020)** ran the gold-standard test: a randomized controlled trial of **787 students across 54 classes and 5 schools** over four months. One month later, on an unannounced test, **the interleaved group scored 61% versus 38% for blocked** - a large effect (d = 0.83) for a real-world school intervention.

The same paradox appears in physical skills. **Shea and Morgan (1979)** had people practice movement patterns either in a fixed order or randomly mixed. The random group performed *worse* during practice but *better* on later retention and transfer - an effect motor-learning researchers call **contextual interference** (the "interference" of mixing tasks hurts you now but helps you later). It's the same story as interleaving, discovered independently in the world of movement.

**In short:** Across math, art, classrooms, and sports, mixed practice reliably loses in the short term and wins on delayed tests - and learners almost always bet on the wrong horse.

## The honest limits: when interleaving helps (and when it doesn't)

Here's where a warm, honest teacher has to slow you down. It would be easy to leave this chapter thinking "interleaving always wins." It doesn't, and believing that will lead you astray.

The most careful summary we have is a **meta-analysis by Brunmair and Richter (2019)**, which pooled 59 studies and 238 separate effect sizes. Overall, interleaving gave a *moderate* benefit (g = 0.42). But the size depended enormously on *what* was being learned. For visual categories like paintings, the benefit was large (g = 0.67). For math, effects were small and ranged all the way from slightly negative to positive.

Why the difference? Interleaving works best when the categories you're mixing are **confusable** - similar enough that you might mistake one for another. Learning to tell Monet from Manet, or one bird species from a near-identical cousin, is exactly the kind of *discrimination* task interleaving supercharges. When you shuffle the artists, your brain is forced to notice what makes a Monet *a Monet* by contrast with everything that isn't. Staring at twenty Monets in a row never triggers that comparison.

So why do Rohrer's math studies show such huge gains when the meta-analysis found math effects small? The likely answer is *what* he interleaves. Rohrer carefully mixes **problem types** - problems that look similar but need different methods - so students must *decide which approach applies*. That decision is the whole point. Randomly mixing unrelated skills doesn't do this.

Two honest cautions follow. First, "**never block**" is wrong. You usually need some blocked practice *first*, just to learn how each method works at all. Interleaving is most valuable *after* you can execute each type - when the real challenge is choosing the right one. Second, interleaving offers little for material where items aren't confusable: rote vocabulary lists, dissimilar facts, unrelated topics. There, its evidence is weak.

**In short:** Interleaving is a discrimination tool - it shines when categories are similar and confusable, works modestly for math problem *types*, and does little for rote or unrelated material.

## The art gallery and the gym: two ways to picture it

Two analogies make this stick.

Picture a **junk drawer** versus a **gym circuit**. Blocking is like tidying a junk drawer - you group all the pens, all the batteries, all the rubber bands. Everything is neat, easy, and satisfying. But neatness isn't strength. Interleaving is like a gym circuit that jumps between squats, push-ups, and rows. Your muscles complain about the constant switching - and that varied, awkward strain is exactly what builds real strength. Comfort and growth are not the same thing.

Now picture an **art gallery**. If someone locked you in a room with twenty Monets and told you to learn "the Monet style," your eyes would glaze over; every painting would blur into the last. But if they hung a Monet next to a Manet next to a Renoir, you'd suddenly *see* the differences - the loose brushstrokes here, the flat light there. Your brain learns categories by contrast, and contrast requires putting different things side by side. That's all interleaving really is: arranging your practice so your brain is forced to compare.

**In short:** Blocking is a tidy drawer that feels good and builds little; interleaving is a gym circuit or a mixed gallery wall - uncomfortable, but that's where discrimination and strength come from.

## Common Mistakes

- **Trusting the feeling of ease.** Choosing blocking because it feels fluent is the exact illusion Kornell and Bjork documented - where 80% of people picked the worse method. *Fix:* Treat smoothness as a warning sign, not a success signal. If it feels a little hard, you're probably in the productive zone.
- **Interleaving too early.** If you shuffle problem types before you understand any of them, you get confusing noise instead of useful comparison. *Fix:* Block briefly to learn each type, *then* mix.
- **Interleaving unrelated junk.** Bouncing between history, then calculus, then Spanish isn't interleaving - it's just task-switching, and it won't give you the discrimination benefit. *Fix:* Mix *related, confusable* types within one subject.
- **Giving up when practice scores dip.** Your accuracy *will* drop during interleaved practice. Quitting because of that means abandoning the method right before it pays off. *Fix:* Judge the method by a delayed test days later, not by how practice felt.
- **Over-applying it.** Forcing interleaving onto rote vocabulary or unrelated facts, where the evidence is weak. *Fix:* Save it for similar categories and problem types that need telling apart.

---

## Try This

1. **Mix your problem sets.** Next time you have math or any problem-solving homework, don't do all of section A, then all of B. After you've learned each type once, deal them out shuffled - A, C, B, A, C - so every problem forces you to ask "which method does this one need?"
2. **Build a "mixed deck" review.** Take old and new material from the past few weeks and shuffle them into one review session. This interleaves *and* spaces at the same time (a two-for-one desirable difficulty from Chapter 5).
3. **Run a discrimination drill.** Pick two things you keep confusing - two grammar rules, two chemistry reactions, two artists, two chord shapes - and practice them *alternating*, back and forth, instead of one at a time. Notice how the contrast sharpens the difference.

## Key Takeaways

- **Blocking** (AAABBBCCC) drills one type at a time and feels fluent; **interleaving** (ABCBCACAB) mixes related types, feels hard, and builds more durable, transferable learning.
- The paradox - "feels worse, works better" - comes from Chapter 3's split: blocking boosts short-term *retrieval strength*, while interleaving's struggle builds long-term *storage strength*.
- **Desirable difficulties** (Robert Bjork, 1994) are conditions that slow you down and add errors during practice but improve lasting learning; interleaving is one of the family.
- **Rohrer and Taylor (2007):** on a test one week later, the mixed group scored **63% versus 20%** for the blocked group.
- **Kornell and Bjork (2008):** interleaving beat blocking for learning painters' styles, yet about **78–80% of learners still believed blocking helped more** - a **metacognitive illusion**.
- **Rohrer et al. (2020):** a randomized trial of 787 students found interleaving scored **61% versus 38%** (d = 0.83) one month later.
- Interleaving isn't universal: **Brunmair and Richter's (2019)** meta-analysis found a moderate overall effect (g = 0.42), large for confusable visual categories (g = 0.67) but small and variable for math.
- Interleaving is a **discrimination tool** - it works best when categories are similar and confusable, and does little for rote or unrelated material.
- Practical rule: **block briefly to learn each type, then interleave** related problem *types* so you practice *choosing* the method - and distrust the feeling of ease.
