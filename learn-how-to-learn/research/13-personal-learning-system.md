## Key concepts (plain definitions)

- **Note-taking vs note-making:** *Note-taking* = capturing what you hear/read (often verbatim). *Note-making* = actively reworking ideas into your own words and structure. The second is a *generative* task and drives deeper learning.
- **Cornell method** (Walter Pauk, Cornell, 1950s): page split into a narrow left **cue column**, wide right **notes column**, and a bottom **summary** band. You take notes on the right, later write recall questions/keywords on the left, and summarize at the bottom — building review and self-testing into the format.
- **Outlining:** hierarchical indented notes (headings → sub-points) that expose structure.
- **Mind maps** (popularized by **Tony Buzan**, 1970s): radial diagrams with a central topic, branching keywords, color and images.
- **Zettelkasten / "slip-box":** a web of small **atomic notes** (one idea each), uniquely IDed and **linked** to related notes, so knowledge compounds.
- **PKM (Personal Knowledge Management):** modern digital slip-boxes (Obsidian, Notion, Roam) using **atomic/evergreen notes** and bidirectional links.
- **SQ3R / PQ4R:** structured reading systems — Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review (add Preview/Reflect for PQ4R).
- **Illusion of competence (fluency illusion):** mistaking the *ease* of reading familiar material for actual learning.

## Studies & evidence (author, year, finding, numbers)

- **Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013),** *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* — reviewed 10 techniques. **High utility: practice testing and distributed (spaced) practice.** **Low utility: summarization, highlighting/underlining, and rereading** — the very strategies students use most.
- **Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014),** *Psychological Science*, "The Pen Is Mightier Than the Keyboard" — 3 studies (Ns ~67–151); laptop note-takers transcribed **verbatim** and scored **worse on conceptual questions** than longhand note-takers who reframed ideas. Mechanism = shallower processing, not the device itself.
- **Morehead, Dunlosky & Rawson (2019),** *Educational Psychology Review* — preregistered replication using the same TED talks **failed to reproduce** the headline effect; no significant comprehension difference, though laptops still produced **more words**. So the caveat is real: the *device* matters less than *whether you paraphrase*.
- **McDaniel, Howard & Einstein (2009),** *Psychological Science*, the **"3R" (Read–Recite–Review)** strategy — beat both rereading and note-taking on immediate and 1-week free recall, and matched note-taking on inference questions **while taking less time**. Direct evidence that reciting (retrieval) beats passive review.
- **Karpicke & Roediger (2008),** *Science* — retrieval practice far outperformed restudy on 1-week retention, yet students who restudied felt *more confident* — the classic demonstration of the illusion of competence.
- **Farrand, Hussain & Hennessey (2002),** *Medical Education* — medical students using **mind maps** on a 600-word passage recalled ~**10% more** at one week (an estimated **15%** if motivation were equalized). Modest, not magical.
- **Rohrer & Taylor (2007)** — **interleaved** math practice hurt practice-session accuracy but massively helped the delayed test (interleaved ~63% vs blocked ~20% one week later).
- **Robinson (1946),** *Effective Study* — origin of SQ3R, developed to train WWII army personnel fast.

## Myths / contested points (with the honest nuance)

- **"Handwriting beats laptops."** Overstated. The 2014 result **has not cleanly replicated (2019)**. Honest takeaway: paraphrasing beats verbatim transcription — longhand just makes verbatim harder. A laptop used for note-*making* is fine.
- **"Mind maps are dramatically superior."** Effects are small (~10%) and students often dislike them; they suit brainstorming/overview more than dense factual mastery.
- **"Luhmann's 90,000 cards prove the Zettelkasten is magic."** He was genuinely prolific (70+ books, ~400 articles), but this is a single anecdote, not controlled evidence. The mechanism that works — writing ideas in your own words and linking them — is elaboration, which *is* supported.
- **"Highlighting/rereading help."** They mostly build fluency-driven false confidence (Dunlosky 2013; Karpicke & Roediger 2008).

## Real-world examples & analogies

- **Luhmann's slip-box** as a "conversation partner": he'd pull a card, see its links, and let the box surface unexpected connections — like a personal search engine you built by thinking.
- **Analogy — pipes vs buckets:** verbatim copying is pouring water through you into a bucket; paraphrasing is building pipes that connect to what you already know.
- **Learning in public:** developers who blog what they learn or answer Stack Overflow questions report faster mastery — teaching forces retrieval and exposes gaps (the same engine behind the **Feynman technique**).
- **Analogy — the gym mirror:** rereading is admiring your form in the mirror; retrieval practice is actually lifting.

## Practical how-to points

- **Default to note-making:** close the source and write ideas in your own words; verbatim only for exact quotes/formulas.
- Use **Cornell** for lectures — the cue column becomes ready-made self-test questions.
- Convert key notes into **atomic, linked "evergreen" notes** (Obsidian/Notion) so knowledge accumulates.
- Read with **SQ3R/PQ4R**; the *Recite* step is where retrieval happens.
- Build a **weekly template** combining: focused deep-work blocks (Ch9) → active recall + spaced review of prior material (Ch3–5) → **interleave** subjects/problem types (Ch6) → elaborate/Feynman-teach one concept (Ch7–8) → protect **sleep + exercise** (Ch11) → weekly self-regulation review (Ch12).
- **Calibrate:** end each session with a closed-book brain-dump; the gap between what you *feel* you know and what you can *produce* is your true progress signal.
- Run a **learning project** and share it publicly.

## Common mistakes learners make on this topic

- **Transcribing verbatim** instead of reframing.
- **Highlighting/rereading** and mistaking fluency for mastery.
- **Collector's fallacy** — hoarding notes/PDFs without processing or linking them (a dead archive, not a slip-box).
- **Fetishizing tools** (endless Obsidian setup) instead of doing the thinking.
- **Blocking one topic at a time** and skipping interleaving/spacing.
- **Never self-testing**, so miscalibration goes undetected until the exam.
