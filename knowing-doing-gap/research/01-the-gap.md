# Chapter 1 Research Notes: The Gap Between Knowing and Doing

Research compiled July 2026. Every claim attributed. Flags note where evidence is contested.

## 1. The core phenomenon: the intention-behavior gap

**The headline numbers (Sheeran, 2002).** Paschal Sheeran's landmark review "Intention–Behavior Relations: A Conceptual and Empirical Review" (European Review of Social Psychology, 2002) was a meta-analysis of 10 meta-analyses covering **422 studies and 82,107 participants**. The sample-weighted average correlation between intention and behavior was **r = .53**, meaning intentions account for only about **28% of the variance in behavior**. Put bluntly: even in the best case, roughly three-quarters of what people actually do is NOT explained by what they intend to do.
Sources: [Sheeran 2002 via ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230821783_Intention-Behavior_Relations_A_Conceptual_and_Empirical_Review); [Sheeran & Webb 2016](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/spc3.12265).

**Correlations exaggerate causation.** Webb & Sheeran (2006), "Does Changing Behavioral Intentions Engender Behavior Change? A Meta-Analysis of the Experimental Evidence" (Psychological Bulletin), analyzed **47 experiments** that successfully changed people's intentions and then measured behavior. Result: a **medium-to-large change in intention (d = 0.66) produced only a small-to-medium change in behavior (d = 0.36)**. Even when you succeed at making someone genuinely intend to act, you get roughly half the behavioral payoff.
Sources: [PDF of the paper](http://www.communicationcache.com/uploads/1/0/8/8/10887248/does_changing_behavioral_intentions_engender_behavior_change-_a_meta-analysis_of_the_experimental_evidence.pdf); [Manchester research record](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/does-changing-behavioral-intentions-engender-behavior-change-a-me/).

**The "inclined abstainers" finding.** Sheeran's 2×2 decomposition (intenders vs. non-intenders × actors vs. abstainers) shows the gap is almost entirely driven by one cell: people who intend to act but don't. In Sheeran & Webb (2016, Social and Personality Psychology Compass), across studies of exercise, condom use, and cancer screening, **the median proportion of positive intenders who failed to act was 47%**, while only **7%** of people with negative intentions acted anyway ("disinclined actors"). So the problem is not people intending the wrong things — it is intenders failing to follow through. Roughly **half of all good intentions die between decision and deed**.
Sources: [Sheeran & Webb 2016, Wiley](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/spc3.12265); [open-access PDF](https://content.workplacegivingaustralia.org.au/app/uploads/2020/10/Intention-Behaviour-Gap-Full-Article-SheeranWebb2016.pdf).

**Exercise-specific gap (Rhodes & de Bruijn, 2013).** "How big is the physical activity intention–behaviour gap?" (British Journal of Health Psychology) meta-analyzed studies using the action control framework and found **46% of people who intended to exercise failed to do so**. Almost half of exercise "intenders" are inclined abstainers.
Source: [Rhodes & de Bruijn 2013](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bjhp.12032).

**Why intentions fail (Sheeran & Webb 2016 taxonomy):** failures of *initiation* (forgetting, missing the moment, letting other priorities crowd in), failures of *persistence* (flagging motivation, obstacles), and problems with the intention itself (weak, ambivalent, or identity-inconsistent intentions decay fastest). Habits, impulses, and environmental cues override deliberate plans.

## 2. The Theory of Planned Behavior and its limits

**The theory (Ajzen, 1991).** Icek Ajzen's Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) holds that behavior follows from intention, which follows from (a) attitudes toward the behavior, (b) subjective norms, and (c) perceived behavioral control. It became the single most-cited model in health/social psychology — precisely because it is a formalization of the intuitive "if people believe and intend, they will do" view.

**The limits.** Meta-analytic work (e.g., McEachan et al., 2011, Health Psychology Review, ~200+ TPB health-behavior tests) consistently shows TPB predicts intention well (~40–45% of variance) but actual behavior poorly (~19–25% of variance) — the model is much better at explaining what people *mean to do* than what they *do*.

**The "retire the theory" fight.** Sniehotta, Presseau & Araújo-Soares (2014), "Time to retire the theory of planned behaviour" (Health Psychology Review), argued TPB should be put out to pasture: it is **static** (ignores feedback from behavior to cognition), **overly rational** (excludes emotion, habit, impulse, non-conscious processes), and empirically weak for behavior prediction — the intention-behavior gap is its Achilles heel. Ajzen (2015) fired back that the theory "is alive and well, and not ready to retire," blaming poor applications and interventions rather than the theory. The honest takeaway for the book: nobody disputes that **intention is the endpoint TPB explains best, and behavior is where it thins out** — which is exactly the knowing-doing gap in academic form.
Sources: [Sniehotta et al. 2014, Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17437199.2013.869710); [Ajzen 2015 commentary PDF](https://people.umass.edu/aizen/pubs/sniehotta.pdf); [BPS overview](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/planned-behaviour-stagnation-or-evolution).

## 3. Organizations: Pfeffer & Sutton's "The Knowing-Doing Gap" (2000)

Stanford professors Jeffrey Pfeffer and Robert Sutton, *The Knowing-Doing Gap: How Smart Companies Turn Knowledge into Action* (Harvard Business School Press, 2000). Core thesis: **organizational performance depends far more on implementing what is already known than on acquiring new knowledge**. Companies pay billions for training, consultants, and knowledge-management systems, yet the "best practices" are usually already known inside the building — they just aren't acted on.

**The Smart-Talk Trap** (also a 1999 Harvard Business Review article): in most organizations, sounding smart — critique, planning, presentations, complex talk — is rewarded as if it were action. Talk substitutes for action because talk is immediate, visible, and evaluable, while doing is slow and risky. Negative, critical talk is especially prestigious ("smart people talk about problems"), which paralyzes doing.

**Five barriers that widen the gap** (each gets a chapter): (1) when talk substitutes for action; (2) when memory ("how it's always been done") substitutes for thinking; (3) when **fear** prevents acting on knowledge; (4) when **measurement obstructs good judgment** (measuring what's easy, not what matters); (5) when **internal competition turns friends into enemies**. Firms that close the gap drive out fear, abolish destructive internal contests, measure what matters, and promote leaders who understand the actual work.

**Vivid case: GM, Saturn and NUMMI.** General Motors created the NUMMI joint venture with Toyota (1984, Fremont, CA) and the Saturn division explicitly to learn Toyota's production system. It worked — the plants learned it — and then **GM failed for years to transfer that knowledge to its other plants**. The knowledge sat inside the company, documented and demonstrated, while the rest of GM kept doing what it had always done. (Also memorable: every fast-food company "knows" the drivers of franchise performance; execution varies wildly anyway.)
Sources: [Pfeffer's book page](https://jeffreypfeffer.com/books/the-knowing-doing-gap/); [Stanford GSB Insights](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/knowing-doing-gap); [Learning Omnivores summary](https://learningomnivores.com/what-were-reading/the-knowing-doing-gap/).

## 4. Health behavior: what people know vs. what they do

- **Exercise.** Virtually everyone "knows" exercise matters, yet CDC/NCHS data: only **47.2% of U.S. adults met the aerobic activity guideline (2024)** and only **22.5% met both aerobic + muscle-strengthening guidelines (2022)**; just **13.9% of adults 65+** meet both. Sources: [AHA News on CDC report](https://www.aha.org/news/headline/2026-04-07-cdc-finds-nearly-half-us-adults-get-recommended-amount-physical-activity); [CDC QuickStats 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11166256/); [ABC News](https://abcnews.go.com/Health/half-us-adults-meeting-physical-activity-guidelines-cdc/story?id=96689335).
- **Smoking (CDC MMWR, 2022 data).** **67.7% of adult smokers want to quit; 53.3% made a quit attempt in the past year; only 8.8% actually succeeded.** Fewer than 40% of quit attempters used evidence-based treatment. The knowledge is universal (everyone knows smoking kills); the annual success rate is under 1 in 10. Sources: [CDC MMWR 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/wr/mm7329a1.htm); [CDC Fast Facts](https://www.cdc.gov/tobacco/php/data-statistics/smoking-cessation/index.html); [Truth Initiative](https://truthinitiative.org/research-resources/quitting-smoking-vaping/quitting-tobacco-facts-and-stats).
- **Medication adherence.** WHO's seminal 2003 adherence report: **only ~50% of patients with chronic diseases in developed countries take medication as prescribed** — people with diagnosed illnesses, holding prescriptions, under doctor's orders. Poor adherence is linked to ~30% of hospital admissions in some analyses. Sources: [Frontiers in Pharmacology 2024 retrospective](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2024.1444012/full); [PMC overview](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4619779/).
- **"Change or Die" — cardiac patients.** Alan Deutschman (Fast Company, 2005; book 2007), drawing on Dr. Edward Miller (then dean, Johns Hopkins Medicine): of patients who undergo coronary bypass surgery, **~90% have not changed their lifestyle two years later** — even facing death. Contrast: Dean Ornish's intensive lifestyle program (support groups, dieticians, psychologists, yoga/meditation instructors) got **77% of patients still adhering after three years**, with a 91% drop in chest-pain frequency in the first month. Lesson: information identical; support structure different; outcomes inverted. Sources: [Fast Company article PDF](https://www.abettercity.org/docs/sustainability/Fast%20Company,%20Change%20or%20Die.pdf); [Milton Academy reprint](https://www.milton.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/change_or_die.pdf).
- **Hand hygiene — the Semmelweis story continued.** Ignaz Semmelweis showed in the 1840s that handwashing slashed maternal mortality; he was ridiculed and ignored (a knowing-doing gap that killed him and thousands of mothers). Today, ~180 years later, with the science beyond dispute, **average hand-hygiene compliance among healthcare workers is still only ~40%** (WHO; CDC reports U.S. average under 40%), and improper hand hygiene contributes to a large share of hospital-acquired infections. Health professionals — the people who know most — still don't reliably do the single cheapest life-saving behavior. Sources: [WHO 2023 research agenda](https://www.who.int/news/item/12-05-2023-first-ever-who-research-agenda-on-hand-hygiene-in-health-care-to-improve-quality-and-safety-of-care); [Healthcare Hygiene magazine](https://www.healthcarehygienemagazine.com/hand-hygiene/); [PMC studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3862659/).
- **New Year's resolutions (Norcross, Mrykalo & Blagys, 2002, Journal of Clinical Psychology).** ~75% of resolvers still succeeding after one week; **40–46% at six months**. Notable nuance: resolvers did far better than non-resolvers with the same goals (**46% vs. 4%** success at 6 months) — deciding helps, it just isn't sufficient. Sources: [Norcross 2002, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jclp.1151); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2008/12/26/98738130/how-to-make-new-years-resolutions-stick).
- **Money.** Federal Reserve SHED survey (2022): **37% of U.S. adults could not cover a $400 emergency expense with cash**; Empower research: **21% have no emergency savings at all**, while 62% say dedicated emergency savings is a priority — a stated-priority/actual-behavior gap in one dataset. Sources: [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2023-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2022-expenses.htm); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2023/05/23/inflation-economy-consumer-finances-americans-cant-cover-emergency-expense-federal-reserve/); [Empower](https://www.empower.com/the-currency/money/safety-net-emergency-savings-research). (Caveat for the book: some of this gap is capacity/income, not just follow-through — be fair.)

## 5. Why "more information" is almost never the fix

- **The information deficit model is discredited.** The 1970s assumption that giving people facts changes attitudes and behavior has repeatedly failed empirically in science communication and health education; knowledge is a weak, often near-zero predictor once attitudes, identity, habits, and environment are accounted for. Sources: [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_deficit_model); [IntechOpen chapter on the health knowledge-action gap](https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/1215210).
- **Financial education: the killer stat.** Fernandes, Lynch & Netemeyer (2014, Management Science) meta-analyzed **201 studies (168 papers)**: interventions to improve financial literacy explain **just 0.1% of the variance in downstream financial behavior**, with weaker effects in low-income samples, and effects **decay to negligible within ~20 months** even after many hours of instruction. Sources: [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2333898); [Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1849). (Honesty flag: later work, e.g., Kaiser et al. 2021 (Journal of Financial Economics), using newer RCTs, finds modest positive effects of financial education on behavior — the field has softened from "zero" to "small"; the direction of the lesson stands: information alone moves behavior a little at best.)
- **Habits run much of life without consulting your intentions.** Wendy Wood's experience-sampling research (Wood, Quinn & Kashy, 2002; Wood's *Good Habits, Bad Habits*, 2019): **~43% of everyday actions are performed almost daily in the same context, largely while thinking about something else**. Habitual behavior is cued by context, not by knowledge or intention — which is why lectures don't rewire commutes, snacking, or phone-checking. Sources: [Wood, Quinn & Kashy 2002 PDF](https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/Wood.Quinn_.Kashy_.2002_Habits_in_everyday_life.pdf); [APA interview](https://www.apa.org/news/podcasts/speaking-of-psychology/behavioral-habits).
- Structural logic: information changes *attitudes and intentions* (upstream), but the documented bottleneck is *between intention and action* (downstream) — so adding information pours more water into a funnel that's clogged below the waterline. (Webb & Sheeran 2006 is the direct evidence: even successfully changed intentions yield d = 0.36 behavior change.)

## 6. Why advice-giving fails

- **Egocentric advice discounting.** Yaniv & Kleinberger (2000, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes) and the Bonaccio & Dalal (2006) review of judge–advisor studies: people systematically **weight advice from others at less than half the weight of their own opinion**, even when the advisor is objectively better informed. Discounting is worst for **unsolicited advice**, which is experienced as intrusion or implied criticism of competence (classic psychological-reactance territory: a perceived threat to autonomy triggers pushback). Sources: [Yaniv & Kleinberger 2000](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v83y2000i2p260-281.html); [Bonaccio & Dalal 2006 review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222276737_Advice_taking_and_decision-making_An_integrative_literature_review_and_implications_for_the_organizational_sciences); [Judge–advisor system, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judge%E2%80%93advisor_system).
- **The advice flip: givers gain, receivers don't.** Eskreis-Winkler, Fishbach & Duckworth (2018, Psychological Science; N = 2,274): struggling people were **more motivated by giving advice than by receiving it** — replicated across saving money, temper control, weight loss, and job seeking; ~72% of participants said giving beat receiving. In a randomized double-blind field experiment (Eskreis-Winkler et al., 2019, PNAS, ~2,000 students), middle-schoolers who *gave* motivational advice to younger students spent more time on homework the following month than those who *received* expert advice. Interpretation: strugglers usually already know what to do; advice-receiving signals incompetence, while advice-giving restores confidence and commitment. Sources: [Psychological Science paper](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797618795472); [PNAS field experiment](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1908779116); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/giving-advice-more-motivating-receiving-it).
- Book-usable line of argument: advice fails not because it's wrong but because (a) it's redundant — the receiver usually already knows; (b) it's discounted — we trust our own judgment ~2x more; (c) it's threatening — unsolicited advice reads as "you're not competent," triggering reactance.

## 7. Knowledge vs. execution vs. mastery

- **Declarative vs. procedural knowledge** (John Anderson's ACT-R cognitive architecture, 1982+): *knowing that* (facts, stored propositions) is a different memory system from *knowing how* (compiled procedures built only through practice). Reading about swimming produces declarative knowledge; only swimming produces procedural knowledge. This is the cognitive-science backbone of the chapter's knowledge/execution distinction.
- **Mastery = execution compounded by deliberate practice.** Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993) proposed that expert performance is "largely accounted for" by accumulated deliberate practice; popularized (and distorted) by Gladwell's "10,000-hour rule" (2008) — Ericsson himself objected that 10,000 was an average at age 20 for violinists, not a magic threshold, and that *deliberate* (coached, feedback-rich, at-the-edge) practice is what matters, not hours.
- **Honesty flag — the dispute:** Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014, Psychological Science) meta-analyzed 88 studies: deliberate practice explained only **12% of performance variance overall** — **26% for games, 21% music, 18% sports, 4% education, <1% professions**. Ericsson's camp countered that most included studies didn't measure deliberate practice as originally defined. Current consensus: practice is necessary but far from sufficient; genes, starting age, opportunity, and quality of practice all matter. For the book: use the debate itself — even the "just do the work" literature shows *doing* is the differentiator that knowledge never is, while avoiding the debunked 10,000-hours-guarantees-mastery framing. Sources: [Macnamara et al. 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797614535810); [Frontiers defense of Ericsson's definition](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full).
- Suggested three-level frame for the chapter: **Knowledge** (can state it) → **Execution** (can do it, at least once, under normal conditions) → **Mastery** (does it reliably, adaptively, under pressure — built via feedback loops and reps). The gap studied by Sheeran sits between levels 1 and 2; the gap studied by Ericsson/Macnamara sits between 2 and 3.

## 8. Related replication-crisis context (for honest footnotes)

- **Ego depletion** (Baumeister's willpower-as-muscle): large registered replications (Hagger et al., 2016, 23 labs) found effects near zero; don't build "the gap exists because willpower is a depletable tank" arguments on it.
- **Marshmallow test**: Watts, Duncan & Quan (2018) conceptual replication with N≈900 found the delay-outcome correlation shrinks by ~two-thirds after controlling for family background; self-control at 4 is not destiny.
- **Grit** (Duckworth): Credé et al. (2017) meta-analysis found grit correlates highly with conscientiousness and adds little incremental prediction; treat as repackaged persistence, not a new key.
- These matter for Chapter 1 only as guardrails: the intention-behavior gap itself (Sheeran, Webb, Rhodes) rests on large multi-meta-analytic foundations and is NOT among the contested findings.

## 9. Story bank for the chapter

1. **Semmelweis (1847)** — the doctor who proved handwashing saved mothers' lives, was ignored by peers who *knew* his data, and died in an asylum; hospitals today still hover at ~40% compliance. Knowing-doing gap measured in lives, across two centuries.
2. **The bypass patient (Deutschman/Miller)** — told "change or die," 9 in 10 don't change; Ornish's community-support program flips it to ~77% adherence. The fix wasn't more information — patients already had the scariest information possible.
3. **GM and NUMMI** — GM co-owned the answer (Toyota's system, running in its own Fremont plant) for two decades and couldn't spread it internally. Companies don't fail from ignorance; they fail from non-implementation (Pfeffer & Sutton).
4. **The smart-talk meeting** — Pfeffer & Sutton's observation that organizations promote people for sounding brilliant about problems rather than fixing them; critique feels like progress.
5. **The advice flip experiment** — middle-schoolers assigned to *give* advice did more homework than kids receiving expert advice (Eskreis-Winkler 2018/2019); perfect illustration that the missing ingredient is motivation/confidence, not information.
6. **Analogy stock:** a map is not the territory walked; owning a cookbook is not cooking; 28% variance = "intentions buy you a lottery ticket, not the prize"; the funnel clogged below the waterline (info pours in at the top, the blockage is downstream).

## 10. One-paragraph synthesis for the chapter

Across 82,000+ participants, intentions explain barely a quarter of behavior (Sheeran 2002); experimentally doubling intention buys only half as much action (Webb & Sheeran 2006); about half of all sincere intenders — 47% median — simply fail to act (Sheeran & Webb 2016; Rhodes & de Bruijn 2013). The same signature appears everywhere: 68% of smokers want to quit, 9% succeed in a year (CDC 2022); half of chronic patients don't take prescribed medication (WHO 2003); 90% of bypass patients don't change their lifestyles (Deutschman 2005); organizations sit on documented best practices for decades (Pfeffer & Sutton 2000). Adding information barely moves anything — financial education explains ~0.1% of financial behavior (Fernandes et al. 2014) — because ~43% of daily behavior is context-cued habit (Wood 2002) and because advice is discounted, resisted, and usually redundant (Bonaccio & Dalal 2006; Eskreis-Winkler 2018). The gap is not a knowledge problem; it is an execution problem, and execution is a different skill with different machinery.
