A doctor stands in front of a room full of colleagues at a medical school in the 1990s. His slide has fourteen bullet points. He reads all fourteen, word for word, in a flat voice. Every single person in that room can read faster than he can talk - so by bullet point three, nobody is listening anymore. They're just watching him catch up to text they already finished.

That scene has a name: **death by PowerPoint**. It isn't a joke about boring meetings. It's a measurable, reproducible effect - putting the same words on a screen and in your mouth at the same time makes an audience understand *less*, not more. Once you know why, you can never build a slide the old way again.

## Why this matters

Nearly every important moment in your career runs through a screen at some point - the pitch, [the board update](/blog/communication-and-influence/27-executive-presence-communicating-up), the project review, the sales call. If your slides fight you instead of helping you, you're sabotaging your own best material before you've said a word.

The good news is that fixing this doesn't require design talent. It requires understanding one thing about how brains process information, and then following a small set of mechanical rules. That's what the rest of this is about.

## A slide is not a document

Here's the mental shift that fixes almost everything else: **a slide and a document have completely different jobs, and most business slides try to do both at once.**

A document is read alone, at the reader's own pace, with every detail spelled out because nobody is there to explain it. A slide is glanced at for a few seconds while a live person talks. Try to make one file do both jobs and you get what's sometimes called a **slideument** - too text-heavy to glance at, too sparse to stand alone as a document. It fails at both.

Think of your slide like a highway billboard, not a magazine page. A billboard carries one big idea, readable in two seconds at 60 miles an hour. A magazine page can hold paragraphs because the reader controls the pace. Your slide is the billboard. Your mouth is the magazine.

**Example:** A product manager is asked to "send the deck" before a board meeting. Instead of stuffing her slides with extra paragraphs so they read well on their own, she keeps the slides lean and attaches a separate two-page brief with the same numbers and context. In the room, she presents the lean slides and talks. Anyone who wants the details already has the brief.

## The real reason text-heavy slides backfire

"Death by PowerPoint" isn't just an aesthetic complaint - it has a clean psychological explanation, and it comes down to how much your brain can hold at once.

**[Cognitive load](/blog/ai-learning-platform/05-cognitive-load-theory-why-too-much-at-once-fails)** is the total mental effort your working memory is juggling right now. Working memory is small - think of it as a desk with room for only a few papers before things start sliding off.

Researcher John Sweller identified something called the **redundancy effect**: people learn *less*, not more, when they have to process the same information twice at the same time - for instance, reading identical words on a slide while also hearing you say those same words out loud. This sounds backwards. Shouldn't seeing and hearing the same thing reinforce it?

The answer is no, because your brain processes information through two channels - visual and verbal - and each one has limited room. When both channels carry the exact same text, they don't reinforce each other; they compete, and the effort of reconciling two identical streams steals bandwidth from actually understanding the point.

```
   TWO CHANNELS, LIMITED CAPACITY

   EYES  ---> [ visual channel ]  ---\
                                      >---> WORKING MEMORY (small!)
   EARS  ---> [ verbal channel ] ---/

   Text-heavy slide + speaker reading it aloud
   = both channels carrying the SAME words
   = channels compete instead of working together
   = audience understands LESS, not more
```

There's also a rough ceiling on visual complexity: once a slide holds much more than about six distinct chunks of information, people stop absorbing it at a glance and start consciously counting and sorting - which burns even more mental energy.

**Common mistake:** writing your full spoken script as bullet points "so I don't forget what to say." You'll either read it aloud (boring, redundant) or ignore it (so why is it there?). Put your notes in the presenter-notes field or on an index card, not on the screen the audience is staring at.

Before adding anything to a slide, ask yourself: does this help people understand the point faster, or is it just filling space? If it's not pulling weight, cut it. White space isn't a failure - it's what lets the one important thing stand out.

## The four rules that fix almost every bad slide

Once you accept that a slide's job is to support you, not narrate for you, slide design gets almost mechanical.

### One idea per slide

Each slide should make exactly one point. If you want to cover two ideas, that's a sign you need two slides - slides are free. Nobody has ever complained "there were too many slides" when each one was clear. They complain when each slide was overloaded.

### Less text

Investor Guy Kawasaki's well-known **10/20/30 rule**: aim for around 10 slides, speak for no more than 20 minutes, and never use a font smaller than 30 points. The font rule is the real enforcer - if your text has to shrink below 30 points to fit, you simply have too much text.

Presentation design author Garr Reynolds, known for his book *Presentation Zen*, makes a related point: audiences didn't come to read, they came to hear you. Anyone can silently read your bullet points faster than you can say them, which makes you - the actual speaker - redundant. Push toward slides with very little text, and make what remains large and simple, closer to a caption than a paragraph.

### Bigger visuals

A relevant photo, diagram, or simple chart usually lands faster than a paragraph, and it doesn't compete with your voice the way text does - your brain doesn't have to "read" a picture the way it reads words. Use one large, high-quality visual instead of four small, cluttered ones.

### High contrast

Text has to be readable from the back row, on a mediocre projector, in a lit room. Dark text on a light background (or the reverse), generous spacing, and no busy background behind the words - non-negotiable. A gorgeous slide nobody in row twelve can read has failed at its one job.

| Weak slide habit | What to do instead |
| --- | --- |
| Six bullet points, sub-bullets, and a clip-art icon | One short headline sentence and one supporting image or chart |
| 12-point font to cram in more text | 30+ point font, forcing you to cut and simplify |
| Pastel text on a busy photo background | Solid background, high-contrast text color |
| Reading the slide word-for-word aloud | Slide shows the point; your voice adds the explanation and story |

**Example:** A sales lead presenting quarterly results skips the slide titled "Q3 Performance" with eight bullets of numbers. Instead she shows one slide with a single sentence - "Renewals grew 34% after we simplified onboarding" - and one clean bar chart underneath. She talks through the why and what's-next out loud, which the slide never needed to hold.

This combination has a name: **assertion-evidence design**. The headline states your conclusion outright, as a full sentence - that's the assertion. The visual underneath proves it - that's the evidence. There's no bullet list, because the sentence already told the room what to think, and the chart just backs it up.

## Charts: making numbers land in three seconds

Numbers are often the most important part of a business presentation, and they're also where slides go wrong most - usually because someone pastes a giant spreadsheet screenshot straight into the deck. A chart has the same job as a slide: make one point land fast. Turning a spreadsheet into that one clean visual is the design half of [data storytelling](/blog/communication-and-influence/18-business-and-data-storytelling) - the other half is picking the right chart for your point.

### Pick the chart that fits your point

- **Bar chart** - best for comparing separate categories (sales by region).
- **Line chart** - best for [showing a trend over time](/blog/clear-thinking-and-expression/22-spotting-patterns-trends-anomalies-signals) (revenue by month).
- **Pie or donut chart** - only for showing how a few parts (2-4) make up a whole. It's one of the most overused and misread chart types, because the human eye is genuinely bad at comparing angles and curved slices.
- **Scatter plot** - best for showing the relationship between two numeric measures (ad spend vs. signups).

Choose the chart based on the sentence you want the audience to walk away with. "We grew steadily every quarter" calls for a line chart, which shows that shape instantly. "The Northeast dramatically outperformed everyone" calls for a bar chart, which makes that one bar jump out. Pick the wrong chart type and you force the audience to hunt for a story you already know - and in a live room, they won't do that work.

### Cut the decoration

Design theorist Edward Tufte coined the term **chartjunk** for decorative chart elements that add zero information - 3D effects, heavy gridlines, drop shadows, busy background colors. He framed it around the **data-ink ratio**: the share of a chart's visual "ink" that's actually showing data versus spent on decoration. Higher is cleaner.

In practice: strip 3D off every bar and pie chart (3D distorts how the eye judges size), remove gridlines you don't need, drop the legend if you can label data directly, and cut any series that isn't relevant to today's point. There's nuance here - some research on "embellished" charts found tasteful flourishes can help people remember a chart later. The rule isn't zero decoration ever; it's nothing that doesn't serve the message.

### Point the eye at the takeaway

Once the clutter's gone, actively direct attention. Make every bar gray except the one you're talking about, colored boldly. Add a one-line callout that states the takeaway in words ("Northeast: 2x the next region") instead of leaving people to infer it from axis labels.

A cluttered, undirected chart is like handing someone a full box of puzzle pieces and asking them to find the cat picture while you keep talking. A well-designed chart hands them the finished picture with the cat circled in red. Same data, radically different amount of work for your audience.

**Common mistake:** pasting a raw spreadsheet or dashboard screenshot into a slide "because the data's already there." It's unreadable from six feet away and buries your one important number inside forty others. Rebuild the single number or trend that matters as a clean chart instead.

## You are the presentation, not the screen

Even with perfect slides, it's easy to accidentally make the screen the star instead of yourself. In a dark room with a bright screen, eyes are pulled toward light and movement - a giant glowing slide will always compete with you unless you actively manage it.

A few habits keep you at the center:

1. **Stand where you can be seen**, not hidden behind a lectern or laptop. Your [face and gestures](/blog/communication-and-influence/12-body-language-nonverbal-communication) carry meaning a slide can't.
2. **Look at the audience, not the screen.** Presenters who turn their back to talk to their own slide lose the room's attention and lose vocal energy - they're talking away from the microphone and the people.
3. **Advance slides on your terms.** Reveal a slide, let it land for a second, then talk. Don't let a new slide interrupt your own sentence.
4. **Use blackouts on purpose.** Most tools let you blank the screen entirely (the "B" key in PowerPoint). When you want the room's full attention on a story or a hard question, black out the slide. Suddenly there's nothing to look at but you.

**Example:** A consultant pitching a turnaround plan reaches the emotional core - the human cost of the client's process failures. She blacks out the screen, steps toward the table, and tells a 30-second story about a real customer affected by the problem. Only after the story lands does she bring the next slide back up to show the fix.

This is the same instinct behind [structuring a presentation people actually remember](/blog/communication-and-influence/10-how-to-structure-a-presentation) - the structure and the delivery matter more than any individual slide ever will.

## What to do when the slides disappear

Sooner or later the projector fails, the file won't open, or you're in a room too small or informal for a screen at all. Presenters who built their entire talk as a slideument - a script glued to bullet points - fall apart here, because they've never practiced separating the talk from the slides. Presenters who've internalized "the talk is what I say, the slides are just a bonus" barely notice the difference.

The fix: know your talk's structure independent of any slide. Before an important talk, be able to state out loud, from memory, in one sentence each - your opening hook, your three main points, and your closing ask. If you can do that without looking at anything, you can [deliver the whole talk with zero slides](/blog/communication-and-influence/14-thinking-on-your-feet-impromptu-speaking). You'll lose the charts and images, not your argument.

```
  YOUR TALK'S BACKBONE (should survive with zero slides)

  [ Hook ]  -->  [ Point 1 ] -> [ Point 2 ] -> [ Point 3 ]  -->  [ Ask / Close ]

  Slides only ever decorate points along this line.
  If the screen dies, the line still holds.
```

Verbally signal the structure you'd normally show visually ("There are three reasons this matters - first... second... third..."), use your hands to represent comparisons you'd normally chart, and if a number matters, [state it slowly and repeat it](/blog/communication-and-influence/11-vocal-delivery-voice-pace-pause) rather than trying to describe an entire chart's shape in words.

Always bring a low-tech backup - a one-page printed handout or simply a whiteboard marker. And if the tech fails, a brief, calm acknowledgment ("Looks like we've lost the projector - let me just talk you through this") followed by confidently continuing reads as competence. Long apologies and visible panic read as a much bigger problem than the actual broken cable - the same poise that helps with [managing nerves under pressure](/blog/communication-and-influence/09-how-to-overcome-stage-fright) is exactly what carries you through a tech failure.

## Common misconceptions

- **"More slides means a more thorough presentation."** More slides just mean more chances to overload the room. A tightly built 8-slide talk almost always lands harder than a padded 30-slide one.
- **"If I put it on the slide, they'll remember it."** Text-heavy slides read alongside your voice can make people remember *less* because of the redundancy effect. What sticks is the story and the single sharp visual, not the wall of bullets.
- **"A pie chart is a safe, neutral way to show data."** It's actually one of the hardest chart types to read accurately, because eyes are bad at comparing angles. Save it for two to four slices, and reach for a bar chart otherwise.
- **"Decoration makes a chart look more professional."** Chartjunk - 3D effects, heavy gridlines, drop shadows - usually makes a chart harder to read, not more credible. Clean and legible reads as more professional than busy.

## How to use this

1. **Split the deck from the document.** If people need details afterward, write a separate one- or two-page brief. Keep the slides sparse.
2. **Run the six-object check.** Before you finalize a slide, count the distinct chunks of information on it. More than about six, and it needs editing.
3. **Rewrite every topic headline as a full sentence.** Change "Q3 Results" into "Renewals grew 34% after we simplified onboarding." If you can't write that sentence, you don't yet know what the slide is for.
4. **Match your chart type to your point.** Bars for comparison, lines for trends, pie only for a few parts of a whole.
5. **Strip chartjunk, then add one visual cue.** Remove 3D, gridlines, and redundant legends. Then color the one bar or line that matters and label it in plain words.
6. **Rehearse where you'll stand and one blackout moment.** Decide in advance where you'll be visible, and plan to black out the screen right before your most important point.
7. **Memorize your talk's backbone.** Hook, three points, close - in one sentence each, so a dead projector never kills your talk.

## Conclusion

The single idea to keep: your slides exist to help the room understand you faster, not to carry the whole conversation for you. Every rule here - one idea per slide, big text, clean charts, blackouts, memorizing your backbone - is just that principle applied to a different part of the deck.

Get this right and something interesting happens: the room starts remembering *you* instead of your slides. And that's the real asset, because the same instinct that makes a slide land - stating your point as one clear sentence before you prove it - is exactly what makes any [structured presentation memorable](/blog/communication-and-influence/10-how-to-structure-a-presentation) long after the screen goes dark.
