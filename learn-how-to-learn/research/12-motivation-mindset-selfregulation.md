## Key concepts (plain definitions)

**Self-regulated learning (SRL)** - a learner steering their own studying through a repeating loop. Barry Zimmerman's cyclical model (social-cognitive theory) has three phases: **forethought** (task analysis, goal-setting, planning, motivational beliefs), **performance** (self-control strategies + self-observation/self-monitoring while working), and **self-reflection** (self-evaluation, causal attributions for success/failure, self-reactions that feed the next cycle).

**Metacognitive monitoring & control** - monitoring is judging what you know (e.g. a "judgment of learning," JOL - predicting whether you'll recall something later); control is what you do about it (deciding what to restudy). Bad monitoring produces bad control.

**Self-Determination Theory (SDT, Deci & Ryan)** - motivation quality depends on three psychological needs: **autonomy** (acting from your own values), **competence** (feeling effective), **relatedness** (feeling connected). Meeting them fuels *intrinsic* motivation; controlling them undermines it.

**Overjustification effect** - paying someone for something they already enjoy can shift their perceived reason to the reward, reducing intrinsic interest.

**Growth vs fixed mindset (Dweck)** - believing ability grows with effort/strategy vs believing it's fixed.

**Goal-setting theory (Locke & Latham)** - specific, difficult goals beat vague "do your best" goals.

**Implementation intentions (Gollwitzer)** - pre-committed "if [situation], then [action]" plans that close the intention–action gap.

**Grit (Duckworth)** - sustained passion and perseverance toward long-term goals.

## Studies & evidence (author, year, finding, numbers)

- **Zimmerman (2000)** - established the three-phase cyclical SRL model; skilled learners cycle forethought → performance → self-reflection, transforming ability into skill through goal-setting and self-monitoring.
- **Dunlosky & Nelson (1992)** - immediate JOLs are poorly calibrated, but *delayed, cue-only* JOLs are highly diagnostic of later recall. Monitoring accuracy depends heavily on timing and cues.
- **Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999)** - meta-analysis of 128 studies: tangible, expected, task-contingent rewards significantly *undermined* intrinsic motivation; unexpected rewards and competence-affirming verbal praise did not (could enhance it).
- **Lepper, Greene & Nisbett (1973)** - the classic overjustification experiment: children promised a reward for drawing later drew less freely than unrewarded children.
- **Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler & Macnamara (2018)** - two meta-analyses. Correlation of mindset with achievement was weak (mindset explained ~1% of variance); mindset *interventions* had a small average effect, **d = 0.08**. Larger effects appeared for academically at-risk and low-SES students.
- **Yeager, Dweck, Walton et al. (2019, *Nature*)** - National Study of Learning Mindsets, a preregistered RCT: ~12,000 ninth-graders, 65 schools reporting grades. A <1-hour online intervention raised lower-achieving students' core GPA by **0.10 points** (0.15 in supportive-climate schools; 0.17 in STEM), and cut the share of low achievers with D/F averages by ~5 percentage points. No overall boost for high achievers - effects were targeted.
- **Locke & Latham (~1,000 studies, 1968–2019)** - specific, hard goals beat vague ones via attention, effort, persistence, and strategy, moderated by commitment, feedback, task complexity, ability.
- **Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006)** - meta-analysis, 94 studies: implementation intentions had a medium-to-large effect, **d = 0.65**, on top of merely having a goal.
- **Steel (2007, *Psychological Bulletin*)** - meta-analysis framing procrastination as "quintessential self-regulatory failure"; strongly linked to low conscientiousness, task aversiveness, impulsiveness, and distance from reward (~20% chronic procrastinators).
- **Milkman, Minson & Volpp (2014)** - temptation bundling: participants allowed to hear page-turner audiobooks *only* at the gym went **51% more often** early in the study.
- **Lally et al. (2010, UCL)** - habit automaticity took a *median 66 days* (range 18–254), debunking "21 days."
- **Credé, Tynan & Harms (2017)** - grit meta-analysis: grit correlated only **r ≈ .15** with academic performance (mostly the perseverance facet), and correlated **~.84** with conscientiousness - largely a relabeling ("jangle fallacy").

## Myths / contested points (with the honest nuance)

- **"Growth mindset transforms achievement."** Overstated. Average effects are small (Sisk d = 0.08). The honest story: brief, well-designed interventions produce *modest, targeted* gains, mainly for at-risk/low-SES students in supportive environments (NSLM 2019). It's a small nudge, not a cure-all - and a third of early studies never checked whether the intervention even changed mindset.
- **"Grit is a special predictor of success."** Contested. It barely outpredicts conscientiousness and correlates only weakly with grades.
- **"Rewards always motivate."** Not for already-interesting tasks - they can backfire (overjustification). Informational praise about competence is safer than controlling bribes.
- **"It takes 21 days to build a habit."** Myth; the median is ~66 days with huge variation.
- **"I feel like I know it, so I've learned it."** Fluency illusion - feeling familiar (rereading) is not the same as being able to retrieve.

## Real-world examples & analogies

- **Gym audiobooks:** Milkman's participants only let themselves enjoy *The Hunger Games* while exercising - bundling a "want" with a "should" lifted attendance 51%. A learner can restrict a favorite podcast/coffee shop to study sessions only.
- **The fluency trap:** A student rereads notes, feels smooth and confident, predicts an A, then bombs - a miscalibrated JOL. A closed-book self-quiz exposes the gap.
- **Analogy - SRL as a pilot's flight loop:** plan the route (forethought), fly and watch the instruments (performance/monitoring), debrief after landing (self-reflection), fly better next time.
- **Analogy - mindset as a small tailwind, not an engine:** it helps the plane already struggling into headwind (at-risk students) more than one cruising fine.

## Practical how-to points

- Run the full SRL loop: set a **specific, hard goal** ("solve 10 problems, no notes"), monitor mid-task, and *debrief* honestly afterward - attribute results to strategy/effort, not fixed talent.
- Replace "I'll study more" with an **implementation intention**: "*If* it's 7pm and dinner's done, *then* I do 25 minutes of retrieval practice at my desk."
- Calibrate monitoring with **retrieval and delay**: test yourself (or use delayed JOLs) instead of trusting the feeling of fluency.
- Beat procrastination structurally: **temptation-bundle**, use the **2-minute rule / start absurdly small**, and shape the **environment** (remove the phone) rather than relying on willpower.
- Protect intrinsic motivation: pursue **autonomy, competence, relatedness**; avoid over-rewarding tasks you already like.
- Expect habits to take ~2 months of consistency; miss a day without quitting.

## Common mistakes learners make on this topic

- **Trusting the "feeling of knowing"** (fluency illusion) and under-studying - the core JOL-calibration error.
- **Skipping self-reflection** - never debriefing, so the loop never improves.
- **Setting vague goals** ("do my best") instead of specific/difficult ones.
- **Relying on motivation/willpower** instead of if-then plans and environment design (the intention–action gap).
- **Over-selling mindset or grit** as guaranteed fixes and blaming themselves when a slogan doesn't produce results.
- **Bribing themselves for genuinely interesting work**, risking overjustification.
- **Quitting a new study habit at ~3 weeks** because "it should be automatic by now."
