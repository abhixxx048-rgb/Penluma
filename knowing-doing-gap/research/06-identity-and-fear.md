# Chapter 6 Research Notes: Identity, Fear, and Self-Sabotage

Research notes for "The Knowing-Doing Gap" study-guide book. Focus: the inner blockers — identity-consistent behavior, fear of failure/success, perfectionism, impostor phenomenon, self-handicapping, the "upper limit problem," and self-efficacy. Every claim attributed; replication caveats flagged.

---

## 1. Identity-Based Behavior: We Act Like the Person We Believe We Are

### The core claim
People do not merely act on incentives and information; they act to remain consistent with their self-concept. When knowledge ("I should exercise") conflicts with identity ("I'm not an athletic person"), identity usually wins. This is a central reason knowing does not become doing.

### Cognitive dissonance — Festinger (1957)
- Leon Festinger, *A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance* (1957): holding inconsistent cognitions (beliefs, attitudes, behaviors) produces an aversive psychological state that motivates people to restore consistency.
- Festinger identified three reduction strategies: (1) change a belief or behavior, (2) add new consonant cognitions, (3) downplay the importance of the conflicting cognition. Crucially, people more often change the *belief* than the *behavior* — we rationalize rather than act.
- Classic demonstration: Festinger & Carlsmith (1959) — participants paid $1 to lie about a boring task later rated the task as more enjoyable than those paid $20, because $1 was insufficient external justification, so they changed their internal attitude.
- Festinger & colleagues' field study *When Prophecy Fails* (1956): a doomsday cult whose prophecy failed became *more* committed and began proselytizing — vivid story of belief protecting identity against evidence.
- Relevance to the knowing-doing gap: acting against your self-image creates dissonance; the cheapest resolution is often not to act at all, or to rationalize inaction ("I'm just not a morning person").

### James Clear's "identity votes" (popular synthesis, not primary research)
- James Clear, *Atomic Habits* (2018): "Every action you take is a vote for the type of person you wish to become." Clear argues for identity-based habits (start with who you want to be) over outcome-based habits (start with what you want to achieve). Example: when offered a cigarette, "No thanks, I'm trying to quit" vs. "No thanks, I'm not a smoker" — the second reflects an identity shift.
- Label carefully: Clear is a synthesizer of research, and the "votes" framing is a metaphor, but it rests on real findings:

### Empirical support: the "voter vs. to vote" studies — Bryan et al. (2011)
- Bryan, Walton, Rogers & Dweck, PNAS (2011), "Motivating voter turnout by invoking the self": framing voting as an identity ("be a voter") rather than a behavior ("to vote") increased actual turnout.
- Numbers: in a California sample of 133 registered voters the day before the 2008 election, 95.5%/96% of the noun ("voter") group voted vs. 81.8%/82% of the verb ("vote") group; a New Jersey replication (n = 214) found ~90% vs. ~79%. Authors called it one of the largest turnout effects ever from such a minimal manipulation.
- HONESTY FLAG: Gerber et al., PNAS (2016), "A field experiment shows that subtle linguistic cues might not affect voter behavior" — a much larger field experiment failed to replicate the noun-framing turnout effect. Current status: identity framing is promising but the size and reliability of the effect are contested. Report both.

### Self-affirmation and identity protection
- Claude Steele's self-affirmation theory (1988): people are motivated to maintain a global image of self-integrity; affirming an unrelated valued identity reduces defensiveness toward threatening information. Explains why feedback that threatens identity is dismissed rather than acted on.

## 2. Fear of Failure — What the Research Actually Shows

### Achievement motivation roots — Atkinson (1957)
- John Atkinson's risk-taking model: behavior in achievement settings is a battle between the motive to achieve success and the motive to avoid failure. People dominated by fear of failure pick either very easy tasks (guaranteed success) or absurdly hard ones (failure carries no shame) — both are ways of never being truly tested. This is a beautiful, teachable pattern: the fear-of-failure person avoids the *diagnostic* middle where effort really counts.

### Fear of failure is fear of shame — Conroy (2001, 2002)
- David Conroy (Penn State) built the Performance Failure Appraisal Inventory (PFAI, 25 items, 5 factors). Fear of failure decomposes into five feared consequences: (1) shame and embarrassment, (2) devaluing one's self-estimate, (3) an uncertain future, (4) important others losing interest, (5) upsetting important others.
- Key insight for the chapter: fear of failure is not fear of the outcome itself — it is fear of what failure would *mean about the self* and to others. That directly links it to identity (Section 1).

### Modern goal framework — Elliot & Church (1997); Elliot & McGregor (2001)
- Andrew Elliot's hierarchical model: fear of failure energizes performance-avoidance goals (goal = don't look incompetent). Performance-avoidance goals reliably predict worse outcomes: shallow studying, anxiety, procrastination, self-handicapping, strategic withdrawal of effort, and lower intrinsic motivation and grades.
- So fear of failure often doesn't stop people from wanting success; it changes *how* they pursue it — defensively — which then undermines performance.

### Behavior signature
- Fear of failure shows up behaviorally as procrastination, self-handicapping, choosing non-diagnostic tasks, and quitting early — i.e., most "laziness" in the knowing-doing gap is avoidance, not absence of motivation.

## 3. Fear of Success — Mostly a Myth in Its Original Form

- Origin: Matina Horner's 1968 doctoral dissertation (popularized in *Journal of Social Issues*, 1972). College students wrote stories about "Anne" (or "John") at the top of a medical school class. ~65% of women vs. ~10% of men wrote negative stories about Anne's success. Horner proposed women have a "motive to avoid success" anticipating social rejection for succeeding in male domains.
- REPLICATION FAILURE: subsequent studies largely failed to replicate a sex difference. A large replication (~700 male and female students) found fear-of-success imagery common in *both* sexes with no significant gender difference (see American Journal of Sociology critique, Levine & Crumrine 1975, "Women and the Fear of Success: A Problem in Replication"). Replications with 5th–11th graders found no sex differences or age trends, and the imagery didn't predict performance. Zuckerman & Wheeler (1975) and later reviews concluded the construct was poorly measured and the "women fear success" claim did not hold up.
- Current consensus: there is no robust evidence for a stable, gendered "fear of success" motive. What *is* real and better supported: (a) fear of the *costs* of success — increased expectations, visibility, responsibility, social backlash; (b) success can raise the stakes of future failure (ties back to impostor feelings and identity); (c) "backlash effects" for counter-stereotypical success (Rudman's research on penalties for self-promoting women) are documented, but that's discrimination-driven, not an internal motive deficit.
- For the book: present "fear of success" as a largely discredited construct in its 1970s form, while acknowledging the genuinely documented phenomenon that success raises expectations and exposure, which some people pre-emptively avoid.

## 4. Perfectionism

### Hewitt & Flett's three types (1991)
- Paul Hewitt & Gordon Flett, Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale (1991; 45 items, 7-point Likert):
  1. **Self-oriented perfectionism** — exacting standards for oneself, harsh self-evaluation ("One of my goals is to be perfect in everything I do").
  2. **Other-oriented perfectionism** — unrealistic standards imposed on others.
  3. **Socially prescribed perfectionism** — believing others demand perfection of you ("My family expects me to be perfect"). This is the most toxic type: most strongly linked to depression, anxiety, hopelessness, and suicidality (Smith et al. 2018 meta-analysis on perfectionism and suicide risk).

### Perfectionism is rising — Curran & Hill (2019, published online Dec 2017)
- Thomas Curran (Bath/LSE) & Andrew Hill (York St John), *Psychological Bulletin*: cross-temporal meta-analysis of 164 samples, 41,641 American, Canadian, and British college students who completed the MPS between 1989 and 2016.
- All three types rose linearly: self-oriented +10%, other-oriented +16%, socially prescribed +33% between 1989 and 2016. Authors attribute the rise to competitive individualism/neoliberal culture, more anxious and controlling parenting, and (in later work) social media comparison.
- The disproportionate rise of *socially prescribed* perfectionism ("I feel everyone expects me to be perfect") is the headline — the type most correlated with mental-health harm.

### Perfectionism → procrastination
- Sirois, Molnar & Hirsch (2017), *European Journal of Personality*, meta-analysis: perfectionism has two dimensions with opposite relationships to procrastination. **Perfectionistic concerns** (fear of mistakes, doubt about actions, perceived pressure) correlate positively with procrastination (r ≈ .23–.24); **perfectionistic strivings** correlate slightly *negatively* (r ≈ −.22 in some analyses). It's the fear component, not high standards per se, that freezes action.
- Mechanism for the book: perfectionism converts every task into an identity referendum; if the work can prove you inadequate, delaying the work delays the verdict.

### Perfectionism → burnout — Hill & Curran (2016)
- Hill & Curran, *Personality and Social Psychology Review* (2016), meta-analysis: 43 studies, N = 9,838, 663 effect sizes. Perfectionistic concerns showed medium-to-large positive relationships with burnout (especially in work settings); perfectionistic strivings showed small negative or null relationships. Again: concerns, not standards, do the damage.

### Vivid material
- Curran's framing (APA/ScienceDaily coverage, 2018): today's young people are "the first generation raised on a diet of social media metrics," turning life into a permanent report card.
- Useful analogy: perfectionism as a debt collector — high standards borrowed against self-worth; every task accrues interest in fear.

## 5. The Impostor Phenomenon

### Origin — Clance & Imes (1978)
- Pauline Rose Clance & Suzanne Imes, "The Impostor Phenomenon in High Achieving Women" (*Psychotherapy: Theory, Research & Practice*, 1978), based on clinical work with ~150 high-achieving women: despite degrees, honors, and promotions, they felt like frauds who had fooled everyone and would be "found out." Note: they called it a *phenomenon*, not a syndrome — it is not a diagnosis in the DSM.
- Clance later built the Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale (CIPS, 1985), still the dominant measure.

### How common is it really?
- Bravata et al. (2020), *Journal of General Internal Medicine*, systematic review: 62 studies, 14,161 participants. Prevalence estimates range from 9% to 82% depending on the measure and cutoff. Impostor feelings are common in men and women and across ages (adolescents to late-career professionals); 16 studies found women reported significantly higher rates, but 17 found no gender difference. Rates were notably high among ethnic-minority groups. Impostor feelings frequently co-occur with depression and anxiety and impaired job performance/satisfaction.
- The famous "70% of people experience impostor feelings at some point" statistic traces to an *unpublished* survey by Gail Matthews and Clance (mid-1980s), popularized by Gravois (2007) in the *Chronicle of Higher Education*. Cite with that caveat — it is folklore-grade, though the Bravata range makes clear the experience is widespread.
- Original 1978 framing as a women's issue has been revised: it affects both sexes broadly. Critics (Tulshyan & Burey, *Harvard Business Review*, 2021, "Stop Telling Women They Have Imposter Syndrome") argue the concept individualizes what is often a rational response to biased environments.

### Vivid stories
- Maya Angelou: "I have written eleven books, but each time I think, 'Uh-oh, they're going to find out now. I've run a game on everybody, and they're going to find me out.'" (Widely reported; she had won multiple Grammys and a Pulitzer nomination.)
- Albert Einstein, late in life, to a friend: "The exaggerated esteem in which my lifework is held makes me very ill at ease. I feel compelled to think of myself as an involuntary swindler." (Quoted in Holton's biography; commonly cited in impostor literature, e.g., *Nature* 2016 "Found out" column.)
- Link to knowing-doing gap: impostors avoid stretch opportunities, don't apply, don't publish, don't ship — because every visible act risks exposure.

## 6. Self-Handicapping: Manufacturing Your Own Obstacles

### The founding experiment — Berglas & Jones (1978)
- Steven Berglas & Edward E. Jones, "Drug choice as a self-handicapping strategy in response to noncontingent success," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 36(4), 405–417.
- Design: college students solved either solvable or unsolvable problems, then ALL were told they'd done brilliantly. Before a retest, they chose between a (fictional) performance-enhancing drug ("Actavil") and a performance-impairing drug ("Pandocrin").
- Finding: men whose success felt random/noncontingent (unsolvable problems) preferentially chose the *impairing* drug. Logic: if I fail the retest, the drug is to blame, not my ability; if I somehow succeed despite it, I look even more brilliant. People will literally sabotage performance to protect the *image* of competence.
- Definition: self-handicapping = creating or claiming obstacles to success in advance, so failure can be attributed externally (protecting self-esteem) and success gains extra credit.

### Real-world scale and cost
- Schwinger et al. (2014), *Journal of Educational Psychology*, meta-analysis: 36 field studies, 49 effect sizes, N = 25,550. Self-handicapping correlates with academic achievement at r = −.23 — a small-to-moderate but consistent drag on grades. It also feeds a vicious cycle: handicap → worse performance → greater self-doubt → more handicapping (Zuckerman & Tsai, 2005, longitudinal evidence).
- Everyday handicaps: procrastinating until the night before ("I only had one night"), staying out late before an interview, not studying, "not really trying," picking impossible workloads, phone distraction, even citing anxiety or illness (claimed vs. behavioral handicaps — Leary & Shepperd 1986 distinction).
- Gender pattern: men more often use *behavioral* handicaps (actually withdrawing effort, alcohol); women more often use *claimed* handicaps (reporting stress/symptoms) — replicated across several studies (McCrea et al., 2008, who tie it to beliefs about effort's worth).

### Vivid stories/analogies
- The student who parties before the final: a failure now has an alibi; an A becomes a legend. The bar for self-worth is protected either way.
- Berglas's own later career note: he studied executives and celebrities who sabotage themselves at the peak of success (his book *The Success Syndrome*, 1986) — success raises expectations, and self-handicapping is insurance against the new, higher bar.
- Analogy: self-handicapping is buying an alibi with your own performance as currency.

## 7. The "Upper Limit Problem" — Popular Psychology, Label It Honestly

- Source: Gay Hendricks, *The Big Leap* (2009). Claim: each person has an internal "thermostat" for how much success, love, and happiness they allow themselves; when life exceeds the setting, we unconsciously sabotage back down to the familiar range ("upper-limiting behaviors": worry, conflict-picking, getting sick, deflecting praise).
- Status: NOT a scientific construct. No peer-reviewed studies test the "upper limit problem"; the book offers anecdotes, no controls, no citations. Reviewers have criticized it for pseudoscientific claims (e.g., illness as self-sabotage) and Hendricks himself has said he deliberately moved away from the scientific model early in his career.
- Why include it at all: it is a useful *metaphor* that rhymes with real research — identity consistency (Section 1), fear of the costs of success (Section 3), self-handicapping after success (Berglas & Jones's noncontingent-success condition is literally about discomfort with success one can't explain). Present as: "a popular framing whose underlying grains of truth have real research analogues, but the 'thermostat' itself is unverified."

## 8. Self-Efficacy — Bandura: Belief in Ability Predicts Attempts

- Albert Bandura, "Self-efficacy: Toward a unifying theory of behavioral change," *Psychological Review* (1977); *Self-Efficacy: The Exercise of Control* (1997). Self-efficacy = belief in one's capability to execute the actions required for a specific outcome. It determines whether people *attempt* tasks, how much effort they invest, and how long they persist under obstacles. Low self-efficacy is thus a direct knowing-doing blocker: you can know exactly what to do and still not start because you don't believe you can pull it off.
- Four sources of self-efficacy (Bandura): (1) mastery experiences (strongest), (2) vicarious experience (models like you succeeding), (3) verbal persuasion, (4) interpretation of physiological/emotional states. Practical implication: small wins are the most reliable efficacy-builder — engineering early success beats pep talks.
- Effect sizes:
  - Stajkovic & Luthans (1998), *Psychological Bulletin*, meta-analysis: 114 studies, k = 157, N = 21,616 — weighted average correlation between self-efficacy and work performance G(r+) = .38, which the authors translate to roughly a 28% gain in performance — larger than typical effects for goal setting or feedback interventions.
  - Multon, Brown & Lent (1991): self-efficacy accounts for ~14% of variance in academic achievement.
  - Moritz et al. (2000): mean r = .38 with sport performance across 45 studies.
  - Steel (2007), *Psychological Bulletin*, meta-analysis of procrastination (691 correlations): self-efficacy is among the strongest inverse correlates of procrastination (r ≈ −.38), alongside impulsiveness, task aversiveness, and (low) conscientiousness; interestingly, "fear of failure" as a raw trait was a weaker direct predictor than expected — its effects run through self-efficacy and avoidance goals.
- Caveat: relationship is bidirectional (performance builds efficacy; meta-analytic cross-lagged work, e.g., Talsma et al. 2018, shows the achievement→efficacy path is at least as strong as efficacy→achievement). Don't oversell belief as magic; sell the mastery-experience loop.
- Distinguish from self-esteem: efficacy is task-specific "can I do this," not global "am I good."

## 9. Why Some People Avoid Challenges While Others Seek Them

- Atkinson (1957): approach-dominant people choose moderate, diagnostic difficulty (~50% success odds); avoidance-dominant people choose very easy or very hard tasks (see Section 2).
- Diener & Dweck (1978), *JPSP*, "An Analysis of Learned Helplessness": among 5th-graders solving puzzles, two responses to identical failure emerged. "Helpless" children attributed failure to low ability, strategy collapsed, and they avoided further challenge; "mastery-oriented" children said things like "I love a challenge," focused on strategy, and often *improved* under failure. Same knowledge, same skills — different interpretation of failure produced opposite behavior.
- Dweck & Leggett (1988) formalized this as entity vs. incremental theories of ability (fixed vs. growth mindset): if ability is fixed, effort and failure are evidence *against* you, so challenges are threats; if ability is malleable, they are information.
- HONESTY FLAG on mindset: Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Taylor & Macnamara (2018), *Psychological Science*, two meta-analyses (129 + 29 studies): mindset–achievement correlation is weak (r ≈ .10) and intervention effects tiny (d ≈ 0.08 overall), though slightly larger for at-risk/low-SES students. Li & Bates (2019) failed to replicate key child studies. Yeager et al. (2019, *Nature*, National Study of Learning Mindsets, N > 12,000) found a small but real effect (~0.10 GPA points among lower-achieving students; effects depend on school context). Consensus: directionally real for some groups, dramatically oversold in pop culture. Present the challenge-seeking/avoiding *pattern* (well documented) without leaning on inflated mindset intervention claims.
- Also relevant: Elliot's avoidance goals (Section 2) and self-efficacy (Section 8) — people seek challenges where efficacy is high and identity feels safe.
- Counterpoint worth one line: Julie Norem's "defensive pessimism" research shows that for some anxious people, imagining failure is a *functional* strategy that fuels preparation — not all negative anticipation is sabotage.

## 10. The Chapter's Through-Line: Self-Sabotage Is Self-Protection

- Unifying claim, well supported across literatures: procrastination, perfectionism, impostor-driven hiding, and self-handicapping are not irrational glitches; they are *strategies* for protecting self-worth and identity from the verdicts that action would deliver.
  - Self-worth theory of achievement motivation — Covington (1984, *The Self-Worth Theory of Achievement Motivation*): in achievement cultures, worth is equated with ability, ability is inferred from performance, so students protect worth by withholding effort ("if I'd tried, I could have").
  - Sirois & Pychyl (2013): procrastination as short-term mood repair — giving priority to immediate emotional relief over the future self.
  - Berglas & Jones (1978): the handicap protects the ability attribution.
  - Steele (1988): self-affirmation reduces defensiveness — when identity is secured elsewhere, people can face threatening feedback and act.
- Practical implications supported by research: identity-level reframes (Bryan et al., with replication caveat), lowering the stakes of any single performance (mastery goals — Elliot), engineering small mastery experiences (Bandura), self-compassion (Breines & Chen 2012: self-compassion after failure increases motivation to improve, vs. self-esteem-protection which does not), and normalizing impostor feelings (Bravata: they're the norm among achievers, not the exception).

### Master analogy bank for the chapter
1. Identity as a thermostat vs. identity as a ballot box (Hendricks' thermostat is folklore; Clear's ballot box has partial evidence).
2. The student who parties before finals = buying an alibi (self-handicapping).
3. Atkinson's ring-toss: fear-of-failure players stand either on top of the peg or across the room — never at the honest middle distance.
4. Maya Angelou's eleventh book — competence never cures impostor feelings; only reinterpreting the feeling does.
5. Perfectionism as a report card that never stops grading (Curran's social-media generation).
