You're on a call, nodding along, genuinely engaged - and the other person later tells a colleague you seemed "checked out." Nothing about your attention changed. What changed is that you were looking at their face on your screen instead of the small glass dot an inch above it.

A video call isn't an in-person meeting that happens to be on a screen. It runs on different rules entirely, and almost nobody teaches you what they are. Most of the fixes below take about ten minutes, and once they're set, you never have to think about them again.

## Why this matters

Most professional communication now happens through a lens and a speaker, not a shared room. If you don't know what actually changes when a conversation moves online, small physics problems - a badly placed camera, a laptop mic, an unbroken hour of talking heads - get misread as personality problems: flat, distant, disorganized, exhausting to deal with.

Fix the physics, and the same person, the same ideas, and the same effort suddenly read as sharp, present, and easy to work with. This isn't about becoming a different communicator online. It's about not accidentally sabotaging the good communicator you already are.

## The eye contact trick nobody tells you about

In a room, eye contact is automatic. You look at someone's eyes, they know it, done. On video, there's a mismatch: your eyes are locked on the person's face on your screen, but the camera capturing you sits an inch or two above or beside that face. Everyone else sees you looking down or off to the side - even though, in your own head, you're looking straight at them.

This one gap is the single biggest reason people come across as "distant" or "shifty" on camera while feeling completely attentive.

The fix takes practice but costs nothing: when you're **talking**, glance at the lens itself, not at the thumbnail of the other person. When you're **listening**, it's fine to look at their face on screen - that reads as normal attentiveness. Some people stick a small arrow or dot next to the webcam as a physical reminder to look up.

A few other physical details compound with this:

- **Camera height.** Prop your laptop on a stack of books so the lens sits at eye level. A camera pointed up your nose from a low laptop is unflattering and quietly undercuts your authority - eye level reads as an equal, not someone looking up or down at the room.
- **Framing.** Your eyes should sit roughly in the top third of the frame, with a small gap of space above your head and your shoulders visible. Not a floating head, not a speck in a giant empty room.
- **Lighting.** Light should hit your face from the front, not from behind. A bright window behind you turns your face into a silhouette because the camera exposes for the background. Face the light source instead.

## Audio beats video, every time

Here's the fact that surprises most people the first time they hear it: viewers forgive grainy, pixelated video far more easily than they forgive bad audio. A blurry face is tolerable. A crackling, echoing, or cutting-out voice makes people mentally check out within seconds, because their brain is suddenly working overtime just to reconstruct missing words.

If you upgrade exactly one thing about your remote setup, make it audio. A $30-$50 USB microphone or a decent pair of wired headphones with a built-in mic will do more for how competent you sound than your camera, your lighting, and your background combined. A laptop's built-in mic in an echoey room, or with a fan running nearby, picks up keyboard clatter and room noise and makes even a great-looking setup sound like you're calling from inside a tin can.

## Turn your energy up, not down

Small speakers and camera lenses flatten everything. Voices sound smaller, faces look stiller, hand gestures barely register inside a tiny video square. To land with the impact you'd have in a real room, you have to deliberately dial your energy **up** - a slightly louder and more [varied voice](/blog/communication-and-influence/11-vocal-delivery-voice-pace-pause), slightly bigger facial expressions, gestures kept inside the frame but a notch more animated than feels natural.

Think of it like acting for a wide camera shot instead of a close-up stage play: the subtle energy that fills a theater looks lifeless on a big screen, so performers consciously amplify. On a video call, you're the performer and the tiny webcam window is the wide shot. Amplify, or you disappear.

A sales manager once noticed her team's video demos got lukewarm reactions even though the material was strong. She recorded herself and realized she talked in the same calm, quiet register she'd use sitting across a desk.

So she started opening every call with a warmer "Great to see you both today!" and added more [hand gestures](/blog/communication-and-influence/12-body-language-nonverbal-communication) while explaining pricing. Close rates went up - not because the pitch changed, but because clients now read her as confident instead of flat. What she'd taken for professionalism was actually invisibility.

## Running a meeting is a different job than attending one

A physical room gives a meeting free structure: shared eye contact, body language that tells latecomers to hush, a natural gravity pulling attention to whoever's speaking. None of that exists on video. Whoever hosts has to manufacture it on purpose.

**If you're running it:**

1. **Send an agenda in advance.** Without one, people don't know why they're there, and side conversations creep into chat.
2. **Open with a [purpose statement](/blog/communication-and-influence/10-how-to-structure-a-presentation) in the first thirty seconds.** "By the end of this call, we need to agree on the launch date" gives everyone a reason to stay tuned in.
3. **Call on people by name.** In a room, a glance invites someone to speak. On video, silence just sits there. Say "Marcus, what's your take?" instead of leaving an open floor nobody fills.
4. **Build in interaction every five to ten minutes.** A poll, a chat question, a show of hands. Without some form of interaction, many participants start quietly checking email within about ten minutes.
5. **Manage screen-share discipline.** Close unrelated tabs and notification pop-ups first, and narrate what you're pointing at - people can't always tell where your mouse is.
6. **End with next steps stated out loud and typed into chat.** Verbal-only decisions evaporate fast in remote work.

**If you're attending**, the discipline runs the other way: stay muted unless you're speaking (an open mic picks up typing, dogs, and doorbells that derail the call for everyone), keep your camera engaged rather than parked and forgotten, and resist multitasking. Eye movement and the tiny delay before you respond to a direct question are both *more* visible on video than in a large physical room, where side glances quietly blend into the crowd.

## Writing async messages that don't create confusion

**Asynchronous communication** ("async") is any message where you don't expect an instant reply - email, a Slack message, a project comment, a recorded update. It's the opposite of a live call: there's no back-and-forth in the moment to patch up confusion, so the message has to do all its own work. It needs to be understood correctly the first time, by someone who wasn't in your head when you wrote it.

Compare two Slack messages asking a designer to fix something:

- *"Can you update the banner? It looks off."*
- *"Hi Priya - on the homepage banner (link attached), the logo overlaps the tagline on mobile. Could you shift it 10px left, or shrink it slightly? No rush - end of day Thursday is fine, since it ships Friday morning."*

The second message answers what, where, how much time pressure, and includes a link. Priya can act immediately, with zero back-and-forth. The first guarantees a clarifying question and a lost half-day if she's six time zones away.

Three rules make async writing work:

1. **[Front-load the point](/blog/communication-and-influence/20-how-to-write-with-clarity).** Put the request or conclusion in the first sentence, then add supporting detail. People skim; don't make them hunt for what you need.
2. **Answer likely follow-ups before they're asked.** If you can predict "when do you need this?" or "why does this matter?", answer it in the same message.
3. **Make tone explicit, because text strips it out.** A short reply like "Fine." can read as calm or furious depending on the reader's mood that day, with no tone of voice to correct the misread. A touch of warmth ("Sounds good, thanks!") or an explicit tag ("Small note, no big deal:") closes that gap.

A useful gut-check before sending anything non-trivial: *if I got hit by a bus right after sending this, could the reader act on it without ever talking to me?* If the answer is no, you're missing context - and a short message that saves you thirty seconds of typing can cost the reader ten minutes of confusion and a full day's delay if they're in a different time zone. It's a small-scale version of the same principle behind [the sender-receiver model](/blog/communication-and-influence/02-sender-receiver-communication-model) - your message only means something once it's decoded correctly on the other end, and async gives you no second chance to fix a bad decode.

## Zoom fatigue is real, and it has four specific causes

**"Zoom fatigue"** is the measurable exhaustion people feel after video calls, beyond ordinary meeting tiredness. Researchers at Stanford didn't just anecdotally note it - they identified four concrete causes, each with a specific fix.

| Cause | Why it drains you | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Excessive close-up eye contact | Seeing many faces staring back at close range, even when they aren't speaking, reads to the brain as unusually intense - not something we evolved to handle calmly. | Shrink the video window instead of going full-screen, or use a grid view that reduces face sizes. |
| Constantly seeing your own face | A mirror running the whole meeting increases self-monitoring and self-criticism, which is mentally tiring. | Turn off "self-view" once you know your framing looks fine. |
| Reduced mobility | In-person meetings let you shift, stand, or walk to a whiteboard. Video calls often mean staying camera-framed and still. | Use an external keyboard and mouse so you can sit further back, or take audio-only stretches with the camera off. |
| Higher cognitive load | Reading tone from a small, sometimes laggy feed while parsing words takes more mental effort than reading a full person in a real room. | Schedule short breaks between back-to-back calls instead of stacking them with zero buffer. |

None of this is a sign of weak stamina. It comes from specific, identifiable features of the medium, and every one of them has a direct fix.

### Keeping an audience awake once you have it

The technique that works across webinars, training, and team meetings is **segmenting**: breaking a session into short chunks - roughly five to ten minutes each - and changing the mode of engagement between them. Talk, then a poll, then a short clip, then a breakout, then talk again. A single unbroken 60-minute monologue loses people no matter how good the content is; a segmented 60 minutes with four or five mode-changes [holds attention](/blog/learn-how-to-learn/09-focus-and-deep-work) because the brain re-engages every time the format shifts.

It's the same reason a long car ride with a child in the back seat feels unbearable by minute twenty with no stops - and completely fine when it's broken into a snack stop, a scenic view, and a rest area. Same distance. Same duration. Far less painful.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A video call is basically the same as an in-person meeting, just remote."** It isn't - eye contact, framing, energy, and attention all behave differently on screen, and treating it identically to an in-person meeting is exactly what makes people come across as flat or hard to read.
- **"If my video looks good, I'm set."** Audio matters more. Viewers tolerate blurry video far better than crackling or dropping audio.
- **"Camera off always means disengaged."** Not necessarily - a brief camera-off with a one-line heads-up (bad connection, quick break) is reasonable. What reads badly is silent, unexplained absence.
- **"A short, quick message is more efficient."** Only if it actually gets understood the first time. A message that saves you thirty seconds of typing can cost the reader ten minutes of confusion and a full day's delay across time zones.
- **"Zoom fatigue means I have low stamina for meetings."** It comes from four specific, well-documented features of video calls - not a personal weakness - and each one has a concrete fix.

## How to use this

1. **Spend ten minutes fixing your camera height and lighting, today.** Prop your laptop up, face a light source, and you never have to think about it again.
2. **Buy a real microphone or headset.** It's the single highest-leverage $30-$50 you can spend on how professional you sound on calls.
3. **Practice looking at the lens, not the screen, when you speak.** Put a sticky arrow next to the camera until it becomes automatic.
4. **Before your next meeting you host, write a one-line purpose statement** and plan one interaction point for every five to ten minutes.
5. **Before sending your next non-trivial Slack or email, run the "hit by a bus" test** - if the reader couldn't act on it without asking you a question, add the missing context.
6. **Shrink your video window and turn off self-view** in your next long call, and notice whether you feel less drained afterward.
7. **Audit your calendar for one call this week that could be an async update instead**, and make the switch.

## Conclusion

A video call isn't a worse version of a meeting - it's a different medium with its own physics, and once you know the rules, they're almost embarrassingly easy to work with: look at the lens, fix your audio, turn your energy up, write async messages that stand alone, and break long sessions into chunks. None of it requires more charisma. It requires knowing what actually changes when the room disappears.

Getting the mechanics of the call right solves how you come across. It doesn't yet solve what you're actually saying and how well it lands - and that's a much older problem than Zoom. If you want the underlying model of why messages succeed or fail regardless of the medium, [the sender-receiver model](/blog/communication-and-influence/02-sender-receiver-communication-model) is the next piece worth understanding.
