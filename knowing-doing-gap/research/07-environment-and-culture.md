# Chapter 7 Research Notes: The Invisible Forces - Environment, Family, and Culture

Research compiled 2026-07-04 for "The Knowing-Doing Gap." Theme: outside forces (environment design, social networks, family background, poverty, culture) shape behavior more than willpower - which is a core reason knowing isn't doing.

---

## 1. Environment design and choice architecture: the easiest option wins

### The default effect - the single most powerful nudge
- **Johnson & Goldstein (2003, Science, "Do Defaults Save Lives?")**: In an online experiment, flipping organ-donation consent from opt-in to opt-out nearly doubled agreement rates - **from 42% to 82%** - with identical people and identical stakes. Across real countries, opt-out ("presumed consent") nations show effective consent rates near or above 90%+, while opt-in nations languish far lower. The famous contrast: **Germany (opt-in) vs. Austria (opt-out)** - culturally similar neighbors separated by a 60–70+ percentage-point gap in registered donors. The only variable that moved was the default. (Caveat for accuracy: later work, e.g., Arshad et al. 2019 and "crowding-out" studies, shows opt-out laws don't automatically raise *actual transplant rates* - family veto and infrastructure matter - so the clean lesson is about *registration behavior*, not transplants.)
- **Madrian & Shea (2001, Quarterly Journal of Economics, "The Power of Suggestion")**: Automatic enrollment **more than doubled 401(k) participation** among newly hired employees at a large U.S. company. About **76% of auto-enrolled participants stayed at the default 3% contribution rate**, ~80% stayed in the default money-market fund, and about **61% did nothing at all** to change any default. People didn't decide to save; the form decided for them. This study seeded the "Save More Tomorrow" program (Thaler & Benartzi) and the auto-enrollment provisions of the U.S. Pension Protection Act of 2006.

### Food placement and proximity
- **Rozin et al. (2011, Judgment and Decision Making, "Nudge to Nobesity I")**: In a pay-by-weight cafeteria salad bar, making a food merely **~10 inches harder to reach**, or swapping a serving spoon for tongs, **reliably reduced intake by 8–16%**. Ten inches of distance beat "knowing better."
- **Thorndike et al. (2012, Am J Public Health; follow-up 2014, Am J Preventive Medicine)**: At Massachusetts General Hospital's cafeteria (~6,500 transactions/day; longitudinal cohort of 2,285 employees), a two-phase intervention - traffic-light labels (green/yellow/red) plus **choice architecture** (healthy items at eye level and within reach, water at every station) - significantly shifted purchases. At baseline 24.9% of sales were "red" and 42.2% "green"; red sales fell and green sales rose, and the 2014 follow-up showed the changes **persisted for two years** with no fade-out. Biggest single effect in phase one: red beverage (sugary drink) sales dropped ~39% when labeled and repositioned.
- **Hollands et al. (2019, Cochrane Review on proximity/availability; also Hollands 2017 "portion size" Cochrane)**: The most rigorous synthesis concludes that altering availability, position, and portion/package size produces real changes in selection and consumption - the proximity effect survives even when the flashier lab literature is discounted.
- **HONESTY FLAG - Brian Wansink**: Much of the most-quoted "food environment" folklore (bottomless soup bowls, cereal-on-the-counter = +20 lbs, "Smarter Lunchrooms" fruit placement) comes from **Brian Wansink (Cornell)**, who **resigned in 2018 after a Cornell misconduct investigation; ~18 papers retracted (JAMA Pediatrics among them) and ~50 flagged** for data inconsistencies and impossible statistics. The chapter should NOT lean on Wansink numbers. The core claim - placement and effort shape eating - still stands on independent evidence (Rozin, Thorndike, Cochrane reviews), but specific Wansink statistics are radioactive.
- **Wendy Wood (USC; "Good Habits, Bad Habits," 2019)**: Experience-sampling studies find **~43% of everyday actions are performed habitually** - same context, little conscious thought (Wood, Quinn & Kashy 2002; Wood & Rünger 2016, Annual Review of Psychology). People with high "self-control" scores mostly aren't gritting their teeth; they arrange contexts so the desired behavior is the path of least resistance.
- **Duckworth, Gendler & Gross (2016, Perspectives on Psychological Science, "Situational Strategies for Self-Control")** and **Duckworth, Milkman & Laibson (2018, PPSI, "Beyond Willpower")**: In randomized studies with students, those taught **situation modification** (e.g., remove the phone, change study location) hit their study goals better the following week than students told to "use willpower" or given nothing; reduced *feelings of temptation* partially mediated the effect. Taxonomy: situational strategies (choose/change the environment) beat purely cognitive/effortful ones because they act *before* temptation fires.

**Story hooks**: (1) The Austrian who is an organ donor and the German who isn't - same values, different form. (2) MGH cafeteria: nobody banned anything; they moved water to eye level. (3) The tongs-vs-spoon salad bar: your "choice" of dessert was made by a utensil.

## 2. Social contagion: behavior spreads through networks

- **Christakis & Fowler (2007, NEJM, "The Spread of Obesity in a Large Social Network over 32 Years")**: Framingham Heart Study network, **12,067 people tracked 1971–2003**. If a **friend** became obese, your own risk of becoming obese rose **57%** (mutual friendships: ~171%); **sibling** +40%; **spouse** +37%. Geographic distance didn't matter (friends far away still "transmitted"), but next-door neighbors didn't - arguing for social norms, not shared environment, as the vector. Effects reached out to about **three degrees of separation** ("three degrees of influence" rule).
- **Christakis & Fowler (2008, NEJM, "The Collective Dynamics of Smoking")**: Same network. Quitting was collective - whole clusters quit "in droves." A **spouse quitting cut your smoking odds by 67%**, a **friend quitting by 36%**, a **sibling by 25%**, a co-worker in a small firm by 34%. Smokers drifted from the network's center to its periphery between 1971 and 2003 as smoking became socially marginal.
- Related: **Fowler & Christakis (2008, BMJ)** - happiness clusters up to three degrees; a happy friend living within a mile raises your probability of happiness ~25%.
- **HONESTY FLAG - the contagion critique**: **Cohen-Cole & Fletcher (2008, BMJ/J Health Econ)** applied the identical statistical method to Add Health data and "found" that **acne, headaches, and height** also appear socially contagious - a reductio showing the method can't fully separate contagion from **homophily** (like befriends like) and shared environment. **Russell Lyons (2011, Statistics, Politics, and Policy)** argued some key estimates weren't statistically significant and the causal claims outran the design; **Shalizi & Thomas (2011, Sociological Methods & Research)** proved homophily and contagion are *generically confounded* in observational network data. Current consensus: peer effects on health behavior are real (supported by experiments like Nicholas Christakis's later randomized village-network studies in Honduras, and Damon Centola's 2010 Science online-network experiments showing behavior spreads faster in clustered networks), but the famous "57%" is an upper-bound estimate from observational data, not a law.
- **Framing for the book**: your network doesn't just carry information ("knowing"); it silently renormalizes what counts as a normal portion, a normal workout, a normal career risk ("doing").

### "You are the average of the five people you spend the most time with"
- **Attribution**: Folk wisdom credited to motivational speaker **Jim Rohn**; per **Quote Investigator (2022)** the earliest close match in print is Jack Canfield's *The Success Principles* (2005), attributing it to Rohn via a chain that runs through Tim Ferriss. No study ever produced the number five. Echoes Aristotle/Confucius and older proverbs ("He that walketh with wise men shall be wise").
- **Scientific status**: Directionally supported (peer effects, network contagion, spousal effects on habits are all real) but wrong in its specifics - influence is not limited to five people, isn't a simple average, extends ~three degrees out (friends-of-friends-of-friends you've never met), and includes online/parasocial exposure. Treat as a useful heuristic pointing at a real force, not a finding. (David Burkus and Psychology Today critiques make this "bigger than five" point.)

## 3. Family, childhood, and socioeconomic status: the shaping of self-control

### The marshmallow test and its unraveling
- **Original**: Walter Mischel's Stanford preschool delay-of-gratification studies (late 1960s–1972); **Shoda, Mischel & Peake (1990)** follow-up (n≈90 with follow-up data, children of Stanford-affiliated families) reported that seconds of preschool waiting predicted adolescent SAT scores and competence.
- **Watts, Duncan & Quan (2018, Psychological Science, "Revisiting the Marshmallow Test")**: Conceptual replication using the NICHD SECCYD cohort - **918 children**, including **552 children of mothers without a college degree** (~10× the original sample, and far more representative). Findings: the bivariate correlation between waiting at age 4½ and achievement at 15 was **about half** the original estimates; adding controls for family background, home environment, and early cognitive ability **cut the association by roughly two-thirds**, to a statistically/practically negligible level. Crucially, **most of the predictive power lived in the first 20 seconds** of waiting, and there was essentially no association with later *behavioral* problems.
- **Interpretation now standard**: the marshmallow test partly measures a child's **environment** - trust, stability, food security, parental modeling - not a raw willpower trait. **Kidd, Palmeri & Aslin (2013, Cognition)**: when the experimenter first proved *unreliable* (promised art supplies never came), children waited a fraction as long as children with a reliable experimenter (means ~3 vs ~12 minutes) - waiting is a *rational bet on your environment*. For a poor child in an unstable home, taking the one marshmallow now can be the smart move.
- Counterpoint to keep it honest: **Falk, Kosse & Pinger (2020, Psychological Science, "Re-Revisiting the Marshmallow Test")** argue Watts et al.'s specification differences understate comparability with Shoda 1990; delay still correlates modestly with outcomes. And the **Dunedin Study (Moffitt et al. 2011, PNAS; ~1,000 New Zealanders followed from birth to 32)** shows a robust gradient: lower childhood self-control predicts worse adult health, finances, and more criminal convictions, even controlling for IQ and class. So self-control matters - but where it *comes from* is heavily environmental.

### SES, stress, and the developing brain
- **Noble, Farah, Hackman and colleagues** (multiple studies, 2005–2015): childhood SES correlates most strongly with **language and executive-function** systems. **Noble et al. (2015, Nature Neuroscience; n=1,099 ages 3–20)**: family income is associated with cortical surface area, with the steepest gains at the low end of the income distribution - moving from very low to modestly higher income tracks with disproportionately large brain-structure differences. Low-SES children in fMRI/EEG work show reduced lateral prefrontal activation during cognitive-control tasks.
- **Hart & Risley (1995) "30-million-word gap"**: famous claim that by age 4, children in professional families hear ~30 million more words than children in welfare-receiving families (from 42 families in Kansas). **HONESTY FLAG**: **Sperry, Sperry & Miller (2019, Child Development)** failed to replicate - in their five communities, some poor children heard as many or *more* words (poor South Baltimore children heard ~1.7× the words of Hart & Risley's "welfare" group when bystander speech counted). The gap's size and framing are contested (Fordham/Brookings defend a real, smaller gap in *child-directed* speech); use it as an example of how a single tiny study became policy folklore.
- **Causal evidence - Baby's First Years (Troller-Renfree, Noble et al., 2022, PNAS)**: RCT giving low-income U.S. mothers **$333/month vs. $20/month** unconditionally; EEG at age 1 (n=435) showed infants in the high-cash group had **more high-frequency brain activity** (a pattern associated with later cognitive development). Effect sizes were modest and some later follow-ups are mixed, but it's the first randomized evidence that *money itself* changes early brain activity - poverty is a cause, not just a correlate.

### Scarcity and the bandwidth tax
- **Mullainathan & Shafir, *Scarcity* (2013)**; key experiments in **Mani, Mullainathan, Shafir & Zhao (2013, Science, "Poverty Impedes Cognitive Function")**:
  - **New Jersey mall study**: participants imagined a $1,500 car repair vs. $150. Rich and poor performed equally on fluid-intelligence tests under the small scenario; under the big scenario, poorer participants' performance dropped by the equivalent of **~13 IQ points** - comparable to losing a full night's sleep or the deficit of chronic alcoholism.
  - **Indian sugarcane farmers (n=464)**, who receive ~60% of annual income at one harvest: the *same farmers* scored the equivalent of **~9–10 IQ points higher** post-harvest (flush) than pre-harvest (broke); when poor they were ~11% slower on Stroop with ~15% more errors. Same person, same "knowledge," different bandwidth.
  - Core concept: **the bandwidth tax** - scarcity (of money, and analogously time) captures attention via "tunneling," taxing working memory and executive control. Implication the book needs: the poor don't fail to act on knowledge because of weak character; scarcity itself consumes the exact cognitive resources (planning, inhibition) that "doing" requires. Willpower advice is cheapest for the people who need it least.
  - Application example from the book: **slack** - why the poor "borrow at 300% APR" (payday loans) is predicted by tunneling; and why one-time "bandwidth-friendly" designs (defaults, reminders, simplified forms) outperform education programs.

## 4. Mindset: real, small, and situation-dependent

- **Original**: Carol Dweck (Stanford), *Mindset* (2006); **Mueller & Dweck (1998)** - praising *ability* ("you're so smart") made children pick easier tasks and lie about scores; praising *effort/process* did the opposite.
- **HONESTY FLAG - the replication debate**:
  - **Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler & Macnamara (2018, Psychological Science)**: two meta-analyses. Mindset–achievement correlation across 365,000+ students: **r ≈ 0.10**. Mindset *interventions* across 43 effect sizes/57,000+ students: **d ≈ 0.08** overall (moving an average student from the 50th to ~53rd percentile) - with somewhat larger effects for low-SES and academically at-risk students.
  - **Li & Bates (2019)** failed to replicate Mueller & Dweck's headline effects in Chinese samples: beliefs moved, downstream performance didn't.
  - **Yeager et al. (2019, Nature, National Study of Learning Mindsets)**: preregistered RCT, **>12,000 ninth-graders in 65 representative U.S. public schools**, a <1-hour online intervention. Result: **+0.10 GPA points among lower-achieving students** (0.15 in core courses in supportive schools), plus higher advanced-math enrollment; no grade effect for high achievers. Critically, effects appeared mainly where **peer norms supported challenge-seeking** - mindset works when the *culture around the student* lets it work (a perfect illustration of this chapter's thesis).
  - Dueling meta-analyses continue: **Macnamara & Burgoyne (2023, Psych Bulletin)** find little robust benefit and flag researcher-allegiance effects; **Burnette et al. (2023)** find meaningful heterogeneity - benefits concentrate in at-risk groups with high-fidelity delivery. **Consensus for the book**: growth mindset is not a miracle; it's a **cheap, small, targeted** effect (~0.1 SD for struggling students in supportive environments), not a personality transplant. Beware "mindset theater" in schools and companies.

## 5. Schools: training compliance vs. initiative

- **John Taylor Gatto** (New York State Teacher of the Year 1991; *Dumbing Us Down*, 1992): argued schooling's hidden curriculum teaches conformity, dependence on authority, and waiting to be told - powerful rhetoric, but **anecdotal**; critics (e.g., Wade A. Carpenter: "one-sided and hyperbolic, but not inaccurate") note he offers little systematic evidence.
- **HONESTY FLAG - the "factory model" story is partly invented history**: education historians (Audrey Watters, 2015; Jennifer Binis; Sherman Dorn) show the claim that Horace Mann copied Prussian schools to staff factories is largely a retrospective myth; age-graded schooling predates and outlived industrial logic. Use the compliance critique carefully: schools *do* reward obedience and penalize initiative in measurable ways (teacher preference studies find teachers rate compliant students more favorably; creativity research - e.g., Westby & Dawson 1995 - found teachers claim to love creative students but actually favor conforming ones), but "schools were designed as factories" overstates the history.
- Better-evidenced angle: **classroom structures that reward right answers over questions**. Research on student question-asking (e.g., Susan Engel's work on curiosity, 2011–2015) finds question-asking collapses between kindergarten and middle school as evaluation pressure rises. Combine with Yeager 2019's finding that peer norms determine whether a mindset "sticks": the environment either licenses initiative or extinguishes it.

## 6. Culture: attitudes toward failure and risk

- **GEM (Global Entrepreneurship Monitor) fear-of-failure rates**: the share of adults who see good opportunities but say fear of failure would stop them from starting a business varies hugely by country - recent GEM waves put **Japan around 54–55%** vs. the **U.S. around 45%** (and mid-30s in earlier waves); Japan persistently ranks among the highest of developed economies. GEM also notes innovation-driven economies and women report higher fear of failure.
- **Japan**: bankruptcy carries deep stigma tied to honor and family shame; personal guarantees historically meant a failed founder lost everything and became unemployable ("The Stigma of Failure and Limited Opportunities for Ex-failed Entrepreneurs' Redemption in Japan," Springer volume, 2011). Masayoshi Son (SoftBank) is the counterexample who talks openly about failures.
- **Silicon Valley**: "fail fast" is an explicit cultural institution - failure as tuition. **FailCon**, founded San Francisco **2009** by Cass Phillipps, spread to **21+ cities** (Tokyo, Tehran, Barcelona…). VCs commonly treat a failed startup as valuable experience on a résumé. (Balance: VentureBeat/Foreign Policy critiques note "fail fast" can become a self-congratulatory ritual that hides survivorship bias and "walking dead" startups.)
- **Finland's "Day for Failure" (Epäonnistumisen päivä), October 13** - started **2010 by Aalto University entrepreneurship students** who reasoned that fear of inadequacy was blocking new company formation; now international, with public figures sharing their failures.
- **Astro Teller, Google X ("Captain of Moonshots"), TED 2016**: X *pays bonuses and gives promotions* to teams that kill their own projects after honest disproof - "We have bonused every single person on teams that ended [failed] projects." The point: an organization deliberately re-engineered its incentive environment so the *rational* act (admitting failure) is also the *rewarded* act - closing a knowing-doing gap by design rather than exhortation.
- Bridge concept: **psychological safety (Amy Edmondson, 1999; Google Project Aristotle, 2015)** - teams act on what they know only where admitting error is safe; culture is choice architecture at the group level.

## 7. Synthesis for the chapter

1. Behavior follows the **path of least resistance**: defaults, distance, and visibility beat intentions (Johnson & Goldstein 42→82%; Madrian & Shea; Rozin's 10 inches; Thorndike's cafeteria).
2. Norms are **contagious** - networks renormalize "normal" out to ~three degrees (Christakis & Fowler), even though exact effect sizes are contested (Cohen-Cole & Fletcher's "contagious acne").
3. "Self-control" is substantially a **product of environment and family background** (Watts 2018; Kidd 2013; Noble 2015; Baby's First Years RCT), and **scarcity taxes the very bandwidth self-control runs on** (~13 IQ points; Mani et al. 2013).
4. Belief interventions (mindset) are real but **small and environment-dependent** (d≈0.08–0.10; work best for at-risk students in supportive peer cultures - Sisk 2018 vs. Yeager 2019).
5. Institutions and cultures either **license or punish initiative and failure** (Japan 54% vs U.S. 45% fear of failure; FailCon; X's kill-bonuses; Finland's Day for Failure).
6. Practical takeaway: to close a knowing-doing gap, **redesign the situation before recruiting willpower** - Duckworth's situational strategies beat "try harder" in RCTs, and ~43% of behavior is context-triggered habit (Wood).
