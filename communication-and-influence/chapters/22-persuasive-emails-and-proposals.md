# 22. Emails, Proposals, and Messages That Get a "Yes"

## 22.1 The Reader's Reality: Why Most Professional Writing Fails

Picture your manager's inbox on a Monday morning: 140 unread messages, three meetings before lunch, and a phone buzzing with Slack pings. Into that chaos, you send a well-meaning, three-paragraph email explaining the background of a problem, then the analysis, then - buried in the last line - the actual request. What happens? It gets skimmed, marked "read later," and forgotten. Not because your idea was bad, but because you wrote for yourself instead of for your reader's reality.

This is the starting truth of persuasive professional writing: **nobody owes you their attention**. Every email, proposal, or message competes with dozens of others for a few seconds of focus. Research on reading behavior backs this up starkly - most people decide whether to even open a message in under a second based on the subject line alone, and once opened, a large share of readers spend less than fifteen seconds actively reading before deciding whether to continue, forward, ignore, or act. If your ask isn't visible in that window, it doesn't exist.

Persuasive writing isn't about fancy vocabulary or clever hooks. It's about respecting the reader's time so completely that saying "yes" becomes the path of least resistance. Every technique in this chapter - from subject lines to follow-ups - serves one goal: **reduce the effort and risk the reader feels in agreeing with you.**

> **Analogy:** Think of your message like a vending machine, not a treasure hunt. A treasure hunt makes the reader dig through clues (background, history, context) to find the prize (your request) at the end. A vending machine shows the exact item and price up front - press one button, get the result. Busy readers want vending machines.

> **Example:** A product manager needs budget approval for a new hire. Version A opens with two paragraphs about team history and workload trends before asking for headcount in paragraph four. Version B opens: "Requesting approval to hire one QA engineer by August 1st - this will cut our release delays by an estimated 30%. Details below." The VP replies to Version B in nine minutes. Version A sits unread for four days.

### What "Persuasive" Really Means at Work

In a professional setting, persuasion isn't manipulation - it's **clarity plus credibility plus ease**. You are not tricking anyone into a decision they wouldn't otherwise make; you are removing the friction (extra effort, confusion, or risk) that stops a reasonable person from agreeing with a reasonable idea. Keep that ethical frame in mind through the rest of this chapter: every tactic here should make truth easier to see, not harder.

> **Section summary:** Readers are busy, distracted, and quick to judge. A message that buries its point loses, no matter how good the idea is. Persuasive writing means making your ask fast to find, easy to understand, and low-risk to approve.

## 22.2 Subject Lines and Openers That Get Read

The subject line (or the first line of a chat message) is a gate, not a decoration. Studies of email behavior consistently find that a huge share of recipients - often cited around 47% - decide whether to open a message based on the subject line alone, before reading a single word of the body. If the gate is vague ("Quick question" or "Update"), your carefully written message may never be opened at all.

### What Makes a Subject Line Work

- **Specificity beats cleverness.** "Budget approval needed by Friday - $4,200" beats "Question about spending" every time. Specific subject lines let the reader triage instantly.
- **Front-load the action or deadline.** Put the most important word first, because mobile inboxes often truncate subject lines after 30–40 characters. "Approve: Q3 hire (decision needed Thu)" works even if cut short.
- **Keep it short.** Research on subject line length points to roughly six to ten words (about 40–60 characters) as the sweet spot for getting opened - long enough to be specific, short enough to scan in one glance.
- **Say what you need, not just what happened.** "Server outage last night" describes a fact. "Need your sign-off: outage fix, 15 min" describes an action. The second gets replies.
- **Match the subject to the content.** A subject that overpromises and underdelivers trains your reader to distrust your next email - a meaningful share of people admit they've unsubscribed or tuned out senders whose subject lines misled them.

> **Best practice:** Use a simple three-part subject line formula: **[Action needed] + [Topic] + [Deadline/stakes]**. Example: "Decide: vendor contract - renews Oct 1" or "Review needed: draft slides for Thursday board meeting."

> **Common mistake:** Writing subject lines that describe your own activity ("Follow-up from our call," "Notes from today") instead of the reader's next action. The reader has to open the email just to figure out if it matters to them - friction you could have removed with one better word.

### The Opening Line Matters Almost as Much

Once opened, the first sentence has to earn the second. Avoid throat-clearing openers like "I hope this email finds you well" or a paragraph of pleasantries before the point. A single warm, brief greeting is fine - but the very next sentence should already be doing work. Compare: "I wanted to reach out because I've been thinking about our onboarding process and had some ideas..." versus "Our onboarding process loses 20% of new hires' first-week productivity - here's a two-step fix." The second sentence has already told the reader why they should keep reading.

> **Example:** A recruiter cold-emailing a candidate writes the subject line "Question for you" - vague, ignorable. A stronger version: "Senior Backend role at Acme - 20% above market, remote-first." It names the opportunity, the appeal, and gives the reader an instant reason to open.

> **Section summary:** Nearly half of readers decide to open a message purely from its subject line, so make the subject specific, short, and action-focused. Once opened, skip the throat-clearing - get to something useful in your very first sentence.

## 22.3 BLUF and the Pyramid Principle: Lead With Your Point

- **BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front)** - A writing method, born in the U.S. military, where you state your main point and required action in the very first sentence or two - before any background or explanation.
- **Pyramid Principle** - A structuring method created by Barbara Minto, the first woman to graduate from Harvard Business School's MBA program and a former McKinsey consultant, which says: state your conclusion first, then support it with a small number of grouped arguments, then back each argument with evidence - like an upside-down triangle, wide point at the top.

Both BLUF and the Pyramid Principle fight the same natural human habit: **building up to the point**. We tell stories chronologically - first this happened, then that, then I realized, so here's my idea. That's how humans think, but it's the opposite of how busy readers want to receive information. Minto's insight, developed while training McKinsey consultants to write clearer reports, was that readers grasp an argument fastest when the answer comes first and the reasoning follows - never the other way around.

```
 TRADITIONAL (buried point)      PYRAMID / BLUF (point first)
 ----------------------------    ----------------------------
 Background                      MAIN POINT / ASK
     |                               |    |    |
 Analysis                        Reason Reason Reason
     |                               |    |    |
 More context                    Evidence Evidence Evidence
     |
 ASK (finally, at the end)       (reader can stop reading
                                  anytime and still "get it")
```

Notice the phrase in the diagram: *the reader can stop reading anytime and still get it*. That's the real test of a well-structured message. If your VP reads only your first sentence, do they know what you want? If they read the first paragraph, do they know why? A pyramid-structured message passes this test at every level; a chronological one only makes sense once you've read the whole thing.

> **Analogy:** News articles use this exact structure - it's called the "inverted pyramid" in journalism. The headline and first paragraph give you the who, what, when, where, and why. Everything after that adds detail for readers who want more. Nobody has to read to the last paragraph to know what happened; you do, for your emails.

### How to Apply BLUF in Practice

1. **Write your last sentence first.** Literally draft the ask or conclusion, then move it to the top.
2. **Answer the five W's fast:** who needs to do what, by when, and why it matters - in the opening lines.
3. **Group your reasons into three or fewer buckets.** Minto's related idea, **MECE** (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive - meaning your reasons don't overlap and together cover the whole picture), keeps your supporting points from becoming a messy, repetitive list.
4. **Push background to the bottom or an appendix.** If someone needs the history, they can scroll down or ask. Most won't need to.

> **Example - before and after:**
*Before:* "Hi team, over the last quarter we've noticed that support tickets have been climbing, and after digging into the data with the CS team, we found that most of them relate to the new billing flow. We also looked at churn data and saw a correlation. Given all this, I think we should consider pausing the billing rollout."
*After (BLUF):* "Recommend pausing the new billing rollout by Friday. Reason: it's driving a 40% spike in support tickets and is correlated with rising churn. Details on the data below."

> **Best practice:** BLUF works best with readers who already trust you or share context with you - internal teams, managers, colleagues. For cold outreach to strangers (a sales prospect, a stranger you're asking a favor of), you often need one warm, credibility-building sentence before the ask - but keep it to one sentence, not one paragraph.

> **Section summary:** BLUF and the Pyramid Principle both say the same thing: put your main point first, then support it, then add background last. This lets a busy reader understand your whole message from the first line, even if they never read the rest.

## 22.4 One Clear Ask: The Call to Action

- **Call to Action (CTA)** - The specific, single action you want the reader to take - approve, reply, click, schedule, sign off - stated so plainly that no interpretation is needed.

Here's a mistake that quietly kills more good ideas than bad writing does: **asking for too many things at once, or asking so vaguely that the reader isn't sure what "yes" even looks like.** "Let me know your thoughts" is not a call to action - it's an invitation to do nothing, because doing nothing technically satisfies the request. A true CTA names the exact behavior and, ideally, a deadline.

| Weak ask | Strong, clear ask |
| --- | --- |
| "Let me know your thoughts on this." | "Reply 'approved' or flag concerns by Wednesday 5pm." |
| "We should probably discuss the budget at some point." | "Can we do 20 minutes Thursday at 2pm to finalize the budget?" |
| "I've attached the proposal for review." | "Please review pages 1–2 only and confirm the pricing works by Friday." |
| "Happy to help however is useful." | "I can take the client call on Tuesday - want me to send the invite?" |

### Make It Easy to Say Yes

Every extra step between "I agree" and the reader's actual action is a chance for them to procrastinate or drop the request entirely. Reduce those steps:

- **Pre-fill the decision.** Instead of "What time works for you?" (which requires them to check a calendar and think), try "Does Tuesday 2pm or Wednesday 10am work? I'll send the invite either way." A small, bounded choice is easier to answer than an open-ended one.
- **Do the reader's homework for them.** Attach the document already formatted the way they need it. Draft the reply they could send. Summarize the three options instead of asking them to generate options themselves.
- **Name a default.** "If I don't hear back by Friday, I'll proceed with Option B" respects their time and prevents your request from stalling indefinitely - as long as it's used sparingly and never for high-stakes or sensitive decisions where silence shouldn't be read as consent.
- **One ask per message.** If you need three decisions, consider three short messages rather than one that buries two asks and forces the reader to reply three separate ways.

> **Analogy:** This mirrors the "foot-in-the-door" principle from persuasion research: people are far more likely to agree to a small, specific, low-effort request than a large, vague one. A clear, bounded CTA is a small step; a vague "thoughts?" is an unbounded one.

> **Common mistake:** Ending a long, well-argued email with "Thoughts?" This throws away all the clarity you built. If you've made a case for something, ask for the specific decision that follows from that case - not an open invitation to debate from scratch.

> **Section summary:** A persuasive message needs exactly one clear, specific action for the reader to take, with a deadline if possible. Reduce the reader's effort by offering bounded choices, doing prep work for them, and never burying your ask in a vague "let me know."

## 22.5 Structuring the Message: Emails, Proposals, and Executive Summaries

The right structure changes with the format, but the underlying logic - point first, then support, then action - stays the same. Below are three field-tested templates you can copy directly.

### Template 1: The Persuasive Email

```
Subject: [Action] + [Topic] + [Deadline]

1. Greeting (one line)
2. BLUF: the ask or recommendation, in 1-2 sentences
3. Why it matters (1-3 short reasons, bulleted if 2+)
4. Evidence or context (short - link/attach the rest)
5. Clear CTA with deadline
6. Warm, brief close
```

> **Example - a real-style persuasive email:**
*Subject: Approve: switch to new invoicing tool (saves ~6 hrs/week)*
Hi Dana,
Requesting approval to switch our invoicing from Sheets to Invoicely by August 1st. It will save the finance team roughly six hours a week and cut billing errors, based on a two-week trial we ran last month.
Why now: our current process caused two late invoices this quarter, and the trial showed zero errors across 40 test invoices. Cost is $40/month, already within our software budget.
Could you reply "approved" by Thursday so I can set it up before month-end billing? Happy to hop on a 10-minute call if you'd like to see the trial results first.
Thanks,Priya

### Template 2: The One-Page Proposal (N-O-S-E Framework)

Proposal expert Tom Sant developed a simple structure that works whether you're pitching a client or your own leadership: **N-O-S-E** - Needs, Outcomes, Solution, Evidence.

- **Needs:** Show you understand the problem from the reader's point of view, not just yours.
- **Outcomes:** Describe the specific result they want - in their language, ideally with a number.
- **Solution:** Present your proposal, tied directly back to the needs and outcomes above.
- **Evidence:** Back it with a case study, data point, or comparable result - proof, not just promises.

### Template 3: The Executive Summary

An executive summary is the short, standalone section at the top of a longer proposal or report that lets a decision-maker act without reading the rest. It matters enormously: most senior readers spend well under a minute deciding whether a document is worth deeper attention, so the executive summary often is the only part that gets read. Keep it to one page, and follow the **Opener → Need → Solution → Evidence → Call to Action** flow. A related structure used across the proposal industry is the "five-box" model: (1) the customer's situation and goals, (2) your solution and its key benefit, (3) cost and value, (4) next steps, (5) a short closing reason to choose you.

| Format | Best for | Core structure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Email | Quick decisions, internal requests | BLUF → reasons → CTA |
| One-page proposal | Pitching a new idea, client or internal | Needs → Outcomes → Solution → Evidence |
| Executive summary | Leading a longer report or full proposal | Opener → Need → Solution → Evidence → CTA |

> **Best practice:** Write the executive summary or persuasive email *last*, after you've done the full analysis - but put it *first* in the document. You can only compress well once you know everything worth cutting.

> **Section summary:** Whether it's a short email or a full proposal, the winning structure is the same shape: state the point, show you understand the need, present the solution, back it with evidence, and close with one clear action. Templates like BLUF, N-O-S-E, and the five-box model just formalize that shape for different situations.

## 22.6 Tone, Politeness, and Requests Up the Chain

Structure gets your message understood; tone gets it welcomed. A perfectly structured email written in a cold or entitled tone can still get a "no" - people help people they like and trust, not just people who are logically correct.

### The Tone Balance

- **Warm but efficient.** A single friendly line ("Hope the launch went well!") costs nothing and builds goodwill - but don't let warmth delay the point past the first two sentences.
- **Confident, not demanding.** "Please approve by Friday" reads more collaborative than "I need this by Friday." Small word choices like "please," "would," and "could" soften a request without weakening it.
- **Own the ask; don't hide behind hedges.** Overusing "just," "sort of," or "I was wondering if maybe" undercuts your credibility. State the ask plainly, then be polite in tone around it - not inside it.

> **Best practice:** Read your draft once for content, then once purely for tone - out loud if possible. If it would sound rude or curt said aloud to the person's face, revise it; if it would sound weak or apologetic, tighten it.

### Handling Requests Up the Chain

Asking your manager, a skip-level leader, or an executive for something requires an extra layer of care, because you're also asking them to spend political or budgetary capital on your behalf. Three adjustments matter most:

1. **Lead with the business impact, not your personal need.** "I'd like a new laptop" is a personal ask. "My current laptop's slow builds are costing the team ~3 hours/week" is a business case that happens to help you too.
2. **Show you've done the thinking.** Senior readers grant approval faster when they can see you've already considered the obvious objections and alternatives. Add a short "Options considered" line if the decision is nontrivial.
3. **Make the decision small.** Where possible, frame the ask as approve/reject on something you've already scoped, rather than "figure out what to do about X." Executives are far more willing to say yes to a ready-made decision than to open-ended homework.

> **Example:** An analyst wants budget for a new tool. Instead of "Can we get budget for a data tool?" she writes: "Requesting $200/month for Tool X. It will cut our reporting time from 6 hours to 1 hour weekly - about $780/month in analyst time saved at current rates. I compared two alternatives; this one had the best ROI. Approve by Friday to start next reporting cycle?" This gives the executive a ready decision, not a project.

> **Common mistake:** CC-ing an executive's boss, or a wide group, to "add pressure" for a decision. This can look like an attempt to force a public yes rather than let the person genuinely evaluate the request, and it often backfires by making the reader defensive rather than cooperative.

> **Section summary:** Tone should stay warm and respectful even when the message is direct and structured. When asking up the chain, translate personal needs into business impact, show you've already thought it through, and hand over a ready decision rather than an open problem.

## 22.7 Follow-Ups That Get Answers Without Annoying Anyone

Most requests don't get a "no" - they get silence. People are busy, and your message simply slipped down the inbox. A good follow-up isn't nagging; it's a service to a reader who likely still intends to respond but needs a nudge.

### The Follow-Up Formula

- **Wait an appropriate interval.** Two to three business days for routine requests; longer for something you know requires real thought or approval from others on their side.
- **Reply in the same thread, don't start fresh.** This preserves context so the reader doesn't have to reconstruct what you asked.
- **Restate the ask in one line - don't retell the whole story.** "Circling back on this - still hoping to get your sign-off on the invoicing switch by Thursday. Happy to answer any questions."
- **Make it easy to respond in five seconds.** Offer a yes/no, a link, or a one-word reply option: "A quick 'approved' works great if you're good with it."
- **Remove blame from the tone.** Never write "as I mentioned before" or "per my last email" - both read as passive-aggressive and put the reader on the defensive rather than moving them toward yes.

```
FOLLOW-UP TIMING
Day 0: Send request (BLUF + clear CTA)
Day 2-3: Friendly nudge, same thread, 1-2 lines
Day 5-7: Second nudge + offer alternative
          ("If now's not the moment, who
           should I loop in instead?")
Day 10+: Escalate politely, or treat
          silence as your cue to proceed
          with a stated default (if safe)
```

> **Example:** "Hi Marco - just floating this back to the top of your inbox! Still need approval on the vendor contract to lock in the discount before it expires Friday. If it's easier, I can grab 5 minutes on a call instead - whatever's fastest for you." This nudges without guilt-tripping and offers an easier path (a call) in case email isn't working.

> **Best practice:** If you've followed up twice with no response and the decision is time-sensitive, it's fair and professional to say so plainly: "Since I haven't heard back and the offer expires Friday, I'll plan to proceed with Option B unless you'd like to weigh in before then." This isn't rude - it's respecting both your deadline and their autonomy to object.

> **Common mistake:** Sending a follow-up that just says "Bumping this" or "Any update?" with no restated ask. The reader now has to scroll back up to remember what you needed, which adds friction right when you're trying to reduce it.

> **Section summary:** Silence usually means "busy," not "no." A good follow-up restates the ask briefly in the same thread, makes replying effortless, and avoids blame - and if a deadline is real, it's fine to state a default action rather than let the request die from neglect.
