# Chapter 3 Research Notes: Why We Procrastinate (Even When We Know the Consequences)

Research notes for "The Knowing-Doing Gap" study guide. Every claim attributed. Compiled July 2026.

---

## 1. The Core Reframe: Procrastination Is Emotion Regulation Failure, Not Laziness or Bad Time Management

**The headline finding of modern procrastination science:** procrastination is a failure of emotion regulation - a strategy of short-term mood repair - not a character flaw ("laziness") and not a scheduling problem.

- **Joseph Ferrari (DePaul University)**, the field's pioneering researcher since the late 1980s, states flatly: *"It really has nothing to do with time-management. To tell the chronic procrastinator to 'just do it' would be like saying to a clinically depressed person, 'cheer up.'"* (APS Observer, "Why Wait? The Science Behind Procrastination," 2013.)
- **Fuschia Sirois (University of Sheffield/Durham) & Timothy Pychyl (Carleton University), 2013** - the field-defining theoretical paper: *"Procrastination and the Priority of Short-Term Mood Regulation: Consequences for Future Self,"* Social and Personality Psychology Compass, 7, 115–127. Core argument: procrastination is "the primacy of short-term mood repair over the longer-term pursuit of intended actions." We don't avoid the task; we avoid the negative feelings the task generates (boredom, anxiety, frustration, self-doubt, resentment). Avoiding delivers instant relief - which negatively reinforces the avoidance.
- **Definition used in the literature (Pychyl; Steel 2007):** procrastination is *"the voluntary delay of an intended action despite expecting to be worse off for the delay."* That last clause is the knowing-doing gap in one sentence - the procrastinator KNOWS the consequences and delays anyway. This distinguishes procrastination from strategic delay or simple prioritization.

### Key experimental evidence

- **Tice, Bratslavsky & Baumeister (2001)**, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 80, 53–67 - "Emotional Distress Regulation Takes Precedence Over Impulse Control: If You Feel Bad, Do It!" Participants put into a bad mood procrastinated more (played games/read magazines instead of preparing for a test) - but ONLY when they believed their mood was changeable and the distraction was enjoyable. When participants were led to believe their mood was "frozen" (unfixable), the sad ones didn't bother procrastinating. This is the smoking gun: procrastination is done *in order to feel better*. Tice & Bratslavsky called it "giving in to feel good."
- **Pychyl's pager study (published 2000)**, *Journal of Social Behavior and Personality*, 15(5), 239–254 ("Five Days of Emotion"): 45 students carried pagers and were beeped 8 times a day for 5 days before a deadline. Students delayed the tasks they rated most aversive and unpleasant, swapped them for pleasant activities - and reported guilt while doing so. Task aversiveness, not poor planning, predicted delay.
- **Steel's 2007 meta-analysis** confirmed task aversiveness as one of the strongest predictors of procrastination (see Section 3).
- **Emotion-regulation intervention evidence:** Eckert, Ebert, Lehr, Sieland & Berking (2016, Learning and Individual Differences) found that training students in emotion-regulation skills - specifically tolerating and modifying negative emotions - reduced subsequent procrastination. (See also the 2022 PMC review "I'll Worry About It Tomorrow – Fostering Emotion Regulation Skills to Overcome Procrastination.")

**Beginner-friendly analogy:** procrastination works like scratching a mosquito bite - instant relief, worse itch later. Or: the procrastinator's brain treats a boring tax form like a hot stove; avoidance is the flinch.

## 2. Present Self vs. Future Self

- Sirois & Pychyl (2013) frame procrastination as an intertemporal bargain in which the present self gets the mood relief and the **future self gets the bill** - we treat our future self like "another person" who will somehow have more time, more energy, and more willpower ("I'll feel like doing it tomorrow"). We are reliably wrong about that (an "affective forecasting" error).
- **Hal Hershfield (UCLA), Ersner-Hershfield, Wimmer & Knutson (2009)**, *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience* - fMRI: when people think about their **future selves, the medial prefrontal cortex activity looks more like the pattern for thinking about a stranger** than for thinking about the present self. The degree of neural "self-continuity" predicted temporal discounting and real saving behavior. People shown **digitally age-progressed photos of themselves allocated roughly twice as much to retirement savings** in Hershfield's follow-up work (Hershfield et al., 2011, Journal of Marketing Research).
- Implication for the chapter: procrastination is literally offloading work onto someone your brain regards as a stranger.
- **Vivid example:** Jerry Seinfeld's "Night Guy vs. Morning Guy" bit - Night Guy stays up late because getting five hours of sleep is "Morning Guy's problem." Comedy, but an exact description of the Hershfield finding.

## 3. Piers Steel: Temporal Motivation Theory and the Procrastination Equation

- **Piers Steel (University of Calgary), "The Nature of Procrastination" (2007)**, *Psychological Bulletin*, 133(1), 65–94 - the landmark meta-analysis: **691 correlations across 216 samples**. Strongest, most consistent predictors of procrastination: **task aversiveness, task delay (how far away the reward is), low self-efficacy, impulsiveness, low conscientiousness** (especially low self-control, high distractibility, low organization, low achievement motivation). Neuroticism, rebelliousness, and sensation seeking were only weakly related - procrastination is not "anxiety" or "rebellion" at its core; it is impulsiveness meeting delay.
- **Temporal Motivation Theory (Steel & König, 2006, Academy of Management Review)** integrates expectancy theory, hyperbolic discounting, need theory and prospect theory into one formula - the **Procrastination Equation** (popularized in Steel's 2011 book of that name):

  **Motivation = (Expectancy × Value) / (Impulsiveness × Delay)**

  - *Expectancy* = how likely you think success is (self-efficacy)
  - *Value* = how rewarding/pleasant the task is
  - *Impulsiveness* = your sensitivity to immediate temptation
  - *Delay* = how far off the payoff is
  - Motivation collapses when the numerator is small (boring task you doubt you can do well) and the denominator is large (impulsive person, distant deadline). Explains why motivation spikes as deadlines approach: Delay shrinks, so the fraction explodes - the "deadline rush."
- Caveat to record: TMT is a useful integrative model, not settled physics. Pychyl himself (Psychology Today, "Temporal Motivation Theory: Formula or Folly?", 2008) criticized reducing procrastination to a utility equation, arguing it misses the emotional core. A longitudinal test (Steel, Svartdal et al., 2018, Frontiers in Psychology) supported TMT's core predictions across goal stages. Present both views: TMT explains the *math* of when we procrastinate; emotion regulation explains the *mechanism*.
- **Genetics:** Gustavson, Miyake, Hewitt & Friedman (2014), *Psychological Science* - twin study, 181 identical + 166 fraternal pairs: procrastination is **~46% heritable**, impulsivity ~49%, and at the genetic level the two are **perfectly correlated (genetic r = 1.0)** - procrastination appears to be an evolutionary by-product of impulsivity, linked through goal-management ability.

## 4. Akrasia: The Ancient Greeks Knew

- **Akrasia** (Greek: *a-* "without" + *kratos* "power/command") = acting against one's own better judgment; "weakness of will." The knowing-doing gap has a 2,400-year-old name.
- **Socrates** (in Plato's *Protagoras*) denied akrasia is even possible: "no one goes willingly toward the bad" - apparent weakness of will must really be ignorance/miscalculation of the good.
- **Aristotle** (*Nicomachean Ethics*, Book VII) disagreed: the akratic person genuinely knows what is right but their knowledge is not "active" at the moment of action - overcome by passion or appetite, like "a drunk man reciting verses" who has knowledge but can't use it. Aristotle distinguished *akrasia* (weakness - you intend right and fail) from *akolasia* (intemperance - you don't even intend right).
- Modern science sides with Aristotle: knowledge is present but motivationally inert when emotion and impulsiveness take over. Procrastination is the everyday face of akrasia.
- Also useful: the word "procrastination" itself - Latin *pro-* (forward) + *crastinus* (of tomorrow): "belonging to tomorrow."

## 5. How Common Is It? Prevalence Statistics

- **~20% of adults worldwide are chronic procrastinators** - Joseph Ferrari's repeated finding (first published Ferrari 1991; Harriott & Ferrari 1996, *Psychological Reports* 78, 611–616; cross-cultural replication Ferrari, Díaz-Morales, O'Callaghan, Díaz & Argumedo 2007, *Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology* - similar ~14–20% rates in the US, UK, Spain, Peru, Venezuela, Australia). Ferrari notes this prevalence is **higher than clinical depression or phobias** and it cuts across gender, age, and culture. Common ranges cited in the literature: **15–25% of adults**.
- **80–95% of college students procrastinate** to some degree; ~50% procrastinate consistently and problematically (Steel 2007, citing Ellis & Knaus 1977; O'Brien 2002).
- **Procrastination is rising:** Steel's historical analysis - in **1978 only about 4–5%** of Americans described themselves as chronic procrastinators; by the 2000s about **20–26%** did (Steel 2007; Steel, *The Procrastination Equation*, 2011). Plausible drivers: an environment engineered for impulsiveness (notifications, infinite scroll = "cued temptation" available 24/7).
- **95% of procrastinators wish they could reduce it** (O'Brien 2002, cited in Steel 2007) - nobody is choosing this on reflection; another marker that it's a self-regulation failure, not a preference.

## 6. The Procrastination Doom Loop (Avoid → Guilt → Worse Mood → More Avoidance)

- Mechanism (Sirois & Pychyl 2013; Flett, Stainton, Hewitt, Sherry & Chen 2012, *Journal of Rational-Emotive & Cognitive-Behavior Therapy*): avoiding a task buys relief, but produces **guilt, shame, and anxiety**, which worsen mood, and since bad mood is the trigger for avoidance, the cycle feeds itself. Journalists dubbed it the **"procrastination doom loop"** (Derek Thompson, The Atlantic, 2014).
- **"Procrastinatory cognitions"** (Stainton, Lay & Flett 2000; Flett et al. 2012): automatic brooding thoughts like "No matter how much I try, I still put things off" and "Why can't I do what I should be doing?" - measured by an 18-item inventory; these thoughts predict distress and further procrastination, maintaining the loop.
- **Breaking the loop - the self-forgiveness study: Wohl, Pychyl & Bennett (2010)**, *Personality and Individual Differences*, 48, 803–808 ("I forgive myself, now I can study"): 119 first-year students tracked across two midterms. Students who **forgave themselves** for procrastinating before exam 1 procrastinated **less** before exam 2; the effect worked by reducing negative affect. Practical upshot: self-compassion, not self-flagellation, is the exit ramp - guilt-tripping yourself fuels the very mood the avoidance is regulating. (Sirois 2014 similarly links self-compassion to lower procrastination via lower stress.)
- Related depression link: three-wave longitudinal work (e.g., Johansson et al. 2024, PMC11100206) shows procrastination → perceived stress → depression/anxiety symptoms in students; the arrow runs both ways.

## 7. The Costs: Grades, Health, Money

### Performance and stress
- **Tice & Baumeister (1997)**, *Psychological Science*, 8, 454–458 - the classic semester-long longitudinal study of students: early in the term procrastinators had **less stress and less illness** than others (the short-term win is real!), but by term's end they had **more stress, more illness, more total symptoms, and lower grades on every assignment**. Conclusion: "a self-defeating behavior pattern marked by short-term benefits and long-term costs."
- Meta-analysis: procrastination correlates negatively with academic performance (Kim & Seo 2015, *Personality and Individual Differences*, meta-analysis of 33 studies) - modest but reliable negative effect on GPA.

### Health
- **Sirois's procrastination–health model** (Sirois, Melia-Gordon & Pychyl 2003; Sirois 2007, 2015): trait procrastination → higher stress + fewer wellness behaviors (delayed doctor visits, less exercise, worse sleep, treatment delay) → worse health.
- **Sirois (2015)**, *Journal of Behavioral Medicine* - community sample of 746 adults (182 with hypertension/cardiovascular disease, 564 healthy): higher trait procrastination was associated with **hypertension and cardiovascular disease** even after controlling for demographics and Big Five personality; procrastinators with HT/CVD used more maladaptive coping (behavioral disengagement, self-blame).
- Swedish cohort of 3,525 university students (Johansson et al. 2023, *JAMA Network Open*): higher procrastination scores at baseline predicted worse depression, anxiety, stress, disabling arm/neck pain, poor sleep, physical inactivity and loneliness 9 months later.

### Money
- **Tax procrastination:** an H&R Block analysis found last-minute filers **overpaid by an average of ~$400** due to rushing and errors - **over $473 million in aggregate overpayments** (widely reported, e.g., TIME 2012; Psychological Science coverage). In 1988, taxpayers claiming refunds lost roughly **$1 billion in interest** by filing late (cited in "April 15 Syndrome," Kasper, MIT Sloan). IRS failure-to-file penalty: 5% of unpaid taxes per month, up to 25%.
- **Workplace:** D'Abate & Eddy (2007, *Human Resource Development Quarterly*) estimated employees spend 60–90+ minutes of the workday on non-work activities, costing **~$8,875 per employee per year**; later industry estimates run to ~$15,000/employee and a claimed **£76 billion/year** for UK businesses (2019–2025 industry surveys - treat these consultancy figures as soft, but directionally consistent).
- **Retirement saving** is the canonical economists' example (Akerlof 1991, "Procrastination and Obedience," *American Economic Review*): each day of delay is individually trivial, cumulatively catastrophic - the reason auto-enrollment pensions (Thaler & Benartzi's Save More Tomorrow) work is that they harness inertia instead of fighting it.

## 8. Why Deadlines Work: Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002) - and a Replication Caveat

**Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002)**, "Procrastination, Deadlines, and Performance: Self-Control by Precommitment," *Psychological Science*, 13(3), 219–224. (Read in full from MIT PDF.)

- **Study 1 (MIT executive-education course, 99 professionals, 3 papers):** the *no-choice* section got fixed, evenly spaced deadlines; the *free-choice* section set their own binding deadlines (1% grade penalty per day late). Rationally, free-choice students should pick the last day (maximum flexibility, no downside). Instead they self-imposed early deadlines - mean 41.8 days early for paper 1, 26.1 for paper 2, 9.8 for paper 3; **only ~27% chose the last day for all three papers**. People know they procrastinate and voluntarily pay for handcuffs. BUT the evenly-spaced (externally imposed) section earned **higher grades (M = 88.76 vs 85.67)** and did better on the final project (86 vs 77).
- **Study 2 (proofreading, 60 participants, 3 texts, 100 planted errors each, 10¢/error found, $1/day late penalty):** performance ranked **evenly spaced external deadlines > self-imposed deadlines > single end deadline** on errors detected, delays, and earnings. Participants who happened to space their self-set deadlines evenly performed as well as the external-deadline group.
- **Three answers:** Do people self-impose costly deadlines? **Yes.** Do self-imposed deadlines help? **Yes** (better than one far-off deadline). Do people set them optimally? **No.**
- Why deadlines work in TMT terms: they slash *Delay* in the denominator and add certain, near-term consequences - motivation rises.
- **Honesty note / replication caveat:** a recent direct replication of Study 2 (Hyndman et al., working paper/SSRN, ~2024–25) found **negligible effects of deadline condition on performance** - the deadline-spacing effect may be smaller or more context-dependent than the famous original suggests. The Study 1 pattern (people voluntarily self-impose costly deadlines) is the more robust takeaway. Record both.
- Related replication honesty for the chapter's neighborhood: **ego depletion** (willpower-as-muscle, Baumeister & colleagues 1998) - a 2016 Registered Replication Report (Hagger, Chatzisarantis et al., 23 labs, ~2,100 participants, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*) found an effect **indistinguishable from zero**, even though 22 of 23 labs predicted success. So avoid framing procrastination as "running out of willpower fuel"; the emotion-regulation and impulsiveness accounts rest on sturdier ground.

## 9. Why Perfectionists Procrastinate

- Old view (Steel 2007, treating perfectionism as one thing): perfectionism was essentially **unrelated** to procrastination overall.
- Updated, better view - **Sirois, Molnar & Hirsch (2017)**, *European Journal of Personality*, meta-analysis: split perfectionism into two dimensions:
  - **Perfectionistic concerns** (fear of mistakes, harsh self-criticism, worry about others' judgment): **positively** correlated with procrastination, **r = +.23** (43 studies, N ≈ 10,000).
  - **Perfectionistic strivings** (holding high personal standards): **negatively** correlated, **r = −.22** (38 studies, N = 9,544).
- Mechanism: perfectionistic concerns trigger **fear of failure and threat appraisals**; the task becomes emotionally aversive ("if it's not perfect, I'm a failure"), and per the emotion-regulation model, aversive tasks get avoided. High standards alone don't cause procrastination - self-punishing evaluation does. Related: Yosopov, Saklofske, Smith, Flett & Hewitt (2024) show fear of failure and overgeneralization of failure mediate the perfectionism→procrastination link.
- Bonus psychological wrinkle (Ferrari): delaying can be **self-handicapping** - if I start the night before, a mediocre result can be blamed on lack of time rather than lack of ability; my ideal self stays untested and unfalsified.

## 10. Vivid Stories, Examples & Analogies (for the chapter)

1. **Victor Hugo's nudity contract (deadline/precommitment):** contracted in 1830 to deliver *The Hunchback of Notre-Dame*, Hugo blew months on everything else. His fix: he had his valet lock away his clothes so he couldn't leave the house, writing wrapped in a shawl. Book delivered early, January 1831. Ancient precommitment = Ariely's costly self-imposed deadline.
2. **Douglas Adams:** "I love deadlines. I love the whooshing noise they make as they go by." His editor once locked him in a hotel suite for three weeks to finish *So Long, and Thanks for All the Fish*; a collaborator said Adams "raised procrastination to an art form."
3. **Margaret Atwood:** self-described world-class procrastinator who frets all morning and starts writing at 3 p.m. - yet says "I would consider it dishonorable to miss a deadline." Useful nuance: procrastination within the day ≠ missing commitments; structure and identity can contain the tendency.
4. **Tim Urban's "Instant Gratification Monkey" and the "Panic Monster"** (Wait But Why; TED 2016, one of the most-viewed TED talks, ~70M+ views): the monkey steers the wheel toward easy-and-fun until the Panic Monster (deadline) wakes up - a pop-culture-perfect rendering of TMT's Delay term and impulsiveness.
5. **Jerry Seinfeld's Night Guy/Morning Guy** - present self vs. future self (Section 2).
6. **The naked truth of the pager study:** students beeped mid-Netflix-equivalent in 2000 reported *feeling guilty right then* - procrastination isn't pleasure, it's relief marinated in guilt.
7. **The tax-day line at the post office** (April 15 midnight postmark rush): millions pay real money - ~$400 each in errors - for a delay they knew was coming 12 months in advance.

## 11. Chapter Takeaways (bridge to "closing the gap" chapters)

- You cannot fix procrastination with better calendars, because the calendar was never the problem; the feeling is. Target the emotion (make the first step tiny, reduce task aversiveness, "just get started" - progress itself repairs mood: Pychyl's "just get started" finding that once begun, tasks are rated as less aversive).
- Manipulate the equation: raise Expectancy (break tasks down, build small wins), raise Value (pair boring tasks with pleasure - "temptation bundling," Katy Milkman 2014), cut Delay (near-term milestones, external deadlines), cap Impulsiveness (remove cues, precommitment à la Hugo).
- Shrink the gap between present and future self (vividly imagine future self; Hershfield's aged-photo interventions).
- Exit the doom loop with self-forgiveness and self-compassion (Wohl et al. 2010; Sirois 2014), not shame.
