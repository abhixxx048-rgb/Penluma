# 20. Writing to Be Read: Clarity and Plain Language

Most professional writing is written to be sent, not to be read. There is a difference. A document is "sent" when the writer clicks submit and feels relief. A document is "read" when a busy, distracted human actually absorbs the point, in the ten seconds they were willing to give it, and knows what to do next. This chapter is about closing that gap: writing so clearly that people actually read what you wrote, understand it the first time, and act on it.

Clarity is not a nice-to-have style preference. It is a form of respect. Every unclear sentence you send is a small tax you charge the reader - extra minutes spent decoding what you meant, extra risk that they guess wrong. Research on organizational communication consistently finds that companies with clear, effective communication practices significantly outperform those with poor communication on measures like shareholder returns and employee engagement. Clear writing is not just polite. It is a competitive advantage.

## Why Most Professional Writing Is Hard to Read

Nobody sets out to write badly. Unclear writing usually happens for three innocent reasons. First, **we write in the order we think**, which is rarely the order the reader needs. We ramble through context, caveats, and backstory before arriving at the point, because that is how the idea unfolded in our own head. Second, **we overestimate our reader's patience and prior knowledge**. We assume they already know the acronyms, the backstory, the stakes - so we skip explaining them, or we bury the real message under jargon that signals "I know what I'm talking about" instead of "here is what you need to know." Third, **we rarely edit**. A first draft is a thinking document. It reflects the writer's process, not the reader's needs. Sent unedited, it forces the reader to do the editing work themselves - reading it twice, hunting for the point, guessing at intent.

> **Analogy:** Unclear writing is like handing someone a jigsaw puzzle instead of a picture. All the information is technically there, but the reader has to do assembly work you should have done for them. Clear writing hands over the finished picture.

> **Section summary:** Writing is usually unclear by accident, not by intent - because we write in the order we thought, assume too much shared knowledge, and skip editing. Fixing this is a skill, not a talent, and it is learnable.

## Front-Load the Point: BLUF

- **BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front)** - A writing structure where you state your main point, request, or conclusion in the very first sentence - before any background, context, or supporting detail.

BLUF began in military and intelligence writing, where officers had seconds, not minutes, to extract the decision-relevant fact from a report. It has since spread into business, consulting, and email culture for a simple reason: readers skim. Studies of email and workplace reading behavior repeatedly show that most readers decide within the first few seconds whether a message is worth their full attention. If your point is buried in paragraph three, most readers never reach it.

The structure is simple to describe and hard to practice, because it goes against how our brains naturally narrate:

```
NATURAL (chronological) ORDER      BLUF ORDER
------------------------------     ------------------------------
Context and background        ->   THE POINT (decision/ask/result)
More context                       Key reason #1
The build-up                       Key reason #2
Finally... the point               Supporting context (if needed)
```

> **Example:** A project manager needs to tell a director that a launch will slip. Buried version: "The vendor had some delays in Q2, and then we had two engineers out sick, and the QA environment had an outage last week, which pushed back testing, so..." BLUF version: "The launch will slip two weeks, to July 19. Root cause: a vendor delay plus a QA environment outage. We do not need budget or headcount - just your sign-off on the new date." The second version lets the director act in one read.

> **Best practice:** Before you write anything longer than three sentences, ask yourself: "If my reader only reads my first sentence, what is the one thing they must know?" Write that sentence first. Everything else supports it.

> **Common mistake:** Treating BLUF as rude or presumptuous - "I need to build up to it so it doesn't sound blunt." In most professional contexts, the opposite is true: burying the point reads as evasive or disorganized, while leading with it reads as confident and considerate of the reader's time.

> **Section summary:** BLUF means stating your main point first, then supporting it - the reverse of how we naturally think out loud. It respects the reader's limited attention and lets them act immediately instead of hunting for your conclusion.

## The Building Blocks of Plain Language

- **Plain language** - Writing designed so the intended reader can find what they need, understand it the first time, and use it - achieved through simple words, short sentences, and clear structure.

Plain language is not "dumbed down" writing. It is precise writing. The U.S. Plain Writing Act of 2010 defines plain writing as text that is "clear, concise, well-organized, and follows other best practices appropriate to the subject or field and intended audience." Government agencies adopted this standard because unclear public documents were costing citizens time, money, and trust - one widely cited estimate from the Center for Plain Language found that a majority of Americans struggle to understand typical government or legal documents. The same tax applies inside companies: unclear policies, emails, and reports slow everyone down.

Here are the core moves that make language plain:

### 1. Short words over long words

Prefer "use" to "utilize," "help" to "facilitate," "start" to "commence," "about" to "approximately." Long words feel formal, but they cost the reader extra processing time for zero extra meaning.

### 2. Short sentences over long sentences

A sentence with more than about 20-25 words starts to strain working memory, especially when it contains multiple clauses. Break long sentences into two short ones. This alone can transform a document.

### 3. Active voice over passive voice

- **Active voice** - A sentence structure where the subject performs the action ("The team missed the deadline") rather than receives it ("The deadline was missed by the team").

Passive voice hides the actor, adds words, and often signals someone is avoiding responsibility ("Mistakes were made"). Active voice names who did what, which is almost always more useful and more honest.

### 4. Strong verbs, not verb-plus-noun phrases

"Conduct an analysis of" should be "analyze." "Make a decision" should be "decide." "Provide assistance to" should be "help." Hunting down these bloated phrases and replacing them with a single strong verb is one of the fastest ways to cut word count without cutting meaning.

### 5. Concrete nouns over abstract nouns

"Deliverables," "synergies," and "solutioning" float above reality. "The report," "the two teams working together," and "fixing the problem" land on it. Concrete language lets the reader picture the thing you mean.

### 6. Cut jargon and filler

Jargon (specialized insider language) is fine between experts who share it, but it excludes and confuses anyone outside the circle - and it is frequently used, consciously or not, to sound impressive rather than to communicate. Filler words ("basically," "in order to," "at this point in time," "due to the fact that") add length without adding meaning.

### 7. One idea per paragraph

A paragraph is a container for a single idea. When a paragraph tries to hold three ideas, the reader has to sort them out mid-read. Split it. A one-sentence paragraph is not a mistake - sometimes it is exactly the right size.

| Wordy / unclear | Plain |
| --- | --- |
| Utilize existing resources | Use what we have |
| Due to the fact that sales declined | Because sales declined |
| A decision was made by leadership | Leadership decided |
| We are currently in the process of reviewing | We are reviewing |
| Provide clarification regarding | Clarify |
| In order to facilitate a smoother onboarding experience | To make onboarding smoother |

> **Section summary:** Plain language means short words, short sentences, active voice, strong verbs, concrete nouns, and one idea per paragraph - with jargon and filler stripped out. None of this makes writing simplistic; it makes writing precise and fast to absorb.

## Readability: Measuring How Hard Your Writing Is to Read

- **Readability score** - A numeric estimate of how difficult a piece of text is to read, usually based on sentence length and word/syllable complexity.

The most widely used tools are the Flesch Reading Ease score (0-100, higher means easier) and the Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (which maps difficulty to a U.S. school grade). Both were developed from research showing that two simple, measurable factors - average sentence length and average syllables per word - predict most of how hard a text is to read. You do not need to calculate these by hand; Microsoft Word, Google Docs add-ons, and free online tools will score any text instantly.

As a rough guide: general business and news writing sits around a Flesch score of 60-70 (roughly 8th-10th grade reading level). Legal and academic writing often scores 30 or below - dense and slow. Healthcare and government communication aiming for broad accessibility targets 80 or higher. The lesson is not "write for children." It is: match your reading level to your audience and your goal, and default to simpler than you think you need, because almost everyone reads faster and remembers more from simpler text - including expert readers who are simply busy.

> **Example:** A compliance team rewrote its internal policy FAQ after learning its Flesch-Kincaid grade level was 16 (post-graduate). Employee support tickets asking "what does this mean?" dropped by more than a third after they rewrote it to a grade level of 9, without removing any actual requirement.

> **Best practice:** Run a readability check on anything that will be read by a broad or busy audience - company-wide emails, customer messages, policies, slide decks. Treat a high grade-level score as a signal to simplify, not a badge of sophistication.

> **Section summary:** Readability tools like Flesch Reading Ease turn "is this clear?" into a number based on sentence length and word complexity. Aim your score at your real audience - usually simpler than your instinct suggests - because clarity, not complexity, signals mastery.

## Editing Ruthlessly: Cut at Least 10%

Writing clearly is not just about how you write the first draft - it is about what you remove afterward. William Zinsser, in his classic guide *On Writing Well*, argued that most first drafts are cluttered with words doing no real work, and that a skilled editor can often cut a large share of a draft's length without losing a single fact. His method: go through every sentence and put invisible brackets around anything not earning its place - the redundant adverb ("smile happily"), the throat-clearing phrase ("it is important to note that"), the adjective stating something already obvious ("tall skyscraper"). Then cut what's bracketed.

A practical target for professional writing: after your first draft, **cut at least 10% of the words** - often more. This is not about making the piece shorter for its own sake. It is a forcing function that catches clutter you didn't notice while drafting, because when you're generating ideas, your brain is focused on getting them down, not on trimming them.

> **Analogy:** Editing is like packing a suitcase for a trip. Your first pass throws everything you might conceivably need onto the bed. The skill is the second pass - taking things back out until only what actually earns its place in the suitcase remains.

A simple editing pass looks like this:

- **Read it aloud.** Anywhere you run out of breath or stumble, the sentence is too long or too tangled.
- **Delete the first paragraph.** Often the true opening point is in paragraph two - the first paragraph was you warming up.
- **Hunt for "that," "very," "really," "basically," "in order to."** Most can be deleted with no loss of meaning.
- **Find every passive sentence and ask: who is doing this?** Rewrite in active voice where the actor matters.
- **Check each paragraph for one idea.** If it holds two, split it.

> **Common mistake:** Editing only for typos and grammar, and skipping structural editing. A grammatically perfect sentence can still be clutter. Ruthless editing cuts whole phrases and sentences, not just commas.

> **Section summary:** First drafts are naturally cluttered because drafting and editing use different mental modes. Cutting at least 10% of your words - reading aloud, killing filler phrases, converting passive to active - reliably makes writing sharper without losing meaning.

## Formatting for Skim-Readers

Even perfectly plain sentences can get lost if they arrive as one dense wall of text. Most professional readers skim before they read - their eyes scan the shape of a document before deciding whether and how deeply to engage. Formatting is how you design for that scan.

- **Headings** act as a table of contents for the eye. A reader should be able to grasp your document's structure from headings alone.
- **Bullets** break lists out of paragraph form so each item is instantly separable and scannable - use them for genuine lists (steps, options, criteria), not as a way to avoid writing full sentences everywhere.
- **Bold** highlights the two or three phrases per page that matter most if someone reads nothing else. Overusing bold defeats the purpose - if everything is bold, nothing stands out.
- **White space** (short paragraphs, line breaks) gives the eye places to rest and signals "this is manageable," which measurably increases the odds someone starts reading at all.

> **Example:** An engineering lead sends a weekly status update as one 300-word paragraph. Reformatted with a one-line BLUF, three bolded headers ("Shipped," "Blocked," "Next"), and bullets under each, the same information takes readers a third of the time to process - and the lead starts getting faster, more specific replies, because people can now see exactly what needs a response.

> **Best practice:** Before sending any document longer than a paragraph, glance at it with fresh eyes for two seconds, as if you were the busy recipient. If you can't tell what it's about from that glance, add a heading, a bold lead sentence, or bullets until you can.

> **Section summary:** Readers scan before they read, so headings, bullets, bold, and white space are not decoration - they are a map that lets busy people find what matters fast. Use them to reveal structure, not to compensate for a lack of it.

## Before and After: Putting It All Together

Seeing the full transformation matters more than any single rule. Here is a real-style example of a status email rewritten using every principle above.

> **Before:** "I wanted to reach out in order to provide an update regarding the current status of the client onboarding project, which, as you may be aware, has experienced some delays due to the fact that there were a number of unforeseen technical issues that arose during the integration phase, and it is anticipated that these issues will be resolved in the coming weeks, at which point we will be in a position to move forward with the remaining steps of the onboarding process."

> **After:** "Onboarding is delayed by two weeks. Cause: integration bugs found last week. Fix: our engineers expect to close them by Friday. No action needed from you now - I'll confirm the new go-live date Monday."

The before version is 78 words and asks the reader to extract the point from the middle of a single sentence. The after version is 38 words - a cut of more than half - states the bottom line first, uses short sentences and active voice, and tells the reader exactly what to do (nothing, yet). Nothing important was lost. Everything unnecessary was.

> **Section summary:** The gap between unclear and clear writing is rarely about information - it's about order, word choice, and length. Lead with the point, use short plain sentences, and cut hard, and the same facts become dramatically easier to act on.
