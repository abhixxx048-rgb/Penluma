## Key concepts (plain definitions)

- **Forgetting curve:** the pattern, first charted by Hermann Ebbinghaus (1885), showing that memory for newly learned material decays rapidly at first and then levels off - an exponential-like decline, not a straight line.
- **Savings method:** Ebbinghaus's measure of memory. He relearned a list and counted how many fewer trials (or how much less time) relearning took versus original learning. If original learning took 25 repetitions and relearning took 20, that is 5/25 = **20% savings**. A "pure" behavioral measure that captures memory even below conscious recall.
- **Decay:** memory traces weaken with the passage of time if unused.
- **Interference:** other memories compete. **Proactive interference** = old learning disrupts new (yesterday's password blocks today's). **Retroactive interference** = new learning disrupts old (learning Spanish scrambles your French).
- **Retrieval failure / cue-dependent forgetting:** the memory is stored but inaccessible because the right retrieval cue is missing (Tulving's *encoding specificity principle* - cues present at encoding must be present at retrieval).
- **Spacing effect / distributed practice:** spreading study across separated sessions produces far better long-term retention than **massed practice** (cramming) of the same total time.
- **Lag effect:** among spaced schedules, *longer* gaps generally beat shorter gaps for long-term retention.
- **Storage strength vs retrieval strength** (Bjork & Bjork's *New Theory of Disuse*, 1992): storage strength = how deeply learned (never decreases); retrieval strength = how accessible right now (fluctuates, decays). Cramming spikes retrieval strength but adds little storage strength.
- **Overlearning:** continuing to study past the point of one perfect recall.

## Studies & evidence (author, year, finding, numbers)

- **Ebbinghaus (1885):** self-experiments with nonsense syllables. Illustrative savings: ~58% after 20 min, ~44% after 1 hr, ~34% after 1 day, ~25% after 6 days, ~21% after 31 days. Steepest loss is in the first hours.
- **Murre & Dros (2015, PLoS One):** replicated Ebbinghaus using his own methods. Savings of ~42% at 20 min, ~34% at 1 hr, ~31% at 1 day, ~17% at 6 days, ~9% at 31 days - confirming the curve's shape holds ~130 years later. They also noted a slight upward "bump" near 24 hours, consistent with sleep aiding consolidation.
- **Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006, *Psychological Bulletin*):** landmark meta-analysis synthesizing **839 assessments across 317 experiments in 184 articles**. Spaced practice reliably beat massed practice, and the key law: the *inter-study interval* producing best retention grows as the desired retention interval grows.
- **Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008, *Psychological Science*):** ~1,350 people learned facts, reviewed after a gap, tested up to 1 year later. **Optimal gap ≈ 10–20% of the retention interval.** Concretely: for a ~1-week target, gap ~1 day; for a 70-day test, best gap was ~21 days; for a 1-year target, optimal gap fell to ~5% (a few weeks). Longer target → proportionally longer optimal gap.
- **Rohrer & Taylor (2005, *Applied Cognitive Psychology*):** 218 students, geography facts / word definitions. Overlearning helped at 1 week but the advantage shrank sharply over weeks; they called overlearning an "inefficient strategy" for lasting retention - extra repetitions in one session buy little durable memory.
- **Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*):** reviewed 10 study techniques; only **two earned a "high utility" rating - distributed practice and practice testing** - effective across ages, abilities, and materials.

## Myths / contested points (with honest nuance)

- **"The famous curve numbers are exact."** No - Ebbinghaus's percentages are illustrative from one subject (himself) on nonsense syllables; meaningful material decays more slowly. Treat the *shape* as robust, the exact figures as approximate.
- **"Memory just decays."** Decay alone is a weak explanation; interference and retrieval failure account for much forgetting (a lost cue can revive a "forgotten" memory instantly).
- **"Cramming doesn't work."** It *does* work for an exam the next morning - that is why it feels effective. It fails only for durable retention. The honest framing: cramming trades long-term memory for short-term performance.
- **"There's one magic interval (e.g., 24 hours)."** The optimal gap is *relative* to how long you need the material, not a fixed number.
- **Spacing effect is one of psychology's most replicated findings**, but recent classroom studies (e.g., on math procedures) show it can be smaller or inconsistent for complex skills - the lab effect doesn't always transfer cleanly.

## Real-world examples & analogies

- **Watering a plant:** dumping a month's water at once floods and runs off; small doses over weeks soak in. Massed vs distributed study.
- **Building muscle / calluses:** one 8-hour gym session injures you and builds little; three sessions a week builds strength. Effortful, spaced retrievals build "memory muscle."
- **The exam-week crammer:** aces Friday's test, remembers almost nothing by the following month - the storage-vs-retrieval gap made visible.
- **Desirable difficulty analogy:** spacing feels *worse* while studying (you've partly forgotten, recall is effortful) yet produces *more* learning - effort is the signal that storage strength is being built.

## Practical how-to points

- Break study into multiple short sessions across days/weeks rather than one long block.
- Scale gaps to your goal: exam in a week → review every 1–2 days; retain for a year → review every few weeks.
- Revisit material *just as you're starting to forget it* - that effortful recall is what strengthens memory.
- Combine spacing with self-testing (the two "high utility" techniques), not passive rereading.
- Stop overlearning in one sitting; bank that time for a later spaced session.

## Common mistakes learners make

- Judging a method by how it *feels*: fluency during cramming feels like learning but measures retrieval strength, which fades.
- Treating one perfect recall as "done" and over-drilling the same night (inefficient overlearning).
- Using tiny within-day gaps (an hour apart) and expecting long-term gains - real benefits need gaps of at least a day for durable material.
- Massing all review right before the test.
- Assuming spacing should feel easy - the difficulty is the point.
