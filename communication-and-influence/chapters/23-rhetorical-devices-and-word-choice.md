# 23. Rhetoric on the Page: Devices and Word Choice

Every sentence you write is made of choices. You choose which word goes where, how long the sentence runs, whether it ends with a question or a statement. Most of the time you make these choices on autopilot. But professional persuaders — the people whose emails get replies, whose pitches get funded, whose speeches get quoted — make some of these choices on purpose. They use **rhetorical devices** (plain meaning: time-tested patterns of language that make writing more memorable and more convincing).

This chapter is a toolbox. Each tool below is small, learnable, and has been used for thousands of years — from Julius Caesar's battle reports to Martin Luther King Jr.'s speeches to the subject line of the last marketing email you opened. You do not need all of them in every message. In fact, using too many at once makes writing sound like a parody of a TED talk. The goal is to know the tools well enough to reach for the right one, use it once, and let it do its work quietly.

> **Key takeaway:** Rhetorical devices are not decoration — they are word-level shortcuts that make ideas easier to picture, easier to remember, and easier to believe.

## 1. The Building Blocks: Concrete Language, Sensory Words, and Power Words

The single biggest lever in persuasive writing is the choice between **abstract language** and **concrete language**.

- **Abstract language** — Words for ideas, categories, or qualities you cannot see, touch, or picture — like "quality," "efficiency," "innovative," or "value."
- **Concrete language** — Words for things you can picture, hear, or touch — like "arrives in 48 hours," "cuts your invoice time from three days to twenty minutes," or "the screen cracked on the first drop."

Researchers who study consumer language have found the effect of concreteness depends on context, and that nuance matters for how you use it. When people talk about a product to friends, broad, abstract praise ("it's high quality") can travel further because it implies the product is good in general. But in direct interactions — a salesperson talking to a customer, a manager writing to a client, a support agent solving a problem — concrete language wins decisively. Customers rate concrete-speaking employees as more helpful and go on to spend meaningfully more time and money with that company afterward. The lesson for a working professional: when you are speaking directly to the person you want to persuade, trade your abstractions for specifics.

> **Example:** Compare "Our platform improves team efficiency" with "Our platform cuts your weekly status-report time from ninety minutes to twelve." The second sentence gives the reader something to picture and something to check. It is harder to forget and harder to doubt.

Inside concrete language sit two useful sub-tools: **sensory words** (words that trigger sight, sound, touch, taste, or smell — "sharp," "quiet," "gritty," "warm") and **power words** (short, high-impact words that carry emotional or urgent weight, such as "free," "proven," "instantly," "because," "imagine," or "guaranteed"). Power words work partly through emotion and partly through familiarity — they are words the brain has learned to associate with reward, urgency, or trust, so they get processed faster and stick around longer in memory.

> **Best practice:** Before you send a persuasive email or slide, scan it for abstract nouns ending in "-tion," "-ity," or "-ness" (implementation, scalability, effectiveness). Replace at least one with a concrete image or number.

> **Common mistake:** Piling on power words until the message reads like spam — "Amazing, guaranteed, instant results, act now!" Power words work because they are rare and true. Overuse burns the reader's trust and the words' effect at the same time.

| Abstract (weak) | Concrete (strong) |
| --- | --- |
| We are committed to customer satisfaction. | We answer every support ticket within four hours. |
| The proposal offers significant savings. | The proposal saves $42,000 a year — one full salary. |
| Our team has deep expertise. | Our team has shipped this exact migration eleven times. |

> **Section summary:** Trade vague, abstract words for specific, concrete ones whenever you are speaking directly to the person you want to persuade — it gives them a picture and a reason to trust you. Use a handful of true, well-chosen power words for emotional lift, and stop before it starts to sound like an ad.

## 2. Painting Pictures: Metaphor and Analogy

A **metaphor** (calling one thing by the name of another, to transfer its qualities — "our roadmap is a minefield") and an **analogy** (a comparison that explains something unfamiliar by linking it to something familiar — "onboarding a client is like training for a marathon: the slow weeks matter more than the race day") are the fastest way to make an abstract or technical idea land in someone's mind. The reason is simple: the brain understands new things by relating them to things it already knows. Instead of asking your reader to build a brand-new mental model, you hand them one they already own and just relabel it.

> **Analogy:** A good metaphor works like a set of stairs someone already knows how to climb — you're just asking them to walk up to a new room. A bad or overused metaphor is a broken step: it draws attention to itself and makes the reader stumble instead of arriving.

In professional writing, metaphor and analogy do three jobs at once: they simplify (a complex system becomes "a leaky bucket"), they make ideas memorable (people forget statistics but remember pictures), and they carry emotional tone (calling a budget cut "trimming fat" feels very different from calling it "amputating a limb," even though both describe the same action).

> **Example:** A product manager explaining technical debt to non-technical executives says: "Right now we're paying interest on a loan we took out two years ago to ship faster. If we don't pay down the principal this quarter, the interest payments — the bug count — will eat the whole engineering budget." No executive needs a computer science degree to understand that.

The classic warning here is George Orwell's advice against "dying metaphors" — comparisons so overused they've lost their picture entirely ("low-hanging fruit," "move the needle," "boil the ocean"). These phrases no longer make anyone see fruit or a needle; they've become filler. The fix is not to avoid metaphor, but to reach for a fresh one that actually fits your specific situation, or to strip it out and just say the plain thing.

> **Best practice:** Build your analogy from something your specific audience already lives inside — sports for a competitive sales team, cooking for a hospitality client, road trips for a logistics company. A borrowed frame from the reader's own world lands twice as fast as a generic one.

> **Common mistake:** Mixing metaphors in one breath — "We need to circle back and open the kimono so we can move the needle" — which confuses more than it clarifies. Pick one image and stay inside it for the whole sentence or paragraph.

> **Section summary:** Metaphors and analogies borrow a picture the reader already understands and use it to explain something new, which makes ideas easier to grasp and remember. Choose one fresh, audience-specific image, use it consistently, and retire the tired ones like "low-hanging fruit."

## 3. The Architecture of Memorable Lines: Rule of Three and Parallelism

Some patterns are persuasive purely because of their shape. The most famous is the **rule of three**, built from a **tricolon** (a series of three parallel words, phrases, or clauses). "Veni, vidi, vici" ("I came, I saw, I conquered"). "Life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness." "Blood, sweat, and tears." These phrases have survived centuries not by accident — three is the smallest number of examples that reads as a pattern rather than a coincidence. Two items feel like a pair; four or more starts to feel like a list you have to work to remember. Three feels complete, rhythmic, and easy to hold in your head — which is exactly why advertisers gave the world "Snap, Crackle, Pop."

The rule of three works at every scale of professional communication: three bullet points in a slide, three reasons in a sales pitch, three words in a tagline, three examples in an answer to an interview question.

> **Example:** In a job interview, instead of saying "I'm organized, I communicate well, and I also have good technical skills, plus I'm a fast learner" (four vague traits that blur together), say: "I plan carefully, I communicate clearly, and I deliver on time." Three, parallel, memorable.

**Parallelism** (giving matching ideas matching grammatical form) is the engine that makes a tricolon — or any list — sound clean instead of clunky. "We plan, we build, we ship" is parallel: three short verb phrases, same shape. "We do planning, we're building things, and shipped product is what happens next" is the same content wrecked by mismatched grammar. Parallelism is not just an aesthetic nicety; it reduces the reader's mental effort, because once they've decoded the shape of the first item, the second and third are free.

```
  PARALLEL (easy to read)          NOT PARALLEL (effortful)
  ------------------------         ---------------------------
  We listen.                       We do a lot of listening.
  We learn.                        Learning happens too.
  We deliver.                      And delivery is the result.
```

> **Best practice:** When you write a list of benefits, reasons, or values, force every item into the same grammatical shape — all nouns, all short verb phrases, or all "-ing" words. Read the list aloud; if the rhythm stumbles, the grammar doesn't match.

> **Common mistake:** Padding a tricolon with a weak third item just to hit three, e.g., "fast, reliable, and good." "Good" adds nothing. A weak third item is worse than stopping at two — it tells the reader you ran out of real points.

> **Section summary:** Three matching, parallel items feel complete and are easy to remember, which is why the rule of three shows up in speeches, slogans, and slide decks across history. Make sure every item in your list has the same grammatical shape, and never add a filler third point just to complete the pattern.

## 4. Sharpening the Point: Contrast, Antithesis, and Rhetorical Questions

**Antithesis** (placing two opposite ideas side by side in the same sentence, usually in parallel grammar) works because contrast is how the brain notices things. A steady gray wall is invisible; a single red door on it is not. Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" speech is full of antithesis: he calls the Emancipation Proclamation "a great beacon light of hope" arriving after "a long night of captivity" — the contrast between darkness and light does more emotional work than a plain description of history ever could. Neil Armstrong's "one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind" is antithesis distilled into eleven words: small versus giant, one man versus all mankind.

> **Example:** A leader announcing a difficult reorganization says: "This plan will cost us headcount today so it can save the company tomorrow." The trade-off, stated as a contrast, is more persuasive and more honest-sounding than burying the bad news in a paragraph of qualifiers.

Antithesis is especially useful in professional writing for stating trade-offs and decisions honestly: "We can move fast, or we can move safely — this proposal chooses safely, and here's why." It signals that you have weighed the alternative, which builds credibility rather than looking like you avoided it.

A **rhetorical question** is a question asked not to get an answer, but to make the reader answer it in their own head — which makes them feel like they arrived at your conclusion themselves. "How much longer can we afford to lose customers to a competitor with a worse product?" does more persuasive work than "We are losing customers to a worse competitor," because the reader has to actively process an answer rather than passively receive a fact. Studies of writing that engages readers consistently note that self-generated conclusions are trusted more than conclusions handed to us — this is sometimes called the **self-persuasion effect**.

> **Best practice:** Use a rhetorical question to open a section you are about to answer in detail — it sets up curiosity and gives the reader a reason to keep going. "What actually determines whether a first-time manager succeeds?" followed by your three-point answer is far stronger than starting with the three points cold.

> **Common mistake:** Stacking multiple rhetorical questions in a row ("Isn't it time we changed? Don't you want better results? Wouldn't you rather succeed?"). One well-placed question invites reflection; three in a row starts to feel like pressure or a sales script, and readers tune it out.

> **Section summary:** Antithesis puts two opposite ideas next to each other so the contrast does the persuading, and it's a clean way to state a trade-off honestly. A single, well-timed rhetorical question gets the reader to reach your conclusion themselves, which they trust more than being told — but one is a tool, three in a row is a tic.

## 5. The Drumbeat: Repetition, Anaphora, and Cadence

Not all repetition is a writing mistake. Deliberate, structured repetition is one of the oldest persuasive devices there is. **Anaphora** (repeating the same word or phrase at the start of successive sentences or clauses) is the engine behind some of the most quoted lines in history. King's "I have a dream" appears at the start of clause after clause; Winston Churchill's wartime speech repeats "we shall fight" seven times. The repeated phrase becomes a drumbeat the listener's mind locks onto, so that each new ending lands with more force because the opening is already familiar.

> **Example:** A founder rallying a team after a hard quarter says: "We built this company when nobody believed in the idea. We built this company when we had no funding. We built this company when our first product failed. We can build our way through this too." The repeated opening turns four separate facts into one rising argument.

Anaphora works in professional writing at a much smaller scale too — three consecutive email sentences that all start "You will," three bullet points that all start "Stop," a slide where each line opens with the same verb. It costs nothing to set up and gives ordinary sentences a sense of momentum.

Underneath all of this sits **cadence** (plain meaning: the rhythm created by the length and stress pattern of your sentences). Read this sentence: "The plan works. It is simple. It ships on time." Three short, clipped sentences create a feeling of confidence and certainty. Now read: "The plan, which has been developed over several weeks with input from multiple stakeholders, is expected to work, is relatively simple in its execution, and should, under normal circumstances, ship approximately on time." Same information, but the long, hedging cadence makes it sound unsure of itself. Varying sentence length on purpose — a run of short sentences to build tension, then one longer sentence to release it — is one of the most underused tools in business writing, because most professional prose defaults to a flat, medium-length monotone.

```
  CADENCE AS RHYTHM
  ------------------
  Short. Short. Short.   -> builds tension, feels urgent, confident
  Medium. Medium.        -> neutral, informative, workmanlike
  One long flowing sentence that finally lets the reader exhale.
```

> **Best practice:** Read your draft aloud before sending anything important — a pitch, an announcement, a eulogy-style tribute email. If every sentence takes the same breath to say, break the pattern deliberately in at least one place.

> **Common mistake:** Confusing intentional anaphora with careless repetition — using the same weak transition word ("Also… Also… Also…") throughout a document is just monotonous, because it isn't building toward anything. The test: does the repeated phrase build momentum toward a climax? If not, it's a tic, not a device.

> **Section summary:** Repeating the same opening phrase across several sentences (anaphora) creates a drumbeat that builds emotional momentum, which is why it powers many of history's most quoted lines. Varying your sentence length on purpose — cadence — controls how confident or reflective your writing feels, and reading a draft aloud is the fastest way to catch a flat, monotone rhythm.

## 6. Framing for the Reader: Positive Framing and the "You" Focus

**Framing** (choosing which angle of a fact to present, since the same fact can be described in more than one true way) changes how people feel about identical information. The classic demonstration: a food product described as "85% fat-free" is chosen far more often than the exact same product described as "15% fat" — even though the numbers say the same thing. This is because **positive framing** (stating what will be gained, rather than what will be lost or avoided) tends to feel more motivating and more pleasant, especially when the audience is unfamiliar with the product or idea being described. Positive framing works because it's promotion-oriented — it points the reader toward progress and gain rather than toward fear or loss, and the emotional lift from that is often stronger than any purely logical difference in the message.

> **Example:** "This process eliminates 90% of manual errors" lands better than "This process is only 10% error-prone," even though they describe the identical result. The first frames the change as a gain (errors eliminated); the second frames it as a residual flaw (errors remaining).

Framing is not the same as lying — the underlying fact must stay true. It is a choice about which true angle to lead with, and it works best on unfamiliar offers where the audience doesn't already have a strong opinion; heavily familiar products or well-known facts are less swayed by framing, because people trust their existing knowledge over your wording.

The second lever in this section is the **"you" focus** (writing from the reader's point of view and interest, instead of the writer's). Compare "We are proud to announce our new dashboard" with "You can now see your whole week at a glance." The first sentence centers the company; the second centers the reader. Professional writers of persuasive copy consistently find that swapping "we/our" for "you/your" — without changing a single fact — measurably increases how engaged and personally addressed a reader feels, because it answers the question every reader is silently asking: "What does this mean for me?"

> **Best practice:** After drafting a persuasive email or proposal, count how many sentences start with "We" or "I" versus "You" or "Your." If "we" wins by a wide margin, rewrite the opening of several sentences to put the reader first.

> **Common mistake:** Using positive framing to hide a real loss, e.g., calling a price increase a "value optimization." Readers eventually decode euphemisms, and once they do, they distrust every other frame you use afterward. Positive framing should highlight a true gain, not disguise a true cost.

| Writer-centered ("we") | Reader-centered ("you") |
| --- | --- |
| We built this feature to save time. | You'll get an extra two hours back every week. |
| Our team reduced the error rate. | You'll see fewer mistakes on every order. |
| We are excited to launch this update. | You now have full control over your dashboard. |

> **Section summary:** The same true fact can be framed as a gain or a loss, and positive framing — especially on unfamiliar ideas — tends to feel more motivating. Writing from the reader's point of view ("you") instead of your own ("we") answers their unspoken question, "what's in it for me?", and makes your message land as personal rather than promotional.

## 7. Using Rhetoric Ethically: When Less Is More

Every device in this chapter shares one danger: overuse. A sentence with a metaphor, a tricolon, an antithesis, a rhetorical question, and a burst of anaphora all at once doesn't sound powerful — it sounds like it's trying too hard, and readers notice performance faster than they notice sincerity. Rhetoric works because it is a spice, not a meal. The strongest professional writing is plain writing with two or three well-placed devices, not a page where every line is doing a trick.

> **Analogy:** Rhetorical devices are like salt in cooking. The right pinch at the right moment brings out the flavor already in the dish. Salt on every bite of every course just tastes like salt.

There is also an ethical line running through every device here. Concrete language, metaphor, and framing are tools of clarity when they make a true thing easier to picture and understand. They become tools of manipulation the moment they are used to make a false or misleading thing feel true — a vague risk framed as a guaranteed gain, a real loss hidden inside a cheerful metaphor, a rhetorical question used to imply a false choice ("don't you want your team to succeed?" implying that disagreeing with the plan means wanting failure). The test is simple: would the device still feel fair to the reader if they could see exactly what you were doing and why? Ethical rhetoric survives that inspection. Manipulative rhetoric depends on the reader not noticing it.

> **Example:** A vendor says, "Our solution has helped teams like yours cut costs" (vague, unverifiable, technically true of almost anything) versus "Three of your closest competitors cut onboarding costs by 30% using this in the last year" (concrete, specific, checkable). Both are persuasive; only the second respects the reader enough to be falsifiable.

Before sending anything that leans on these devices, run it through a short check.

```
  THE STRAIGHT-FACE TEST
  -----------------------
  1. Is the underlying claim true?         -> if no, stop
  2. Would I explain my word choice        -> if no, soften it
     openly if asked?
  3. Does removing one device weaken       -> if no, remove it
     the sentence, or just shorten it?
```

> **Best practice:** Draft your persuasive message plainly first, with no devices at all. Then go back and add exactly one or two devices at the points that matter most — the opening line, the closing ask. This keeps the emphasis on your strongest moments instead of spreading it thin.

> **Common mistake:** Treating rhetorical skill as a substitute for a weak argument. No tricolon or antithesis can rescue a proposal that doesn't actually make sense; it will just make the weak argument more noticeable once the reader sees past the polish.

> **Section summary:** Rhetorical devices work best in small, well-placed doses — one or two per message, not one per sentence — because overuse reads as performance rather than conviction. Use them to make true things clearer and more memorable, never to make a false or hidden thing feel true, and always check that the underlying claim would survive being explained in plain sight.
