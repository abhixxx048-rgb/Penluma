A restaurant server leaves one mint with the check. Tips tick up slightly. She leaves two mints instead, and tips don't just double - they jump by roughly 14%. Then she tries one more thing: she drops off a single mint, starts to walk away, turns back, and says, "For you nice people, here's an extra mint." Tips climb even higher. Same mint. Same cost. Wildly different result.

That gap isn't luck. It's one of seven well-documented patterns behind almost every "yes" you've ever given - and every "yes" you've ever gotten.

## Why this matters

You are persuading people, and being persuaded, constantly. A colleague wants your sign-off. A vendor wants your budget. You want a stakeholder to approve your project or a recruiter to pick you. Most of us handle this by instinct, hoping the right words show up in the moment.

Psychologist Robert Cialdini spent years inside sales floors, charities, and marketing departments studying how influence actually works outside a lab. He found that people don't weigh every request from scratch. They lean on a small set of [mental shortcuts](/blog/communication-and-influence/06-cognitive-biases-in-persuasion) to decide, fast, whether something deserves a yes. Learn the shortcuts, and two things happen: you get harder to manipulate, and you get much better at presenting your own honest, valuable requests in a way people can actually hear.

One thing to hold onto before we start: these seven principles are not tricks. They only become manipulation when the signal behind them is fake. Used honestly, they just make a true, valuable message easier for a busy brain to accept.

## Reciprocity: we repay what we're given

**Reciprocity** is the pull to return a favor, gift, or concession with something similar - even one you never asked for. It's one of the oldest social rules in every human culture, and it's strong enough to work on a two-cent mint.

The mechanism matters more than the mint. It wasn't the size of the gift that moved the tip - two mints aren't worth much more than one. It was how personal the gesture felt. The server who turned back and made it about "you nice people" wasn't giving more, she was giving *warmer*, and warmth is what amplifies the obligation to give something back.

**At work:** A consultant sends a prospective client a genuinely useful two-page benchmark report before any contract is on the table. Nobody asked for it, and the client owes nothing on paper. But weeks later, when the pitch happens, that same client is far more inclined to give it a fair hearing.

The rule for using this honestly: give first, give something real, and give without conditions attached. The moment you say "I did you this favor, so now..." you've turned a gift into an invoice, and the effect collapses. Reciprocity works because it feels like generosity, not a loan with interest.

## Commitment and Consistency: we act like the person we said we'd be

Once you say or write down that you'll do something, you feel pressure to follow through - because backing out would mean seeing yourself as someone who doesn't keep their word. Nobody wants to be that person, even in small ways.

This is why the **foot-in-the-door technique** works so reliably: get someone to agree to a small, easy request, and they become far more likely to agree to a bigger, related one later. The first yes isn't the goal. It's the anchor everything after it gets measured against.

Public commitments carry even more weight than private ones. Saying something out loud in a meeting, or writing it where others can see it, raises the cost of quietly walking it back.

**At work:** A manager asks a hesitant team member to simply "share one idea" at the next planning meeting. Once they've spoken up and gotten a nod from the group, they're noticeably more willing to later volunteer to own that idea end-to-end - their own public statement has become part of how they now see themselves.

Used well, this looks like asking people to state their own goals out loud in front of peers, rather than assigning goals from above. Used badly, it looks like trapping someone into a small yes purely to extract a much bigger commitment they'd never have agreed to upfront. If the real ask wouldn't survive being stated plainly at the start, sneaking up on it isn't persuasion - it's a trap.

## Social Proof: we copy people who look like us

When you're uncertain, you outsource the decision to the crowd. It's an efficient shortcut - if a hundred similar people already tried something and it worked, it's a reasonable bet for you too. It's also why "bestseller" labels and star ratings work on you even when you know, intellectually, that they're marketing.

A hotel study shows exactly how much the "similar to you" part matters. A standard card asking guests to reuse towels to help the environment got about 35% compliance. Switch the message to a **descriptive norm** - a plain statement of what people actually do, like "75% of guests who stayed in this room reused their towels" - and compliance jumped to roughly 49%. The specific, similar-to-you crowd beat the generic appeal to save the planet.

**At work:** A product manager pitching a new internal tool opens with, "Twelve teams have already piloted this and cut their reporting time in half." The room visibly relaxes. The tool has already been vetted by people just like them, so the perceived risk drops.

The honest version of this is specificity: cite real, similar peers, not vague popularity. "Two other regional offices adopted this and saw fewer errors" beats "industry best practice suggests..." every time, because it's concrete and checkable. Vague claims like "everyone loves this" are easy to disbelieve and can backfire the moment someone checks.

## Authority: we defer to people who seem to know

You can't personally verify every claim you hear, so you lean on [credible signals of expertise](/blog/communication-and-influence/03-ethos-pathos-logos) - a title, a certification, a well-organized office, even a confident tone of voice. This shortcut usually serves you well, because genuine experts often do know more. The catch is that the shortcut responds to the *appearance* of authority almost as strongly as to the real thing.

Research on influence consistently ranks authority among the strongest of all seven principles, right alongside commitment and reciprocity - especially in professional settings where formal expertise genuinely matters.

**At work:** A consulting firm opens a client proposal with a one-line bio: "Led by a former Head of Ops at [a respected company], who ran this exact transformation for three Fortune 500 firms." The technical content of the pitch hasn't changed at all - but the client now reads every following page with more trust.

The honest use is simple: make your real expertise visible before you need it. A short, true credential in your bio. A mention of relevant experience in your introduction. "I spent four years doing exactly this." Let actual authority speak instead of borrowing signals you haven't earned - because a padded title collapses the moment someone asks a follow-up question you can't answer, and that kind of damage to trust rarely repairs.

## Liking: we say yes more easily to people we enjoy

This one sounds obvious, but the mechanics are worth knowing. Three things reliably build liking: **similarity** (shared background, hometown, or interests), **praise** (specific, sincere compliments - not flattery), and **cooperation** (working side by side toward a shared goal, which builds warmth faster than almost anything else).

Liking works like a lubricant, not an engine. It doesn't manufacture a yes out of nothing. It removes friction, so a genuinely good idea moves faster instead of getting stuck in unnecessary skepticism.

**At work:** Before a tense [cross-department negotiation](/blog/communication-and-influence/28-negotiation-and-everyday-persuasion), a manager spends the first five minutes asking real questions about the other team's challenges, finds genuine common ground - both teams are short-staffed this quarter - and only then makes the ask. The request itself is identical to what a cold email would have said. It lands completely differently.

[Build rapport](/blog/communication-and-influence/25-active-listening-and-building-rapport) during calm periods, not only when you need a favor. Give specific, honest praise ("that framework in the deck was really clear") instead of generic flattery. People are remarkably good at detecting insincerity, and a discovered fake compliment does more damage than no compliment at all.

## Scarcity: we want what seems about to disappear

Scarcity works because of **loss aversion** - the well-documented fact that losses feel roughly twice as painful as equivalent gains feel good. "This offer ends Friday" isn't just information. It reframes the decision from "should I gain this?" to "am I about to lose this?" - a far more motivating question for most brains.

Worth knowing before you reach for this one: cross-cultural research suggests scarcity is generally the *least* influential of the seven principles, compared to commitment, reciprocity, and authority. It's useful, but it's not your strongest lever, especially with audiences that value collective norms over individual advantage.

**At work:** A hiring manager tells a strong candidate, truthfully, "We have two other final-round interviews this week, and we're aiming to make an offer by Monday." No pressure tactic involved - just an honest timeline. But the real scarcity of time naturally speeds up the candidate's own decision-making.

Only use scarcity when it's real. If a deadline, budget cap, or limited slot genuinely exists, say so plainly and let people make an informed, faster decision - that's honest, and often kinder than ambiguity. Inventing fake urgency ("only today!") gets discovered easily in professional relationships, and it burns long-term credibility for a short-term push.

## Unity: we help the people we see as "us"

Unity is the seventh principle, added decades after the original six, and it's subtly different from liking. Liking is warmth toward another person. Unity is a shared sense of identity - the feeling that you and someone else belong to the same group, so helping them is really an extension of helping yourself. It's why people work harder for their hometown team, their own company's mission, or a cause tied to their alma mater than they would for an identical cause framed as belonging to strangers.

Unity runs deeper than ordinary liking because identity runs deeper than opinion. People will change their mind about a fact far more readily than they'll abandon a group they identify with.

**At work:** Before asking two newly merged departments to share a difficult workload, a leader spends a full meeting establishing a shared identity: "We are one delivery team now, not two departments." Only after that shared "we" is established does the request for cooperation land as a natural extension of who they already are, rather than a demand from outside.

Build and name a genuine shared identity before you need cooperation - a team charter, a shared mission, regular cross-team rituals. Say "we" and mean it, backed by real shared stakes. "We're a family here" language used to pressure people into unpaid overtime is unity without real belonging, and it's recognized as manipulation almost instantly - it breeds exactly the resentment it was trying to avoid.

## Common misconceptions

- **"These principles are just manipulation tactics."** They describe how people already decide, not tricks to override their judgment. The line into manipulation is crossed only when the signal is faked - a false deadline, a borrowed credential, a manufactured compliment.
- **"More persuasion techniques stacked together always work better."** They stack, but only when every piece is true. A pitch that opens with a shared identity, cites a real track record, references real peers, and closes with a genuine deadline works precisely because none of it is invented.
- **"Scarcity is the strongest lever, so lean on it hard."** It's actually the weakest of the seven in most comparative research. Authority, commitment, and reciprocity carry more weight, especially at work.
- **"Liking and unity are basically the same thing."** Liking is about the person. Unity is about the group you both belong to - and unity tends to move people even when they don't especially like the individual making the ask.

## How to use this

1. **Give before you ask.** Share a useful contact, review a colleague's work unprompted, or offer real help with no strings attached - reciprocity, done honestly, is just generosity that happens to build trust.
2. **Invite public, self-authored commitments.** Ask "what's one thing you're committing to this week?" instead of assigning the goal yourself. People honor their own stated words more than instructions from above.
3. **Cite specific peers, not vague popularity.** Replace "everyone loves this" with "two teams like yours already tried this and here's what happened."
4. **Make your real credentials visible early.** A short, honest line about relevant experience does more work than you'd expect - say it before you need it, not defensively after a challenge.
5. **Build rapport before you need a favor.** Invest in relationships in calm periods. Find genuine common ground and give specific praise, not flattery.
6. **Only state scarcity that's true.** If a deadline or limited slot is real, say so plainly. If it isn't real, don't invent one.
7. **Name the shared "we" before asking a group to pull together.** A team charter or shared mission, backed by fair treatment, makes cooperation feel like self-interest instead of a demand.

## Conclusion

Every one of these seven principles already runs inside your brain and everyone else's, whether you name it or not - the only choice you actually have is whether the signal behind it is real. Give a genuine gift, cite a genuine peer, name a genuine deadline, and you're not manipulating anyone; you're just making the truth easier to say yes to.

The mints, the towel cards, the "12 teams already piloted this" - none of them are tricks in themselves. They're proof that people accept honest, valuable requests faster when those requests come dressed in a shape the brain already trusts. Once you can spot the shape, you'll notice it working on you constantly - which is its own kind of protection.

Persuasion is only half the conversation, though. The other half is what happens once someone says yes and has to actually remember, believe, and act on it - which is where the real work of [communicating clearly](/blog/communication-and-influence/20-how-to-write-with-clarity), not just convincingly, begins.
