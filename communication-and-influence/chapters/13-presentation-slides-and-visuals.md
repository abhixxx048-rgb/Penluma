# 13. Slides and Visuals That Help Instead of Hurt

Most people think a presentation is the slides. It isn't. A presentation is a person talking to other people. The slides are a prop — a visual aid that should make the speaker's point land faster and stick longer. The moment slides stop helping and start being the show, the audience checks out. This chapter teaches you how to build slides that quietly do their job: support you, clarify your data, and then get out of the way.

## Slides Support the Speaker, Not Replace Them

Here is the single biggest mental shift you need to make: **a slide is not a document.** A document is meant to be read alone, in full, at the reader's own pace, with every detail spelled out because no one is there to explain it. A slide is meant to be glanced at for a few seconds while a live person talks. These are two completely different jobs, and most business slides fail because they try to do both at once — and end up doing neither well.

- **Slideument** — A hybrid slide that tries to work both as a live visual aid and as a standalone leave-behind document. It usually fails at both jobs because it is too text-heavy to glance at and too sparse to read alone.

If you have ever sat in a meeting and watched someone read their own bullet points out loud, you have watched a slideument in action. The audience silently races ahead, reading faster than the presenter can talk, then waits — bored — for the speaker to catch up to a sentence they already read thirty seconds ago. Nobody is listening at that point. They're just watching a slow-motion transcript.

> **Analogy:** Think of your slide like a highway billboard, not a magazine page. A billboard has one big idea, readable in two seconds at 60 miles an hour. A magazine page can hold paragraphs because the reader controls the pace and can linger. Your slide is a billboard — your mouth is the magazine.

The practical consequence is that you should design two separate things when a presentation matters: the **slide deck** (sparse, visual, meant to be seen while you talk) and, if people need details afterward, a **separate handout or leave-behind document** (full sentences, data tables, footnotes). Trying to merge them into one file is how you end up with 40-point walls of text that nobody can absorb.

> **Example:** A product manager is asked to "send the deck" before a board meeting so members can prepare. Instead of stuffing her presentation slides with extra explanatory paragraphs, she keeps the slides lean and attaches a two-page written brief with the same key numbers and full context. In the room, she presents the lean slides and talks. Board members who want to dig into details already have the brief.

> **Section summary:** A slide's job is to support you while you talk, not to read like a report. When you try to make one file do both jobs, you get a "slideument" that bores live audiences and still isn't a good stand-alone document. Split the two jobs into two different things.

## Cognitive Load and "Death by PowerPoint"

"Death by PowerPoint" is the popular name for the exhausted, glazed-over feeling an audience gets after sitting through slide after slide of dense bullet points, read aloud, slide after slide. It has a real psychological explanation, and understanding it will change how you build every slide from now on.

- **Cognitive load** — The total amount of mental effort your working memory is using at any given moment. Working memory is small and fills up fast — think of it as a desk with room for only a few papers at once.
- **Redundancy effect** — A finding from cognitive load research (associated with psychologist John Sweller) showing that people learn *less*, not more, when they have to process the same information twice at the same time — for example, reading identical words on a slide while also hearing you speak those same words aloud.

This sounds backward at first. Shouldn't seeing the words *and* hearing them help reinforce the message? Research on multimedia learning says no — when the visual channel and the audio channel are fighting over the exact same text, the brain has to reconcile two competing streams of identical information, and that reconciliation work steals mental bandwidth away from actually understanding the idea. This is closely related to a broader model, sometimes called the cognitive theory of multimedia learning, which holds that people process information through two separate channels — visual and verbal — and each channel has limited capacity. Overload either channel, or force them to duplicate each other, and comprehension drops.

```
   TWO CHANNELS, LIMITED CAPACITY

   EYES  ---> [ visual channel ]  ---\
                                      >---> WORKING MEMORY (small!)
   EARS  ---> [ verbal channel ] ---/

   Text-heavy slide + speaker reading it aloud
   = both channels carrying the SAME words
   = channels compete instead of working together
   = audience understands LESS, not more
```

This is precisely why a slide packed with bullet points that the presenter then reads aloud is one of the worst possible formats — it maximizes redundancy and overloads working memory at the same time. Cognitive research on visual complexity also suggests a rough ceiling: once a slide holds much more than about six distinct objects or chunks of information, people stop being able to take it all in at a glance and start having to consciously count and sort, which burns even more mental energy.

> **Common mistake:** Writing your full spoken script as bullet points on the slide "so I don't forget what to say." This guarantees you'll either read it (boring, redundant) or ignore it (so why is it there?). Put your notes in the presenter notes field or on an index card — not on the screen the audience is staring at.

> **Best practice:** Before adding anything to a slide, ask: "Does this help people understand the point faster, or is it just filling space?" If it's not pulling weight, cut it. Silence and white space on a slide are not failures — they're what let the one important thing stand out.

> **Section summary:** Your brain has a small amount of working memory, split between what you see and what you hear. When a slide's text duplicates the speaker's words, the two channels collide and people understand less. Simple, sparse slides protect the audience's limited mental bandwidth.

## One Idea Per Slide, Less Text, Bigger Visuals, High Contrast

Once you accept that slides exist to support you, not narrate for you, the design rules become simple and almost mechanical.

### One idea per slide

Each slide should make exactly one point. If you find yourself wanting to cover two ideas on one slide, that's a sign you need two slides. This isn't wasteful — slides are free. Nobody has ever complained "there were too many slides" when each one was clear; they complain when each slide was *overloaded*.

### Less text

A well-known, widely repeated rule of thumb from investor and entrepreneur Guy Kawasaki is the **10/20/30 rule**: aim for around 10 slides, speak for no more than 20 minutes, and never use a font smaller than 30 points. That last number is the real enforcer — if your text has to be smaller than 30-point font to fit, you have too much text, full stop. Large font forces brevity, and brevity forces you to say the extra explanation out loud instead of dumping it on the screen.

Presentation design author Garr Reynolds, known for his book *Presentation Zen*, makes a related point: audiences didn't come to read, they came to hear you. Any competent reader can silently read bullet points faster than you can say them — so bullet-point slides make the live speaker redundant. Reynolds pushes toward slides with very little text, and what text remains is large and simple, closer to a caption than a paragraph.

### Bigger visuals

A relevant photo, diagram, or simple chart usually communicates faster than a paragraph of text — and it doesn't compete with your voice the way text does, because your brain doesn't have to "read" a picture the way it reads words. Use one large, high-quality visual instead of four small cluttered ones.

### High contrast

Text must be easy to read from the back row, on a mediocre projector, in a lit room. Dark text on a light background (or the reverse) with strong contrast, generous spacing, and no busy background images behind the text is non-negotiable. A gorgeous slide that nobody in row 12 can actually read has failed at its one job.

| Weak slide habit | What to do instead |
| --- | --- |
| Six bullet points, sub-bullets, and a clip-art icon | One short headline sentence and one supporting image or chart |
| 12-point font to cram in more text | 30+ point font, forcing you to cut and simplify |
| Pastel text on a busy photo background | Solid background, high-contrast text color |
| Reading the slide word-for-word aloud | Slide shows the point; your voice adds the explanation and story |

> **Example:** A sales lead is presenting quarterly results. Instead of a slide titled "Q3 Performance" with eight bullet points of numbers, she shows one slide with a single large sentence — "Renewals grew 34% after we simplified onboarding" — and one clean bar chart underneath it. She then talks through the "why" and the "what's next" out loud, which is information the slide never needed to hold.

> **Section summary:** Good slides carry one idea, use large text, favor images and simple charts over paragraphs, and stay high-contrast and readable from a distance. If a slide needs a small font to fit everything, it needs editing, not a smaller font.

## Data Visualization Basics

Numbers are often the most important part of a business presentation, and they're also where slides go wrong most often — usually because the presenter copies a giant spreadsheet screenshot straight into the deck. A chart's job is the same as a slide's job: make one point land fast.

### Pick the right chart for the point you're making

- **Bar chart** — Best for comparing separate categories side by side (e.g., sales by region).
- **Line chart** — Best for showing a trend or change over time (e.g., revenue by month).
- **Pie or donut chart** — Best only for showing how a few parts make up a whole — and only when there are very few slices (2–4). It's one of the most overused and misread chart types, since the human eye is genuinely bad at comparing angles and curved slices.
- **Scatter plot** — Best for showing the relationship between two different numeric measures (e.g., ad spend vs. signups).

Choose the chart type based on the sentence you want the audience to walk away with. If your point is "we grew steadily every quarter," a line chart shows that shape instantly. If your point is "the Northeast dramatically outperformed every other region," a bar chart makes that one bar visually jump out. Using the wrong chart type forces the audience to work to find the story you already know — and in a live presentation, they won't do that work.

### Remove clutter

Design theorist Edward Tufte coined the term **chartjunk** for decorative chart elements that add no information — 3D effects, heavy gridlines, drop shadows, redundant labels, and busy background colors. He proposed thinking in terms of a **data-ink ratio**: the proportion of ink (or pixels) on the chart that is actually showing data, versus ink spent on decoration.

- **Chartjunk** — Any visual element on a chart — 3D bevels, gradients, unnecessary gridlines, shadows — that doesn't help convey the data and often actively distracts from it.
- **Data-ink ratio** — The share of a chart's visual "ink" that is dedicated to showing actual data, rather than decoration. Higher is generally cleaner and clearer.

In practice: strip 3D effects off every bar and pie chart (3D distorts how the eye judges size and is almost always a readability downgrade), remove gridlines you don't need, drop the legend if you can label data directly instead, and cut every data series that isn't relevant to today's point. Note there's nuance here — some research on "embellished" charts found that certain tasteful visual flourishes can actually help audiences remember a chart later. The rule isn't "zero decoration ever," it's "nothing on this chart that doesn't serve the message."

### Highlight the point

Once the clutter is gone, actively point the eye at your conclusion. Use color deliberately: make every bar gray except the one you're talking about, which you color boldly. Add a simple callout arrow or a one-line label directly on the chart that states the takeaway in words ("Northeast: 2x the next region") instead of leaving the audience to infer it from axis labels alone.

> **Analogy:** A cluttered, undirected chart is like handing someone a full box of puzzle pieces and asking them to find the cat picture while you keep talking. A well-designed chart is like handing them the finished picture with the cat circled in red. Same data, radically different amount of work for your audience.

> **Common mistake:** Pasting a raw spreadsheet or a dashboard screenshot straight into a slide "because the data's already there." Dense spreadsheets are unreadable from six feet away and bury your one important number inside forty others. Rebuild the one number or trend that matters as a clean, simple chart instead.

> **Section summary:** Match the chart type to the point you want to make — bars for comparison, lines for trends, pie only for a few parts of a whole. Strip out decorative clutter that adds no information, and then use color and labels to point the audience straight at the takeaway.

## The Presenter Is the Focus

Even with perfect slides, it's easy to accidentally make the screen the star of the show instead of yourself. Watch how audience attention behaves in a dark room with a bright screen: eyes are pulled toward light and movement, which means a giant glowing slide will always compete with you for attention unless you actively manage it.

A few habits keep you, the human, as the actual center of the presentation:

- **Stand where you can be seen, not hidden behind a lectern or laptop.** Your face and gestures carry meaning that a slide can't.
- **Look at the audience, not the screen.** Presenters who turn their back to talk to their own slide lose the room's attention and their vocal energy drops because they're talking away from the microphone and the people.
- **Advance slides on your terms, not the other way around.** Reveal a slide, let it land for a second, then talk — don't let a new slide's arrival interrupt your own sentence.
- **Use blackouts strategically.** Many presentation tools let you press "B" (in PowerPoint) or a similar key to blank the screen entirely. When you want the room's full attention on a story, a hard question, or a moment of connection, black out the slide. Suddenly there is nothing to look at but you.

> **Best practice:** Before an important talk, physically rehearse where you'll stand relative to the screen, and practice at least one deliberate blackout moment — usually right before or during your most important point — so the room's attention snaps to you exactly when it matters most.

> **Example:** A consultant pitching a turnaround plan reaches the emotional core of her pitch — the human cost of the client's current process failures. She blacks out the screen, steps toward the table, and tells a 30-second story about a real customer affected by the problem. Only after the story lands does she bring the next slide back up to show the proposed fix.

> **Section summary:** A bright screen will always fight you for attention unless you manage it — by standing visibly, facing the audience, and occasionally blacking out the slide entirely. The presenter, not the projector, should be what people remember.

## Handling "No Slides" Situations

Sooner or later, the projector will fail, the file won't open, someone will hand you a microphone with zero warning, or you'll be in a room too small or too informal for a screen at all. Presenters who have built their entire talk as a slideument — a script glued to bullet points — fall apart here, because they've never practiced separating the talk from the slides. Presenters who've internalized "the talk is what I say, the slides are just a visual bonus" barely notice the difference.

The fix is to always know your talk's structure independent of any slide. Before any important talk, be able to state out loud, from memory, in one sentence each: your opening hook, your three main points, and your closing ask. If you can do that without looking at anything, you can deliver the entire talk with no slides at all — you'll simply lose the charts and images, not your argument.

```
  YOUR TALK'S BACKBONE (should survive with zero slides)

  [ Hook ]  -->  [ Point 1 ] -> [ Point 2 ] -> [ Point 3 ]  -->  [ Ask / Close ]

  Slides only ever decorate points along this line.
  If the screen dies, the line still holds.
```

Practical steps for a no-slides moment: verbally signal the structure you'd normally show visually ("There are three reasons this matters — first...second...third..."), use your hands and simple gestures to represent comparisons or steps you'd normally chart, and if numbers matter, state the single most important number slowly and repeat it, rather than trying to describe an entire chart's shape in words.

> **Best practice:** Always bring a low-tech backup — a one-page printed handout with your key numbers or diagram, or simply a whiteboard marker. If you know your content, a marker and a whiteboard beat a broken projector every time.

> **Common mistake:** Treating a tech failure as an excuse to apologize repeatedly and stall. A brief, calm acknowledgment ("Looks like we've lost the projector — let me just talk you through this") followed by confidently continuing reads as competence. Long apologies and visible panic read as a much bigger problem than the actual broken cable.

> **Section summary:** Know the spoken structure of your talk well enough to deliver it with zero slides, because equipment fails. If you built your talk to depend on reading bullet points, a dead projector kills your talk; if slides were always just decoration, you barely miss a beat.

## Assertion-Evidence Style

If you want one concrete template to replace the traditional "topic + bullet points" slide, look at the **assertion-evidence approach**, developed and researched by Penn State engineering professor Michael Alley.

- **Assertion-evidence style** — A slide design method where the headline is a full, specific sentence stating your conclusion (the "assertion"), and the body of the slide is a single visual — a chart, diagram, image, or very short data table — that backs up that sentence (the "evidence"). There are no bullet-point lists.

Compare the two approaches on the same content:

| Traditional style | Assertion-evidence style |
| --- | --- |
| Headline: "Q3 Results" (a topic, not a message) | Headline: "Renewals grew 34% after we simplified onboarding" (a complete claim) |
| Body: bullet list of five stats | Body: one clean bar chart showing the growth, nothing else |
| Audience has to infer what matters | Audience is told the point, then shown the proof |

```
  ASSERTION-EVIDENCE SLIDE LAYOUT

  +----------------------------------------------------+
  |  Renewals grew 34% after we simplified onboarding   |   **Best practice:** For your next important slide, delete the topic-phrase headline and force yourself to write the one full sentence you actually want the audience to believe after seeing this slide. If you can't write that sentence, you don't yet know what the slide is for — which is a sign to keep working on your argument, not your formatting.

> **Example:** An engineer presenting a root-cause analysis to leadership changes her headline from "System Downtime Overview" to "A single misconfigured cache setting caused 90% of last month's downtime." Under it, she places one simple timeline chart marking the spike. The room understands the conclusion before she says a word — her talk then explains the "why" and "what we're fixing," which the slide never needed to hold.

> **Section summary:** Assertion-evidence slides replace vague topic headlines and bullet lists with a full-sentence conclusion and a single supporting visual. Research shows audiences understand and remember this style better, because it hands them the point immediately instead of making them piece it together from a list.
