# Chapter 6 Research Brief: Interleaving and Desirable Difficulties

## Key concepts (plain definitions)

**Blocking (massed practice):** Studying one type of problem or category repeatedly before moving to the next (AAA BBB CCC). Feels smooth and confident.

**Interleaving (mixed practice):** Shuffling different but related problem types or categories within one session (ABC ABC BAC). Feels harder and more error-prone in the moment.

**Desirable difficulties (Robert A. Bjork, 1994):** Learning conditions that *slow you down and increase errors during practice* but produce stronger, more durable, more transferable long-term learning. The main ones: spacing, interleaving, retrieval practice (testing), varying study conditions, and reducing feedback.

**Contextual interference:** A term from motor-learning research (borrowed from Battig, 1966) describing the "interference" created when you mix tasks. High interference hurts short-term acquisition but helps long-term retention - the same paradox as interleaving.

**Metacognitive illusion:** Learners systematically misjudge which method works. Blocking *feels* more effective because it feels fluent, so people prefer the worse strategy.

## Studies & evidence (author, year, finding, numbers)

**Rohrer & Taylor (2007),** *Instructional Science* 35(6), 481–498. College students learned to compute the volume of four obscure solids (e.g., wedge, spheroid, half-cone). In Experiment 2, practice was blocked or mixed. On an unannounced test **one week later, the mixed group scored 63% vs 20% for the blocked group** - roughly triple - even though during practice the blocked group actually solved problems *more* accurately. Landmark study establishing interleaving in math.

**Kornell & Bjork (2008),** "Learning Concepts and Categories: Is Spacing the 'Enemy of Induction'?" *Psychological Science* 19(6). Participants studied **six paintings from each of 12 artists**, shown either blocked (all six by one artist together) or interleaved (mixed across artists). On a later test naming the artist of *new, unseen* paintings, **interleaving beat blocking** for learning painters' styles. The twist: after the test, roughly **78–80% of participants still believed blocking had helped them more** (only about 20% correctly judged interleaving superior) - the classic metacognitive illusion. This popularized interleaving for perceptual/inductive learning.

**Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic (2015),** *Journal of Educational Psychology* 107(3), 900–908. A real classroom study with **126 seventh-graders** over ~3 months. Interleaved practice beat blocked on both an immediate and a delayed unannounced test. *Scientific American*'s summary: about **25% higher scores at one day, and ~76% higher at one month**.

**Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2020),** *Journal of Educational Psychology* 112(1), 40–52. The strongest evidence: a **randomized controlled trial of 787 students in 54 classes across 5 schools**, over a 4-month period. One month later, on an unannounced test, **the interleaved group scored 61% vs 38% for blocked, d = 0.83** - a large effect for a real-world education intervention.

**Shea & Morgan (1979),** *JEP: Human Learning and Memory* 5, 179–187. Founding motor-learning study: random (interleaved) practice of movement patterns produced *worse* performance during practice but *better* retention and transfer than blocked practice - the contextual-interference effect, later folded into Bjork's desirable-difficulties framework.

**Brunmair & Richter (2019),** "Similarity Matters: A Meta-Analysis of Interleaved Learning," *Psychological Bulletin.* 59 studies, 238 effect sizes; overall **interleaving effect g = 0.42 (moderate)**. Crucially, it varied by material: **paintings/visual categories g = 0.67 (large); math effects were small and ranged from negative to positive**. Interleaving works best when the categories are *confusable* and need discrimination.

## Myths / contested points (with honest nuance)

- **"Interleaving always beats blocking."** No. Brunmair & Richter show the benefit is reliable for confusable *categories* (paintings, bird species, math problem *types*), weaker and inconsistent for texts, vocabulary, and some math contexts. The effect depends on *similarity between items*.
- **"Never block."** Also wrong. You typically need some blocked practice *first* to encode the basic procedure; interleaving is most valuable *after* you can execute each type, when the challenge is choosing the right approach.
- **"It feels bad, so it must be less effective."** The signature finding across all these studies is that *fluency during study is a false signal.* Feeling slow and error-prone is often the sign it's working.
- **Math effect sizes are debated.** Rohrer's own classroom RCTs show huge gains (d = 0.83), but the meta-analysis found math effects smaller/variable - likely because Rohrer carefully interleaves *problem types* (which forces strategy selection), not random unrelated skills.

## Real-world examples & analogies

- **Baseball batting cage (Hall, Domingues & Cavazos, 1994):** Batters facing *randomly mixed* pitch types (fastball/curveball/changeup) hit better in later games than those who drilled one pitch at a time - even though block-drillers looked better in practice.
- **The art-gallery analogy:** To learn to tell Monet from Manet, you don't stare at 20 Monets in a row; you shuffle painters so your brain is forced to spot what makes a Monet *a Monet* by contrast.
- **The junk-drawer vs. gym analogy:** Blocking is like organizing a drawer - everything neat and easy. Interleaving is like a gym circuit: your muscles complain, but that varied strain is exactly what builds real strength.

## Practical how-to points

- **Interleave problem *types*, not random topics.** Mix related, confusable categories so you must *decide which method applies* - that discrimination is the mechanism.
- **Block briefly, then interleave.** Learn each type once, then shuffle.
- **Mix old and new material** in each session (this also produces spacing automatically).
- **Vary conditions** - locations, problem formats, contexts - to make knowledge flexible.
- **Distrust the feeling of ease.** If study feels effortful and you're making errors, you're likely in the productive zone.

## Common mistakes learners make on this topic

- **Trusting fluency.** Choosing blocking because it feels good - the exact illusion Kornell & Bjork documented.
- **Interleaving too early,** before understanding each type, so it becomes confusing noise rather than useful discrimination.
- **Interleaving unrelated junk** ("history, then calculus, then Spanish") and expecting the categorization benefit - that's just task-switching, not true interleaving.
- **Giving up during practice** because scores dip, not realizing the payoff shows up only on delayed tests.
- **Over-applying it** to material where evidence is weak (rote vocabulary, dissimilar items).
