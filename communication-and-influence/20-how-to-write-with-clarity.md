You hit send on a [three-paragraph email](/blog/communication-and-influence/22-persuasive-emails-and-proposals). Relief washes over you - it's out of your hands now. Except it isn't out of your reader's hands: they open it, skim the first line, don't find the point, and either guess, ignore it, or reply asking the exact question you thought you'd already answered.

That email was **sent**. It was never **read** - not really. There's a difference, and almost everyone confuses the two.

Clarity closes that gap. It's not a style preference for people who like tidy sentences. It's the difference between writing that gets acted on and writing that gets skimmed, misread, or quietly ignored.

## Why this matters

Every unclear sentence you send is a small tax on the person reading it. They have to slow down, reread, guess at what you meant, and hope they guessed right. Multiply that by every email, memo, and Slack message you send in a year, and it adds up to a lot of wasted attention - theirs and, eventually, yours, when they come back confused and you have to explain it all over again.

Teams that communicate clearly tend to move faster and argue less, simply because fewer decisions get relitigated over a misunderstanding. That's not a coincidence. Clarity isn't decoration on top of good ideas - it's what lets good ideas actually travel from your head into someone else's, intact.

The good news: clear writing is a skill, not a talent. It's learnable in an afternoon and improvable for the rest of your life.

## Why most writing is hard to read

Nobody sits down intending to write something confusing. Unclear writing happens for three very ordinary reasons.

**You write in the order you thought, not the order your reader needs.** Your first draft rambles through context, backstory, and caveats before reaching the point - because that's the order the idea unfolded in your head. Your reader doesn't need your thought process. They need the destination.

**You overestimate how much your reader already knows, and how much patience they have.** You assume they remember the acronym, recall the backstory, understand the stakes - so you skip explaining any of it. Or you reach for jargon that signals "I know what I'm talking about" instead of plainly saying what they need to know.

**You rarely edit.** A first draft is a thinking document. It reflects your process, not your reader's needs. Sent as-is, it forces the reader to do the editing work themselves - rereading it, hunting for the point, guessing at what you meant.

Think of unclear writing as handing someone a jigsaw puzzle instead of the finished picture. All the pieces are technically there. But you've made them do assembly work that was your job to do first.

## Put the point first: BLUF

**BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front)** is a simple structure: you state your main point, request, or conclusion in the very first sentence, before any background or supporting detail.

It started in military and intelligence writing, where officers had seconds - not minutes - to pull the one decision-relevant fact out of a report. It spread into business for the same reason: people skim. Most readers decide within the first few seconds whether a message is worth their full attention. If your point sits in paragraph three, most people never get there.

Here's the shift, side by side:

| Natural order (how you thought it) | BLUF order (what they need) |
| --- | --- |
| Context and background | The point - decision, ask, or result |
| More context | Key reason one |
| The build-up | Key reason two |
| Finally, the point | Supporting context, if needed |

**Example.** A project manager needs to tell a director that a launch will slip.

*Buried version:* "The vendor had some delays in Q2, and then we had two engineers out sick, and the QA environment had an outage last week, which pushed back testing, so..."

*BLUF version:* "The launch will slip two weeks, to July 19. Root cause: a vendor delay plus a QA environment outage. We don't need budget or headcount - just your sign-off on the new date."

The second version lets the director act after one read. The first makes them dig for it.

A quick test before you write anything longer than three sentences: *if my reader only reads my first sentence, what's the one thing they must know?* Write that sentence first. Everything else is there to support it.

One common objection: "I need to build up to it, or it sounds too blunt." In most professional settings, it's the opposite. Burying the point reads as evasive or disorganized. Leading with it reads as confident and considerate of your reader's time.

## The building blocks of plain language

**Plain language** is writing designed so your reader can find what they need, understand it the first time, and use it - achieved through simple words, short sentences, and clear structure. It is not dumbed-down writing. It's precise writing.

The U.S. government adopted plain-language standards after realizing that unclear public documents were quietly costing citizens time, money, and trust. A majority of Americans struggle to understand typical government or legal documents as written. The same tax applies inside your company: unclear policies, emails, and reports slow everyone down, one confused reader at a time.

Here are the moves that actually make language plain.

**Short words beat long words.** "Use" instead of "utilize." "Help" instead of "facilitate." "Start" instead of "commence." Long words feel formal, but they cost your reader extra processing time for zero extra meaning.

**Short sentences beat long ones.** Past roughly 20-25 words, especially with multiple clauses, a sentence starts to strain working memory. Break it into two. This one habit alone can transform a document.

**Active voice beats passive voice.** In active voice, the subject does the action: "The team missed the deadline." In passive voice, the actor disappears: "The deadline was missed." Passive voice adds words and often signals someone is quietly avoiding responsibility ("mistakes were made"). Active voice says who did what - almost always more useful, and more honest.

**Strong verbs beat verb-plus-noun phrases.** "Conduct an analysis of" becomes "analyze." "Make a decision" becomes "decide." "Provide assistance to" becomes "help." Hunting down these bloated phrases and replacing them with one strong verb is one of the fastest ways to cut word count without losing meaning.

**Concrete nouns beat abstract ones.** "Deliverables," "synergies," and "solutioning" float above reality. "The report," "the two teams working together," and "fixing the problem" land on it. Concrete language lets your reader picture the actual thing you mean.

**Cut jargon and filler.** Jargon works fine between experts who share it, but it excludes and confuses everyone else - and it's often used, consciously or not, to sound impressive rather than to communicate. Filler ("basically," "in order to," "at this point in time," "due to the fact that") adds length without adding meaning.

**One idea per paragraph.** A paragraph is a container for a single idea. When it tries to hold three, your reader has to sort them out mid-read. Split it. A one-sentence paragraph isn't a mistake - sometimes it's exactly the right size.

Here's the difference in practice:

| Wordy | Plain |
| --- | --- |
| Utilize existing resources | Use what we have |
| Due to the fact that sales declined | Because sales declined |
| A decision was made by leadership | Leadership decided |
| We are currently in the process of reviewing | We are reviewing |
| Provide clarification regarding | Clarify |
| In order to facilitate a smoother onboarding experience | To make onboarding smoother |

None of this makes writing simplistic. It makes writing fast to absorb - which is the entire point.

## How to tell if your writing is too hard to read

You don't have to guess whether your writing is clear. There's a number for that.

A **readability score** estimates how difficult a piece of text is to read, based mostly on two things: average sentence length and average syllables per word. The most common tools are the Flesch Reading Ease score (0-100, higher is easier) and the Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, which maps difficulty onto a U.S. school grade. You don't need to calculate either by hand - Google Docs, Microsoft Word, and free online tools will score any text instantly.

As a rough guide: general business and news writing sits around a Flesch score of 60-70, roughly an 8th-to-10th-grade reading level. Legal and academic writing often scores 30 or below - dense and slow going. Healthcare and government material aimed at broad audiences targets 80 or higher.

The lesson isn't "write for children." It's: match your reading level to your [actual audience](/blog/communication-and-influence/04-how-to-know-your-audience), and default to simpler than you think you need. Almost everyone - including expert readers - reads faster and remembers more from simpler text, because they're busy, not because they're unsophisticated.

A compliance team once rewrote an internal policy FAQ after discovering it scored a Flesch-Kincaid grade level of 16 - post-graduate. Employee tickets asking "what does this mean?" dropped by more than a third after they rewrote it down to a grade level of 9, without removing a single actual requirement. Same rules. Far fewer confused employees.

## Edit ruthlessly: cut at least 10%

Clear writing isn't just about how you write the first draft. It's about what you remove afterward.

William Zinsser, in his classic *On Writing Well*, argued that most first drafts are cluttered with words doing no real work - and that a skilled editor can often cut a large share of a draft's length without losing a single fact. His method: go through every sentence and mentally bracket anything not earning its place - the redundant adverb ("smile happily"), the throat-clearing phrase ("it is important to note that"), the adjective stating something already obvious ("tall skyscraper"). Then cut what's bracketed.

A practical target: after your first draft, **[cut at least 10% of the words](/blog/clear-thinking-and-expression/07-editing-turning-a-messy-draft-into-something-clear)** - often more. This isn't about making the piece shorter for its own sake. It's a forcing function that catches clutter you didn't notice while drafting, because when you're generating ideas, your brain is focused on getting them down, not on trimming them.

Editing is like packing a suitcase. Your first pass throws everything you might conceivably need onto the bed. The skill is the second pass - taking things back out until only what actually earns its place remains.

A simple editing pass:

1. **Read it aloud.** Anywhere you run out of breath or stumble, the sentence is too long or too tangled.
2. **Delete the first paragraph.** Often your real opening point is in paragraph two - the first paragraph was just you warming up.
3. **Hunt for "that," "very," "really," "basically," "in order to."** Most can be deleted with no loss of meaning.
4. **Find every passive sentence and ask who's doing this.** Rewrite in active voice where the actor matters.
5. **Check each paragraph for one idea.** If it holds two, split it.

A common mistake here is editing only for typos and grammar while skipping structural editing. A grammatically perfect sentence can still be clutter. Ruthless editing cuts whole phrases and sentences, not just commas.

## Format for skim-readers

Even perfectly plain sentences can get lost if they arrive as one dense wall of text. Most professional readers skim before they read - their eyes scan the shape of a document before deciding whether, and how deeply, to engage. Formatting is how you design for that scan.

- **Headings** act as a table of contents for the eye. A reader should grasp your document's structure from headings alone.
- **[Bullets](/blog/communication-and-influence/13-presentation-slides-and-visuals)** pull lists out of paragraph form so each item is instantly separable. Use them for genuine lists - steps, options, criteria - not as a way to avoid writing full sentences.
- **Bold** highlights the two or three phrases per page that matter most if someone reads nothing else. Overusing bold defeats the purpose - if everything is bold, nothing stands out.
- **White space** - short paragraphs, line breaks - gives the eye somewhere to rest and signals "this is manageable," which measurably increases the odds someone starts reading at all.

An engineering lead once sent a weekly status update as one 300-word paragraph. Reformatted with a one-line bottom line, three bolded headers ("Shipped," "Blocked," "Next"), and bullets under each, the same information took readers a third of the time to process - and the lead started getting faster, more specific replies, because people could finally see exactly what needed a response.

Before sending anything longer than a paragraph, glance at it for two seconds as if you were the busy recipient. If you can't tell what it's about from that glance, add a heading, a bold lead sentence, or bullets until you can.

## Common misconceptions

**"Getting straight to the point is rude."** In most professional contexts, the opposite is true. Burying your point reads as evasive; leading with it reads as confident and respectful of your reader's time.

**"Plain language means dumbing it down."** Plain language is precise language. You can explain something genuinely complex in short sentences and simple words without losing any accuracy - you're removing friction, not substance.

**"A high reading-level score proves I sound smart."** It usually proves the opposite - that you haven't yet done the work of making a complex idea simple. Simplicity, not complexity, is what actually signals mastery.

**"Editing is just fixing typos."** Grammar and structure are different jobs. A grammatically flawless paragraph can still bury its point under clutter. Real editing cuts whole phrases and sentences, not just commas.

## How to use this

1. **Write your bottom line first**, in one sentence, before you write anything else. Everything after it should support that sentence.
2. **Swap five long words for short ones** in your next email: utilize → use, facilitate → help, commence → start.
3. **Run one sentence-length check.** If a sentence tops 25 words, split it into two.
4. **Find one passive sentence and make it active** by naming who did the thing.
5. **Run a readability check** on your next company-wide or customer-facing message, and treat a high score as a signal to simplify.
6. **Cut 10% of your next draft** on a second pass - read it aloud and remove anything you stumble over.
7. **Add one heading, one bold sentence, or a bulleted list** to the next document you send that's longer than a paragraph.

## Conclusion

Notice what didn't change in any of the examples above: the facts. The launch still slipped two weeks. The policy still had the same requirements. Nothing about the underlying truth got simpler - only the path it took to reach another person's brain. That's the real definition of clarity: not less information, just less friction between what you know and what they understand.

The one true test isn't how your writing reads to you - it's whether your reader ever has to ask "wait, what did you mean?" Try it on the very next message you write. And once your point actually lands, a new problem shows up: landing on someone's desk clearly is not the same as landing on their priority list. Getting people to act on what they now understand - that's a different game, built on trust, [framing](/blog/communication-and-influence/07-framing-in-communication), and timing rather than sentence structure.
