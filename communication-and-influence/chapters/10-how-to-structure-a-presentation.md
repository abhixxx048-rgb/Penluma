# 10. Building a Talk That Sticks: Structure and Openings

Think about the last five presentations you sat through. Chances are you remember almost nothing from four of them. Not because the speakers were unintelligent — most professionals know their material cold. They fail for a much simpler reason: nobody gave the audience a map. Great communicators don't just have good content; they have good **architecture**. This chapter teaches you how to build a talk from the studs up — from the single sentence that justifies the whole thing, to the structure that carries it, to the opening seconds that decide whether anyone is still listening.

> **Key takeaway:** A talk isn't a container you pour information into — it's a journey you design so the audience arrives somewhere on purpose.

## 10.1 One Core Message: Finding Your "Ask"

Before you write a single slide or bullet point, answer one question: **if my audience forgets everything else, what is the one thing I need them to remember or do?** This is your **core message** (the single idea your entire talk exists to deliver), sometimes called your **throughline**.

- **Core message (throughline)** — The one idea that connects every part of your talk into a single coherent picture — the thing the whole talk is "about."
- **The ask** — The specific action or decision you want your audience to take after hearing you — what should change in the real world because you spoke.

Chris Anderson, the longtime curator of TED, built this into a hard rule for TED speakers: your throughline should fit in fifteen words or fewer. If you can't compress your idea that small, you don't have one talk — you have several talks fighting each other for airtime. Anderson's argument is blunt: a talk is not a grab-bag of everything you know about a subject. It is one idea, built carefully, so that the listener and the speaker "arrive together" at the end.

This matters more in professional life than people assume. A product manager pitching a roadmap doesn't need to explain every feature under consideration — they need one sentence: "We should build the mobile app before the analytics dashboard because it unlocks 80% of new revenue." A job candidate answering "tell me about yourself" doesn't need a biography — they need one throughline: "I'm a data analyst who turns messy spreadsheets into decisions executives actually act on." Everything else in the talk exists only to support that one sentence.

> **Example:** Before a budget review, a finance manager writes their core message on a sticky note above their laptop: "We should cut the events budget by 30% and reinvest it in paid search." Every slide that doesn't serve that sentence gets deleted. The presentation goes from 22 slides to 9, and the room actually makes a decision instead of nodding politely and moving on.

> **Best practice:** Write your core message as a complete sentence, not a topic. "Q3 marketing results" is a topic. "We should double down on referrals because they convert three times better than paid ads" is a message. Only the second one can drive a decision.

> **Common mistake:** Trying to cover everything "just in case someone asks." This is the single biggest reason talks feel bloated and forgettable. A talk with five core messages has zero core messages — the audience remembers none of them because their brains have nowhere to file five competing ideas.

> **Section summary:** Before you build anything, decide the one sentence your whole talk is trying to prove or the one action you want people to take. Every fact, story, and slide should serve that single sentence. If it doesn't, cut it.

## 10.2 Classic Structures: Choosing Your Skeleton

Once you know your message, you need a shape to carry it. A **structure** (the underlying skeleton that organizes your points in a logical order) does two jobs: it helps you build the talk faster, and it helps your audience follow along without getting lost. You don't need to invent one — professional speakers reuse a handful of proven skeletons.

### Tell them three times (the classic teaching structure)

This is the oldest structure in public speaking, often summarized as: *tell them what you're going to tell them, tell them, then tell them what you told them.* It sounds repetitive on paper, but repetition is exactly what makes it work — listeners can't rewind a spoken talk the way they can reread a paragraph, so hearing the main point three times (once as a preview, once in full, once as a recap) is what makes it stick.

### Problem–Solution

You open by making the audience feel a real problem, then you present your solution and why it works. This is the natural structure for pitches, sales talks, and change proposals. A sales rep doesn't start with product features — they start with the customer's pain ("your support team is drowning in duplicate tickets"), then introduce the fix.

### Chronological (or story) structure

You walk the audience through events in the order they happened: past, present, future; or setup, complication, resolution. This suits case studies, project retrospectives, and personal narratives ("Here's where the project started, here's where it went wrong, here's how we fixed it, here's where we are now").

### PREP: Point, Reason, Example, Point

PREP is a compact structure built for short-form speaking — answering a question in a meeting, responding on the spot in an interview, or making one point in two minutes.

- **PREP** — A four-step mini-structure: state your **Point**, give your **Reason**, back it with an **Example**, then restate the **Point** to close the loop.

> **Example:** In a job interview, you're asked, "How do you handle disagreement with a manager?" Using PREP: *Point* — "I raise it privately and early." *Reason* — "because public disagreement puts people on the defensive and private conversations get to the real issue faster." *Example* — "When my manager wanted to ship a feature I thought was risky, I asked for fifteen minutes, laid out the data, and we adjusted the launch plan together." *Point* — "So my approach is: raise concerns privately, back them with evidence, and stay solution-focused." Twenty seconds, fully structured, no rambling.

| Structure | Best for | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tell-them-three-times | Teaching, training, briefings | Preview → Body → Recap |
| Problem–Solution | Pitches, sales, proposals | Pain → Fix → Proof |
| Chronological | Case studies, retrospectives, stories | Past → Present → Future |
| PREP | Q&A, interviews, quick answers | Point → Reason → Example → Point |

> **Analogy:** A structure is like a recipe, not a straitjacket. You can season it however you like — your own stories, your own tone — but if you skip the steps entirely, the dish falls apart no matter how good the ingredients are.

> **Best practice:** Pick your structure before you pick your content. Deciding "this is a Problem–Solution talk" instantly tells you what to research and what to cut — you need one strong problem story and one clear solution, not ten scattered facts.

> **Section summary:** You don't need to invent a new shape for every talk — reuse proven skeletons like tell-them-three-times, Problem–Solution, chronological, or the quick four-step PREP. Choosing the structure first makes deciding what content to include (and cut) much easier.

## 10.3 The Rule of Three

Human working memory struggles to hold more than a handful of new items at once, and audiences listening to speech (which they cannot rewind) struggle even more. This is why experienced speakers lean hard on the **rule of three** (the principle that ideas grouped in threes feel more complete, persuasive, and memorable than groups of two, four, or five).

Three feels like a pattern; two feels incomplete and four feels like a list you'll forget. Toastmasters trainers teach this explicitly: open with three points, elaborate on each in the body, and return to the same three at the close. This is also why famous phrases come in threes: "blood, sweat, and tears," "life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness," "veni, vidi, vici." The rhythm itself is persuasive, independent of content.

> **Example:** A team lead presenting a hiring plan doesn't list eight reasons to add headcount. They group everything into three: "We need to hire for three reasons — speed, coverage, and morale." Each reason gets one supporting stat or story. The audience leaves able to repeat all three; they would have retained none of the original eight.

> **Best practice:** When you have more than three points, don't add a fourth bucket — nest the extras inside one of the three. "Speed" can quietly include two sub-reasons. The audience only ever has to track three top-level ideas.

> **Section summary:** Audiences remember groups of three far better than longer lists — it's a pattern the brain latches onto. Whenever you have several points to make, look for a way to compress or nest them into three main buckets.

## 10.4 Strong Openings: Earning the Right to Be Heard

You gain or lose an audience in the first thirty to sixty seconds. Before that window closes, listeners are silently deciding whether you're worth their attention — and if you open with "Hi everyone, thanks for having me, today I'm going to talk about...", you've spent your best seconds on throat-clearing. A strong **hook** (an opening designed to immediately capture attention and create curiosity) does the opposite: it starts in the middle of the action.

Here are four reliable hook types, each with a clear mechanism for why it works:

### 1. The question hook

Open with a question that the audience can't help but form their own answer to. Questions force the brain into active participation instead of passive listening. *"How many of you have rewritten the same status update three times this week because nobody read the first one?"*

### 2. The story hook

Open with a short, specific, human moment. Stanford researcher Jennifer Aaker's well-known classroom studies found that when students gave short pitches, audiences remembered statistics from a talk only about 5% of the time — but remembered the human story woven into a talk roughly 63% of the time. Stories work because they activate emotional and sensory parts of the brain that plain facts don't touch.

### 3. The surprising statistic

Lead with a number that makes people recalculate what they thought they knew. A surprising stat signals "I've done the homework" and creates an instant knowledge gap the audience wants closed. *"Only 5% of what I'm about to show you as a number will be remembered next week — which is exactly why I'm not going to lead with numbers."*

### 4. The bold claim

Open with a confident, slightly provocative statement that the rest of the talk will justify. *"Your onboarding process is the single biggest reason new hires quit in the first ninety days."* A bold claim works because it creates tension — the audience needs to hear your reasoning to decide if you're right.

> **Analogy:** An opening is like the first ten seconds of a movie trailer. Nobody watches a trailer that opens with the studio logo and cast list — they watch because the first cut is a car mid-chase. Your talk needs its own "car mid-chase" moment before you get to the logistics.

> **Common mistake:** Opening with an apology or a disclaimer — "Sorry, I didn't have much time to prepare" or "I'm not really an expert on this." This trains the audience to discount everything that follows before you've said anything of value. Cut it, even if it's true.

> **Best practice:** Write your opening line last, after you know your core message. It's far easier to craft a hook once you know exactly what it needs to set up.

> **Section summary:** The first thirty seconds decide whether people keep listening, so never waste them on pleasantries. Open with a question, a short story, a surprising number, or a bold claim — anything that creates curiosity faster than "today I'm going to talk about."

## 10.5 Signposting and Transitions: Keeping the Audience Oriented

**Signposting** (verbally marking where you are in a talk, the way road signs mark a highway) is what stops a good structure from feeling invisible to your audience. You know your talk has three parts — but unless you say so out loud, your listeners are guessing.

Signposting phrases are simple and can feel almost too obvious when you say them — that's exactly why they work: *"There are three things I want to cover today. Let's start with the first."* ... *"That brings me to my second point."* ... *"So, to bring this together..."* These phrases cost you nothing in credibility and buy you enormous clarity, especially for listeners who briefly zoned out and need a landmark to reorient.

**Transitions** (the bridging sentences that connect one section or idea to the next) do a related but different job — they explain the logical link between what you just said and what's coming, so the talk feels like one continuous argument instead of a stack of disconnected slides. Chris Anderson's TED coaching puts heavy emphasis on this: speakers rehearse their transitions as carefully as their content, because a talk can have brilliant individual sections and still fail if the audience can't feel how they connect.

> **Example:** Instead of jumping from a slide about customer complaints straight to a slide about the new support tool, a presenter bridges it: *"So complaints are up 40% — which is exactly the problem we built this tool to solve. Here's how it works."* That one sentence does more for comprehension than either slide alone.

> **Best practice:** Say your talk's outline out loud in the first minute, and then physically echo it — repeat the same three words ("speed," "coverage," "morale") every time you move to a new section. Consistent labels act like chapter headings the audience can hold onto.

> **Section summary:** Never make your audience guess where they are in your talk — tell them out loud when you're moving to a new point, and explain how each idea connects to the last. This small habit is what makes a talk feel like one coherent story instead of a pile of separate slides.

## 10.6 Memorable Closings and a Clear Call to Action

Audiences remember beginnings and endings far better than middles — a well-documented memory pattern called the **serial position effect** (the tendency to best recall the first and last items in a sequence). This means your closing carries almost as much weight as your opening, and yet it's the part most speakers rush through because they've run out of time or energy.

A strong close does three things: it returns to the throughline (often by echoing your opening hook), it makes the takeaway unmistakably concrete, and — if you're asking for something — it states the **call to action** (the specific next step you want the audience to take) in plain, direct language.

- **Call to action (CTA)** — A clear, specific instruction for what you want your audience to do next — approve a budget, try a tool, reply by Friday, ask a question.

> **Example:** A vague close: "So that's basically it, thanks for listening, any questions?" A strong close: "We opened by asking why 40% of new hires leave in year one. The answer is onboarding — and the fix is the three-week buddy program I've outlined. I need your approval today to start piloting it with the next cohort in two weeks. Can I get a yes from this room?"

> **Best practice:** "Bookend" your talk — close by returning to the exact question, story, or statistic you opened with. If you opened with a question, answer it explicitly in your last thirty seconds. This creates a satisfying sense of a completed circle and is one of the most reliable ways to make a talk feel finished rather than just stopped.

> **Common mistake:** Ending on logistics ("that's all my slides, I think IT has the next session") instead of on your message. The last thing you say is the thing most likely to be remembered — don't waste it on housekeeping.

> **Section summary:** People remember endings almost as strongly as beginnings, so never let your close fizzle out. Return to your opening idea, restate your one core message in plain words, and — if you want action — ask for it directly and specifically.

## 10.7 Putting It Together: A Skeleton Template

Here is a reusable template that combines everything above. It works for a five-minute update or a forty-minute keynote — only the amount of detail inside each block changes.

```
 ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │  1. HOOK (0:00–0:30)                           │
 │     question | story | stat | bold claim       │
 ├───────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │  2. PREVIEW / SIGNPOST                         │
 │     "Today I'll cover three things: A, B, C"   │
 ├───────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │  3. BODY — POINT A                             │
 │       reason → example/proof → transition      │
 ├───────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │  4. BODY — POINT B                             │
 │       reason → example/proof → transition      │
 ├───────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │  5. BODY — POINT C                             │
 │       reason → example/proof → transition      │
 ├───────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │  6. RECAP                                      │
 │     "So: A, B, and C" (tie back to hook)       │
 ├───────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │  7. CALL TO ACTION                             │
 │     specific, single, direct next step         │
 └───────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Notice how this template quietly folds in everything covered so far: the hook from Section 10.4 opens it, the rule of three from Section 10.3 shapes the body into exactly three points, the signposting and transitions from Section 10.5 hold the sections together, and the bookended close and CTA from Section 10.6 land the plane. This is also a direct implementation of tell-them-three-times: the preview tells them what's coming, the body tells them, and the recap tells them what they just heard.

> **Analogy:** Think of this skeleton like scaffolding on a building under construction. Nobody sees the scaffolding in the finished talk — your audience just experiences a confident, well-paced speaker — but without it, the whole structure would collapse under its own weight.

> **Best practice:** Fill in this template with single sentences before you write a single slide or paragraph of script. If the seven boxes make sense as a skeleton on their own, the full talk will almost write itself.

> **Section summary:** A reliable talk always has the same bones: a hook, a preview of three points, three well-supported body sections connected by transitions, a recap that echoes the opening, and one direct call to action. Master this one skeleton and you can build almost any professional talk on top of it.
