Here's a strange fact about studying: the two techniques with the strongest scientific backing are the two almost nobody uses. And the three most popular methods - the ones you probably did last night - are near the bottom of the pile.

That's not a small gap. On a test a month later, students who use the good methods routinely beat the ones using the popular methods by wide margins. Same brains, same hours, wildly different results.

This is the page you keep. Everything else you've learned about [how memory works](/blog/learn-how-to-learn/02-how-memory-works) - the forgetting curve, spaced repetition, active recall - gets squeezed here into something you can pin above your desk. Read it once now. After that, don't read it. Use it.

## Why this matters

Most people were never taught how to study. They were handed textbooks and left to figure it out, so they defaulted to the methods that *feel* productive: rereading, highlighting, marathon cram sessions the night before.

The cruel twist is that those methods feel good precisely because they're easy and shallow. The methods that actually build durable memory feel harder and slower - so they get abandoned first.

Fixing this doesn't cost you more time. It costs you the same hours spent differently. That's the whole promise of this page: not "study more," but "study in a way your brain can actually keep."

## Techniques ranked by evidence

This ranking comes straight from the research - especially Dunlosky and colleagues' 2013 review that rated ten common study techniques, plus the wider body of memory science. Two clear winners, several strong seconds, and three popular losers.

| Rank | Technique | How to do it, in one line |
|------|-----------|---------------------------|
| Highest | **Practice testing** (active recall) | Close the book and force yourself to recall - flashcards, past papers, blank-page recall. |
| Highest | **Spaced practice** | Spread study across days and weeks; review just as you're about to forget, not all at once. |
| Strong | **Interleaving** | Mix related topics or problem types in one session instead of doing all of one kind in a block. |
| Strong | **Elaboration** | Keep asking "why is this true?" and "how does it connect to what I already know?" |
| Strong | **Self-explanation** | Talk yourself through *why* each step works, in your own words, as you go. |
| Strong | **Dual coding** | Pair words with a picture or diagram so the idea has two routes into memory. |
| Strong | **The Feynman technique** | Explain it in plain words as if to a 12-year-old; find the gaps; go fix them. |
| Moderate | **Summarising** | Works *if* you rephrase in your own words. Useless if you copy verbatim. |
| Weak | **Highlighting** | Marking text feels productive but adds almost nothing on its own. |
| Weak | **Rereading** | Breeds the illusion of competence - feels fluent, isn't learned. |
| Weak | **Cramming** | Great for a test tomorrow, gone by next week. |

**The 30-second version:** *Test yourself, space it out, mix it up, explain it simply, and get enough sleep.* If you remember one sentence from everything you've read about learning, remember that one.

Two of the winners deserve a word, because they're the ones you're probably not doing.

**Practice testing** means retrieving, not reviewing. When you [close the book and try to recall what you just read](/blog/learn-how-to-learn/05-active-recall-study-technique), the act of pulling the information out is what strengthens it - far more than putting it in again by rereading. The struggle is the point.

**Spaced practice** means letting yourself forget a little between sessions, then reviewing right as the memory starts to fade. This is why [spreading study across a week beats one long marathon](/blog/learn-how-to-learn/03-forgetting-curve-spacing-effect), and it's the engine behind every [spaced-repetition flashcard system](/blog/learn-how-to-learn/04-spaced-repetition-anki).

## The conditions that make techniques work

Techniques don't work in a vacuum. These are the settings that let them fire.

- **Focus.** [One task at a time](/blog/learn-how-to-learn/09-focus-and-deep-work). Multitasking isn't real - it's fast switching, and every switch leaves *attention residue* that drags on the next thing. Try a Pomodoro: 25 minutes on, 5 off.
- **Sleep.** Seven to nine hours. Sleep is when fragile new memories get locked in, and [a single sleepless night can cut your brain's ability to form new memories by roughly 40%](/blog/learn-how-to-learn/11-sleep-exercise-and-learning). Sleep both before *and* after learning.
- **Exercise.** Aerobic movement raises BDNF, a protein that helps neurons grow and connect. Even a walk before studying helps.
- **Effort.** If it feels a bit hard, it's probably working. These are *desirable difficulties* - struggle that builds durable memory.
- **Deliberate practice.** To improve a skill, [work at the edge of your ability, on your weak spots, with immediate feedback](/blog/learn-how-to-learn/10-deliberate-practice) - not comfortable repetition of what you already do well.

## Common misconceptions

A handful of beliefs quietly sabotage smart people. Here's the reality behind each.

- **"Rereading and highlighting is studying."** It's recognition, not recall. The words look familiar, so you feel ready - then the test asks you to produce the answer from a blank page. Swap most rereading time for self-testing.
- **"Studying until it feels easy means I've learned it."** "Feels easy" is the *illusion of competence*. Stop when you can *explain it*, not when it *feels familiar*.
- **"Comfortable studying is effective studying."** Usually the opposite. If a session feels smooth and effortless, you're probably not building much. Welcome the difficulty.
- **"I'm a visual (or auditory) learner."** Matching teaching to a personal "style" has no reliable evidence. Match the format to the *material* - a map is visual, a poem is verbal - and pair words with visuals for everyone.
- **"The 10,000-hour rule guarantees mastery."** It's a catchy misreading of Ericsson's research. It's not raw hours but *deliberate practice* on your weaknesses that builds expertise. Ten thousand mindless hours make you experienced, not expert.
- **"I'll study once I feel motivated."** [Motivation usually arrives *after* you start](/blog/learn-how-to-learn/12-motivation-and-growth-mindset), not before. Shrink the start and go.

## How to use this: a 30-day quick-start plan

You don't need to adopt everything at once - that's how people burn out and quit. Layer one habit at a time.

1. **Week 1 - Capture and test.** Pick one subject. After every study block, close the book and write down what you remember. Nothing fancy. Goal: replace rereading with retrieval.
2. **Week 2 - Space it.** Start flashcards (an app or a paper box of cards you move between review piles). Each day, review yesterday's *and* last week's cards, not just today's. Goal: let the intervals stretch as recall gets easier.
3. **Week 3 - Mix and deepen.** [Interleave: shuffle problem types and topics within a session](/blog/learn-how-to-learn/06-interleaving-practice). For each hard idea, give a five-minute [Feynman-style explanation out loud](/blog/learn-how-to-learn/08-feynman-technique). Goal: harder sessions, deeper connections.
4. **Week 4 - Systematise and sustain.** Set a weekly review slot. Protect a fixed bedtime. Add one phone-away Pomodoro of deep focus per day. Goal: a repeatable system you'll still use in month six.

Keep it small. A habit you actually repeat beats a perfect system you abandon in a week.

### A quick plain-English glossary

The terms worth keeping, one line each:

- **Active recall / retrieval practice** - pulling information *out* of memory rather than putting it in; the core of practice testing.
- **Spacing effect** - you remember more when study is spread over time than when it's crammed.
- **Interleaving** - mixing different topics or problem types within a session instead of blocking them.
- **Elaboration** - deepening memory by asking "why?" and "how?" and linking new material to what you already know.
- **Dual coding** - combining words with visuals so an idea gets two routes into memory.
- **Desirable difficulties** - struggles introduced on purpose (spacing, testing, interleaving) that feel harder but build stronger memory.
- **Illusion of competence** - mistaking familiarity for mastery; the trap of passive rereading.
- **Consolidation** - the process, mostly during sleep, that turns fragile new memories into stable ones.
- **Deliberate practice** - focused, effortful work at the edge of your ability, on your weaknesses, with immediate feedback.
- **Attention residue** - the mental smear left behind when you switch tasks, hurting the next one.
- **Forgetting curve** - the finding that memory drops steeply at first, then levels off - which spacing is designed to beat.
- **Metalearning** - learning how to learn; the master skill under all of this.

## Conclusion

If you strip away every study tip ever written, one line survives: **the methods that feel worse in the moment are the ones that work.** Testing beats rereading. Spacing beats cramming. A little struggle beats smooth comfort. Your gut will always vote for the easy option, and your gut is wrong about this.

So don't trust the feeling of fluency - test it. The only honest signal that you've learned something is your ability to pull it back out of an empty head.

And notice the loop hiding inside this whole page: forget a little, look it up, use it again. That's not a failure of memory - it *is* the mechanism.
